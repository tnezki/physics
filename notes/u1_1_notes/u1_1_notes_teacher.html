The diagram shows the geometry used by Eratosthenes: sunlight is nearly parallel, Syene and Alexandria are about 800 km apart, and the measured angle at Alexandria is about 7.1°, close to 1/50 of a full circle. Use the shared diagram for all parts.

(a) Identify the two measured pieces of evidence that connect the two cities in the model.

(b) If 7.1° is about 1/50 of a circle, what fraction of Earth’s circumference does the 800 km separation represent?

(c) Use your conclusion from part (b) to estimate Earth’s circumference.

(d) Explain why this is a testable physics claim rather than a guess. Name one measurement that another group could repeat.

Answer / Acceptable Reasoning

  • (a) The measured evidence is the roughly 800 km city separation and the roughly 7.1° shadow angle.
  • (b) Because 7.1° is about 1/50 of a circle, 800 km represents about 1/50 of Earth’s circumference.
  • (c) The circumference estimate is about 50(800 km) = 40,000 km.
  • (d) The claim is testable because the distance and shadow angle are measurable and can be repeated by another group.

Teacher Moves

  • Ask students to name the measured distance and angle before discussing the circumference model.
  • After reading, ask where the factor of 50 comes from physically.

Student Moves

  • Identify the two measurements in the diagram.
  • Use the angle-as-fraction model to connect 800 km to a full circumference.

Listen For / Likely Misconception

  • A student may multiply by 50 without connecting 7.1° to about 1/50 of a circle.

I can distinguish between an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, and theory.

Observation, inference, law, and theory do different jobs

Physics begins with observations and measurements, but it does not end there. An observation reports what is directly detected or measured. An inference is an interpretation built from those observations. A hypothesis is a tentative explanation that can be tested. A scientific law summarizes a consistent pattern or relationship, while a scientific theory is a broad explanatory framework supported by many lines of evidence.

The distinction matters because the same evidence can be described at different levels. Eratosthenes could observe a shadow and measure an angle; the conclusion about Earth’s circumference required an inference and a geometric model. A theory does not “turn into” a law when it becomes certain. Laws describe patterns; theories explain why broad sets of patterns and observations fit together.

I can explain why a claim must be testable to be scientific.

A scientific claim must meet evidence

A claim is scientific only when evidence could, in principle, support it or count against it. A testable claim names conditions or outcomes clearly enough that some observation or measurement could check it. That means the quantities or observations in the claim must be clear enough to measure or observe. “This surface has good energy” is not testable until the words are tied to something measurable; a claim about a cart’s travel time can be tested by repeating timed trials under controlled conditions.

Models extend what can be measured directly. Scale drawings and ratios let scientists connect a nearby measurement to a distant system. A useful model makes its assumptions visible and gives other people a way to repeat the measurement, check the reasoning, or improve the model.

Example 2: Testable Claims

A student claims, “This surface is better because it has good energy,” but never defines what “better” or “good energy” means. Evaluate the claim’s testability and explain what would need to be measured.

Answer / Acceptable Reasoning

  • The claim is not yet testable because “better” and “good energy” are undefined.
  • Define a measurable outcome—such as travel time, speed, rebound height, or another specified quantity—and compare it under controlled conditions.

Teacher Moves

  • Ask, “What observation could prove this claim wrong?”
  • Have students replace vague language with one measurable variable.

Student Moves

  • Identify what is not operationally defined.
  • Propose a measurable quantity and a repeatable check.

Listen For / Likely Misconception

  • Listen for students who call a claim testable merely because an experiment is mentioned.

I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

Formulas are compact physical stories

An equation in physics is a compact statement about a relationship among physical quantities. Symbols must stand for measurable ideas, units matter, and the relationship should be interpreted before numbers are substituted. A formula is useful because it organizes evidence and makes a prediction or estimate possible—not because it turns numbers into an answer automatically.

For Eratosthenes, the angle represents a fraction of a full circle. If 7.1° is about one-fiftieth of a complete circle, then the 800 km surface separation is modeled as about one-fiftieth of Earth’s circumference. The multiplication follows from the model. The same habit of thinking applies to later force equations: choose a direction, identify the physical quantities, and explain why the relationship matches the situation.

Formula / RelationshipEratosthenes’ circumference model

\(C\approx 50(800\text{ km})\approx 40{,}000\text{ km}\)

Physical meaning: The measured city separation represents about one-fiftieth of Earth’s circumference because the measured angle is about one-fiftieth of a full circle.

Example 3: Relationships Guide Thinking

A class models average speed with speed = distance/time. One student claims a cart that travels farther is always faster, even when the travel times differ. Critique the reasoning and state how the relationship should guide the thinking.

Answer / Acceptable Reasoning

  • Traveling farther does not by itself mean the cart is faster; average speed depends on both distance and time.
  • Use the relationship as a comparison of physical quantities: compare distance per unit time, not distance alone.

Teacher Moves

  • Ask students to state the physical meaning of each quantity before calculating.
  • Press for the assumption or sign convention that makes the relationship valid.

Student Moves

  • Name the quantities and direction convention.
  • Use the relationship to compare physical effects rather than manipulate unlabeled numbers.

Listen For / Likely Misconception

  • Listen for formula substitution with no stated physical meaning or direction convention.

Example 4: Model the Physics

A student draws a box labeled “formula” with arrows from “numbers” directly to “answer,” but includes no physical quantities, units, or evidence. Critique the model and describe one feature you would add so it represents physics reasoning.

Answer / Acceptable Reasoning

  • The model is incomplete because it hides the physical quantities, units, evidence, and relationship.
  • Add labeled physical quantities and show how the equation connects evidence about the situation to a predicted or calculated result.

Teacher Moves

  • Ask, “What object is the system, and what interaction creates each arrow?”
  • For critique tasks, ask for the smallest feature that would make the model physically informative.

Student Moves

  • Draw or describe only forces acting on the chosen system.
  • Label directions/interactions and make relative arrow lengths consistent with the situation.

Listen For / Likely Misconception

  • Listen for inertia or motion drawn as a force, or forces placed on the diagram that act on another object.

You Try It 4: Model the Physics

The relationship \(F_{net}=F_{right}-F_{left}\) is proposed for a one-dimensional force situation. Explain in words what each term represents and why the equation is a model of the situation rather than merely an instruction to subtract numbers.

Answer / Acceptable Reasoning

  • \(F_{right}\) is the rightward force component, \(F_{left}\) is the leftward force magnitude under the stated sign convention, and \(F_{net}\) is the resulting horizontal force.
  • The equation models how opposite directional effects combine; it is not merely an instruction to subtract unlabeled numbers.

Vocabulary with Definitions / Reference

TermPhysics meaning
observationInformation gathered directly with the senses or measuring tools.
inferenceAn interpretation or conclusion drawn from observations.
hypothesisA tentative, testable explanation for observations.
scientific lawA concise statement or relationship that describes a consistent pattern in nature.
scientific theoryA broad, well-supported explanatory framework that connects many observations and tested ideas.
evidenceObservations or measurements used to evaluate a claim or model.
modelA useful representation of a system or relationship used to explain and predict.
testable claimA claim that can be checked against observable or measurable evidence.

Summary

  • Physics claims earn trust through observations, measurements, evidence, and testable models.
  • Observations report what is detected; inferences interpret; hypotheses are testable explanations; laws describe patterns; theories explain broadly.
  • Equations are compact models of physical relationships and must be interpreted, not merely used for arithmetic.
