Thinking Like a Physicist
Physics begins by noticing carefully, measuring when possible, and asking what evidence would support or challenge an explanation.

Mathematical idea: A formula is a compact model that shows how measured quantities are related, not just a place to plug in numbers.

Today you will practice separating what you observe from what you infer, testing claims with evidence, and reading formulas as guides for thinking.

Content: How Physics Builds Knowledge

Reading Apprenticeship directions

Read with a partner. Mark one sentence that feels important, circle words you need to revisit, and place a question mark beside any claim that makes you ask, “How would we know?”

Physics starts with the natural world, not with an answer key. Scientists observe events, make measurements, compare patterns, and build explanations that can be checked against evidence. Good measurements make observations more precise because numbers let different people compare what happened.

The textbook gives Eratosthenes as an early example of evidence-based reasoning. He measured the angle of a pillar's shadow in Alexandria and used the known distance to Syene to reason about Earth's size. The shadow was an observation; the conclusion about Earth's circumference was an inference built from measurement, geometry, and a model.

Scientists also make hypotheses. A hypothesis is an educated explanation or prediction that can be tested. A scientific claim must allow some possible evidence to show that it is wrong; if no observation or experiment could ever count against it, science has no way to test it.

Scientific laws and theories do different jobs. A law or principle describes a pattern or relationship that has been tested many times, while a scientific theory connects a large body of evidence into an explanation of how or why part of nature works. A theory is not simply a weak guess, and both laws and theories can be refined when strong new evidence appears.

Evidence matters more than reputation or popularity. The textbook describes how ideas can survive for a long time when people trust authority, but a repeatable experiment that disagrees with the idea can force scientists to revise it. Changing an explanation because of solid evidence is a strength of science because the goal is to improve the model, not defend a favorite answer.

Mathematics helps physicists express relationships clearly. The textbook calls equations “guides to thinking” because an equation can show which quantities are connected and how changing one quantity is related to another. Before calculating, a physicist should be able to say what the symbols represent and what relationship the formula is modeling.

Stop and Discuss

  1. In the Eratosthenes example, what was directly observed or measured, and what was inferred from that evidence?
  2. Why does a scientific claim need a possible test that could show it is wrong?
  3. Why can changing a theory after strong new evidence be a strength instead of a weakness?

Content discussion answers: 1. Eratosthenes directly measured the shadow angle (and used a known city-to-city distance); Earth's size was an inference from those measurements plus a geometric model. 2. A scientific claim needs a possible result that could count against it so evidence can actually test the idea. 3. Revising a theory when evidence changes improves the explanation and shows that evidence matters more than protecting an old idea. Follow-up: Ask students which answer came directly from the text and which required combining ideas.

Math Notes Scientific ideas: what kind of statement is it?

When you read a physics claim, first decide whether it reports evidence, interprets evidence, proposes a testable idea, describes a repeated pattern, or explains a large body of evidence.

Observation

Something directly noticed or measured.

Inference

An interpretation or conclusion based on observations.

Hypothesis

A testable explanation or prediction that evidence can support or challenge.

Law / Principle

A well-tested description of a pattern or relationship in nature.

Theory

A broad explanation supported by a large body of evidence and tested ideas.

Evidence

Measurements, observations, or results used to evaluate a claim.

Model

A simplified representation used to explain or predict a real system.

Testable

There is some possible observation or result that could count against the claim.

Example 1: Observation, Inference, or Scientific Idea?

A class rolls a toy car down a ramp. The car crosses a line on the floor and bumps a book.

  1. Classify this statement: “The book moved 18 cm after the car hit it.”
  2. Classify this statement: “The car transferred motion to the book.”
  3. A student says, “If we release the same car from higher on the ramp, the book will move farther.” What kind of scientific statement is this, and what makes it different from the first two?

Example 1: 1. Observation/measurement. 2. Inference. 3. Hypothesis because it predicts a result that can be tested by changing release height and measuring book motion.

You Try It 1: Sort the Statements

A student watches a pendulum swing and records what happens.

  1. Classify: “The pendulum completed 12 swings in 20 seconds.”
  2. Classify: “The pendulum slowed because energy was transferred to the surroundings.”
  3. Classify: “If the string is made longer, the time for one swing will increase.” Explain why that label fits.

You Try It 1: 1. Observation/measurement. 2. Inference. 3. Hypothesis; it predicts a measurable change in swing time when string length changes.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What words or details helped you separate an observation from an inference?
  2. Why is a hypothesis more useful when it predicts something that can be measured?

Discussion guide: Direct observations name what was seen or measured; inferences add an explanation. A useful hypothesis points to a measurable test. Misconception watch: Students may call any sentence with a number a hypothesis.

Example 2: Is the Claim Testable?

Consider two claims about the same cart.

Claim A: “If the ramp angle increases, the cart will take less time to travel 2 meters.”

Claim B: “This cart moves well because it is a lucky cart.”

  1. Which claim is scientific enough to test with measurements?
  2. Describe one measurement that could be used to test Claim A.
  3. What result could count against Claim A? Why is it harder to name a result that would disprove Claim B?

Example 2: Claim A is testable. Measure travel time over the same 2 m for different ramp angles while keeping the cart and other conditions the same. If steeper angles do not reduce time, or consistently increase it, that evidence counts against the claim. Claim B does not define a measurable meaning of “lucky.”

You Try It 2: Build a Test

A student says, “A stretched rubber band launches a paper ball farther when the band is pulled back a greater distance.”

  1. Is this claim testable? Explain.
  2. Name the quantity you would change and the quantity you would measure.
  3. Describe one pattern in the data that would make you question the claim.

You Try It 2: Yes. Change pullback distance and measure launch distance. A pattern with no increase in launch distance as pullback increases, or a consistent decrease under controlled conditions, would challenge the claim.

Stop and Discuss

  1. How does asking “What result would make this claim look wrong?” improve a scientific test?
  2. What is the difference between a claim sounding reasonable and a claim being testable?

Discussion guide: A falsifiable claim tells students what evidence would matter. Sounding sensible is not enough. Follow-up: Ask students to rewrite one vague claim so it becomes measurable.

Math Notes Read a formula as a relationship

A physics formula should make sense as a sentence before it becomes a calculation. Ask: What does each symbol represent? Which quantity depends on the others? If one quantity changes while the others stay the same, what should happen?

The textbook uses the circle relationship $$C=2\pi r.$$ Read it as: “Circumference is two times pi times the radius.” It tells you that a larger radius goes with a larger circumference.

Later in this unit you will see $$W=mg.$$ For now, do not calculate with it. Read it as a model: weight $W$ depends on mass $m$ and the gravitational field strength $g$.

  • Before calculating: name the quantities and describe the relationship.
  • After calculating: ask whether the result fits the relationship and the physical situation.
  • Important: a formula is a model of a relationship; it does not explain every detail of the real world by itself.

Example 4: Formula as a Thinking Tool

A student sees the relationship $C=2\pi r$ and immediately asks for a number to plug in.

  1. Before using any numbers, what does $C$ represent and what does $r$ represent?
  2. Without calculating, predict what happens to $C$ if $r$ increases.
  3. Explain why that prediction shows the formula is useful even before a calculation is done.

Example 4: $C$ is circumference and $r$ is radius. As $r$ increases, $C$ increases. The formula is already useful because it predicts a relationship before any values are substituted.

You Try It 4: Read the Relationship First

Preview the later-unit relationship $W=mg$. You are not solving with it yet.

  1. What quantity does $W$ represent? What does $m$ represent?
  2. If $g$ stays the same and $m$ increases, what should happen to $W$?
  3. Write one sentence explaining how the formula guides your thinking before you ever substitute a number.

You Try It 4: $W$ is weight and $m$ is mass. If $g$ stays the same, increasing $m$ increases $W$. The relationship can be understood qualitatively before calculation. Teaching note: Keep this as a formula-literacy preview; do not turn it into a $W=mg$ calculation lesson yet.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What can a formula tell you before you know any numbers?
  2. Why might “plug and chug” lead to an answer that has no physical meaning?

Discussion guide: Formulas can identify quantities, dependencies, and expected directions of change. “Plug and chug” can hide a wrong interpretation or an unreasonable result.

Summary

Physics builds knowledge by observing, measuring, testing, and comparing explanations with evidence.

An observation reports what was directly noticed or measured. An inference explains what the observation may mean, and a hypothesis makes a testable explanation or prediction.

Scientific claims must be open to evidence that could challenge them. Laws describe tested patterns, while theories organize a large body of evidence into explanations that can be refined.

Formulas are compact models of relationships. Strong physics thinking starts by interpreting the quantities and predicting how they are connected before calculating.

Possible summary: Physics separates observations from interpretations, tests claims against evidence, revises explanations when evidence demands it, and uses mathematical relationships as models. Check for transfer: Ask a student to give one new observation and one inference about the same classroom event.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling an inference an observation. Why it is tempting: the explanation feels obvious. What to check instead: could a camera or measuring tool directly record the statement?
  • Thinking “theory” means “just a guess.” Why it is tempting: everyday speech uses the word that way. What to check instead: a scientific theory explains a large body of tested evidence.
  • Calling any believable claim scientific. Why it is tempting: reasonable ideas sound convincing. What to check instead: name a possible observation or result that could count against the claim.
  • Using one result as final proof. Why it is tempting: one trial gives a quick answer. What to check instead: repeat measurements and look for a consistent pattern while controlling important variables.
  • Treating formulas as plug-in machines. Why it is tempting: numbers feel faster than explanation. What to check instead: name the quantities, describe the relationship, and predict the direction of change first.
