A class tests the claim: ‘A larger pull on the same spring produces a larger stretch.’ They record:

Pull force (N) | Spring stretch (cm)
2 | 1.1
4 | 2.0
6 | 3.2
8 | 4.1

(a) State one observation from the table and one inference a student could make from it.
(b) Does the evidence support the claim? Cite a specific pattern.
(c) A student sketches the relationship as stretch ∝ pull force. Explain what that relationship tells you physically without calculating a new value.
(d) Name one additional measurement or repeated trial that would make the evidence stronger.
Canonical solution: (a) Example observation: the measured stretch is 1.1 cm at 2 N. Example inference: stronger pulls cause larger stretches. (b) Yes; every increase in pull force is accompanied by an increase in stretch. (c) The relationship says stretch changes in the same direction as pull force for this spring; it organizes the pattern rather than serving only as an arithmetic rule. (d) Repeat each force several times or add additional force levels.
The table contains directly observed measurements. An inference interprets the pattern. Because all four measurements rise together, the claim is supported by this data set, though repeated trials would strengthen the evidence. The proportional-style relationship summarizes how the two measured quantities are connected.

Scientific Measurements: Observation, Evidence, and a Model

Measurements are a hallmark of science because they give us observations that can be checked by other people. A measurement is not the same thing as an explanation: the measurement is evidence, while the explanation is an inference built from that evidence.

Eratosthenes used this distinction more than two thousand years ago. He compared shadows at Alexandria and Syene when the Sun was high in the sky. The shadow angle in Alexandria and the known distance between the cities were observations or measurements. From those measurements, he reasoned that the angle represented a fraction of a full circle and used a geometric model to infer Earth’s circumference.

The important physics habit is to keep the layers separate. First ask, “What was directly measured or shown?” Then ask, “What conclusion does the model allow us to infer from those observations?” A strong explanation can point back to the specific evidence that supports it.

Stop & Discuss
  1. Name one thing directly measured or shown.
  2. Name one conclusion that requires reasoning beyond the direct observation.
  3. What evidence in the figure supports the conclusion?
Listen for: Students point to a specific measurement/visual feature as evidence and label interpretation as inference rather than observation.

Example 1

During a cooling-water test, a student writes: ‘The thermometer reads 18.4 °C,’ then writes, ‘The water cooled because the room was cold.’ Classify each statement as observation or inference and explain the distinction.
Answer: 18.4 °C is an observation; the cause statement is an inference.
The thermometer reading is directly measured. The proposed cause interprets why the measurement occurred, so it is an inference.

You Try It 1

A motion sensor records a cart speed of 0.62 m/s. A student says, ‘The cart is slowing because the wheels are rubbing.’ Classify the measured statement and the causal statement.
Answer: 0.62 m/s is an observation; the rubbing explanation is an inference.
A measured sensor value is an observation. The explanation about wheel rubbing goes beyond what was directly measured, so it is an inference.

Mathematics—The Language of Science

Physics becomes especially powerful when ideas are expressed mathematically. An equation is a compact way to state a relationship between physical quantities. Because the symbols have defined meanings, the relationship is less ambiguous than a loose verbal description.

The equations of physics are therefore guides to thinking. Before substituting numbers, identify what each quantity represents, decide what is changing and what is being held fixed, and predict how the relationship should behave. Calculation comes after the physical meaning is clear.

A mathematical model can also be tested. If measurements do not follow the relationship the model predicts, the model, the assumptions, or the measurements need to be reconsidered. That connection between relationship and evidence is part of what makes mathematics so useful in science.

Stop & Discuss
  1. What relationship does the equation say changes together?
  2. What could you predict if one quantity changes and the others are held fixed?
  3. Why is that more than “plugging in”?
Listen for: Students describe variable relationships qualitatively and connect the symbols to physical meaning.
Model NoteEquations as Physical Models

A physics equation is a compact model of a relationship. Name each quantity, identify what is held fixed, and predict how one quantity should change when another changes. The symbols represent the phenomenon before they become numbers in a calculation.

Example 3

A cart is pushed with different forces while its motion change is rated on the same scale:
Force (N) | motion-change rating
2 | 1
4 | 2
6 | 3
8 | 4
A student claims that a larger applied force is associated with a larger change in motion. Is the claim supported? What does the pattern communicate physically?
Answer: Yes; doubling the force doubles the rating in this data set. The table communicates that stronger applied forces are associated with larger motion changes under the tested conditions.
The numerical pattern rises consistently: 2→1, 4→2, 6→3, 8→4. The relationship is a model of how the quantities co-vary, not merely numbers to substitute.

You Try It 3

Students stretch the same elastic band:
Pull (N) | stretch (cm)
1 | 0.8
2 | 1.6
3 | 2.4
4 | 3.2
Does the evidence support the explanation that stretch grows with pull? Explain what the table says about the phenomenon.
Answer: Yes; each additional 1 N is accompanied by 0.8 cm more stretch. The relationship summarizes a consistent increase in stretch as pull increases.
The measured pairs form a regular increasing pattern, so the explanation is supported over the tested range.

Example 4

A model says the distance traveled at constant speed is d = vt. Without calculating, predict what happens to distance if the travel time doubles while speed stays the same. Explain using the relationship.
Answer: Distance doubles.
In d = vt, with v unchanged, d is directly proportional to t. Doubling t doubles the product and therefore the distance.

You Try It 4

For a constant-speed conveyor, d = vt. Without substituting numbers, predict how the distance changes if the speed is cut in half while the operating time stays fixed.
Answer: Distance is cut in half.
With t fixed, distance changes directly with speed. Halving v halves d.

Scientific Methods and the Scientific Attitude

There is no single recipe that every scientist follows, but scientific work commonly moves between questions, possible explanations, predictions, measurements, and revisions. A hypothesis is a proposed explanation or prediction that can be tested against evidence.

A scientific claim must be testable in a meaningful sense: there must be some observation or measurement that could count against it. A claim does not become scientific merely because it sounds reasonable, uses technical vocabulary, or is stated by an authority.

Scientific laws and theories also have specific roles. A law describes a dependable pattern or relationship in nature. A scientific theory is a broad, evidence-supported explanatory framework. Neither is simply a guess. Both remain open to revision when reliable evidence demands it.

The scientific attitude is therefore a willingness to let evidence change an explanation. Good science asks two persistent questions: How would we know? What evidence would show that this idea is wrong or incomplete?

Stop & Discuss
  1. What observation could support this claim?
  2. What result would count against it?
  3. Is the statement a hypothesis, law/theory-level explanation, observation, or inference?
Listen for: Students name an observable/measurable test and a possible disconfirming result instead of treating plausibility or authority as evidence.

Example 2

Claim: ‘A heavier pendulum bob always swings faster because heavy objects prefer downward motion.’ Is this claim scientifically testable as written? Revise it into a testable claim and name one measurement.
Answer: Not adequately testable as written; a testable revision compares period for different bob masses while holding length constant and measures swing period.
Scientific claims need observable consequences. A measurable revision is: with pendulum length fixed, changing bob mass changes the period. Measure the period for several masses.

You Try It 2

Claim: ‘Magnets know when another magnet is nearby.’ Explain why this is not a useful scientific claim as written. Rewrite it so a class could test it and identify a measurement.
Answer: ‘Know’ is not operationally observable; revise to a measurable claim such as decreasing magnet separation increases measured magnetic force, then measure force and separation.
A testable statement connects conditions to observable outcomes. Force versus separation can be measured, whereas ‘knowing’ cannot.

Vocabulary

observationInformation recorded directly with the senses or a measuring instrument.
inferenceAn interpretation or conclusion drawn from observations and evidence.
hypothesisA proposed, testable explanation or prediction.
scientific lawA concise description of a repeatedly observed pattern or relationship in nature.
scientific theoryA broad explanatory framework supported by a large body of evidence and repeated testing.
evidenceObservations, measurements, or data used to evaluate a claim or explanation.
modelA representation or relationship used to describe, explain, or predict a physical system.
testable claimA claim connected to observations or measurements that could support it or show it to be wrong.

Summary

  • Observation is direct; inference interprets evidence.
  • Scientific claims must be testable and open to being shown wrong.
  • Hypotheses, laws, and theories play different explanatory roles.
  • Equations/models express relationships and guide physical reasoning, not just arithmetic.
