Section 8.2
Electricity and Magnetism
Electric current does more than move charge—it creates a magnetic field around the path of the current.

Mathematical idea: Direction diagrams show a current direction and the circular or concentrated magnetic-field pattern it produces.

You will use compasses, field lines, and iron filings as evidence to compare the magnetic fields of a straight wire, a loop, and a coil.

Learning Target

Students will explain how electric current produces magnetic fields and how changing electric and magnetic fields are connected.

Reading 1: A current produces a magnetic field

An electric current is moving electric charge. Because moving charge produces magnetism, a current-carrying wire has a magnetic effect in the space around it. The source demonstrates this by surrounding a wire with compasses and then turning on the current.

The needles do not point randomly. They arrange around the wire in a circular pattern, revealing a magnetic field produced by the current. A compass works as a detector because its tiny magnetic needle turns to match the local field direction.

Image read: Trace a circle through several compass needles. Mark the direction of the current and describe the pattern of needle directions around the wire.

When the current is reversed, the compass needles turn around. This means the field direction is tied to current direction. The evidence is especially useful because the wire itself looks almost unchanged; the compass response reveals what changed in the surrounding space.

This is Oersted’s result in a controlled form: an electric current creates a magnetic field. The effect disappears when the circuit is open and current stops, so the field is linked to moving charge rather than simply to the presence of the wire.

The circular pattern also gives a useful scale idea. Very close to the wire, the current-produced field can noticeably redirect a compass; farther away, Earth’s field may dominate the needle’s direction. The field is still present around the current, but the visible compass response depends on the relative strength of all fields at that location.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What observation in the compass image is evidence that the field around a straight current-carrying wire is circular?
  2. If the current reverses, what should happen to the compass directions, and what does that tell you about the field?

Teacher answer: The compass directions form a circular pattern around the wire. Reversing current reverses the field direction and therefore the compass orientations.

Example 1: Compass near a wire

A compass is placed beside a straight wire. With the circuit open, it points with Earth’s field. When the circuit closes, the needle turns.

  1. What new field caused the change?
  2. What would you expect if the current direction were reversed?

Teacher answer: Closing the circuit creates a current-produced magnetic field. Reversing current should reverse the needle deflection.

You Try It 1: Current on, current off

Several compasses surround a wire. They form a circular pattern only while current flows.

  1. Explain why the pattern appears only when current is present.
  2. Identify what the compasses are measuring indirectly.

Teacher answer: The pattern appears only with moving charge; the compasses indirectly map the magnetic field direction.

Reading 2: Bending the wire changes the field pattern

A straight wire produces circular magnetic field lines centered on the wire. If the wire is bent into a loop, the field from different parts of the wire overlaps. Inside the loop, many contributions point in compatible directions, so the field becomes more concentrated.

Image read: Compare the upper straight-wire model with the lower loop model. Mark where field lines become more concentrated after the wire is bent.

Adding another loop causes more overlap in the same central region. A many-turn coil therefore produces a stronger, more organized magnetic field than a single loop carrying the same current, because each turn contributes to the total field.

The important comparison is not that a loop somehow creates “extra electricity.” The same physical rule is operating: moving charge creates a magnetic field. Geometry changes how the fields from many sections of wire combine in space.

Thinking in terms of superposition helps: each small section of the wire contributes to the field. In the center of a loop, many contributions reinforce one another. Outside the loop, the pattern spreads through a larger region. That is why the field drawing changes even when the amount of current stays the same.

Stop and Discuss

  1. How does the field pattern change when a straight wire is bent into a loop?
  2. Why can additional loops strengthen the field in the central region without creating new charge?

Teacher answer: Bending into a loop makes field contributions overlap in the center. More loops add more contributions without creating charge.

Example 2: One loop or two?

Two coils carry the same current. Coil A has one turn; Coil B has two turns placed close together.

  1. Which coil should have the stronger central magnetic field?
  2. Explain using overlapping field contributions.

Teacher answer: Coil B, with two turns, should have the stronger central field because the turn fields overlap and add.

You Try It 2: Shape matters

The same length of current-carrying wire is first straight and then bent into a circular loop.

  1. Compare the expected field shape in the two arrangements.
  2. Identify the region where the loop concentrates the magnetic field.

Teacher answer: The straight wire has a circular field around the wire; the loop concentrates the field through its center.

Reading 3: Many loops make a solenoid

A long coil made of many current-carrying loops is a solenoid. Inside it, the magnetic fields from the individual turns add together. The source’s iron-filing photographs let us compare the progression from wire to loop to coil using the same kind of evidence.

Image read: Compare panels (a), (b), and (c). Describe how the field becomes more concentrated and organized as the conductor changes from wire to loop to coil.

The word electromagnetism captures the connection seen across these examples. Electricity and magnetism are not separate topics accidentally placed together: the movement of electric charge produces magnetic fields, and later we will see magnetic conditions act back on moving charge.

The coil also creates a bridge to electromagnets. A current-carrying coil already has a magnet-like field pattern. Adding more current, more turns, or a suitable iron core can strengthen that effect, which is the starting point for the next section.

The source’s Practicing Physics example makes the same point from the opposite direction: ordinary iron objects can respond to surrounding magnetic fields and become weakly magnetized. A compass therefore responds to the total magnetic environment, whether the field comes from a permanent magnet, Earth, or electric current.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Use the three-panel image to describe one similarity and one difference among the fields of the wire, loop, and coil.
  2. How does the wire-to-coil sequence support the idea that electricity and magnetism are connected?

Teacher answer: All three fields come from moving charge. The multi-turn coil has the most concentrated central field of the three.

Example 3: Interpret the three models

A diagram shows a straight wire, a one-turn loop, and a many-turn coil carrying current.

  1. Rank the arrangements by how concentrated the central field is likely to be.
  2. Explain what stays physically responsible for the magnetic field in all three cases.

Teacher answer: The multi-turn coil should have the most concentrated central field; all three arrangements still depend on moving charge as the source of magnetism.

You Try It 3: Reverse the coil current

A solenoid carries steady current and acts like a magnet with two ends.

  1. Predict what happens to its magnetic field direction if the current reverses.
  2. Explain why the field does not remain unchanged.

Teacher answer: Reversing current reverses the solenoid field direction, so its magnetic polarity reverses.

Vocabulary: Define the Terms

You have now seen these words in the reading, images, and practice. Use this table to consolidate the physics meaning of each term.

Vocabulary termDefinition
electric currentA flow of electric charge.
current-carrying wireA conductor through which electric charge is moving.
magnetic fieldThe region around a magnet or moving charge where magnetic effects can be detected.
compassA small magnet free to turn and align with a magnetic field.
field directionThe direction a north test pole or compass north end would point.
loopA current path bent into a closed turn, causing magnetic-field contributions to overlap.
coilSeveral loops of wire arranged so their magnetic fields add.
solenoidA long multi-turn coil that produces a concentrated magnetic field when current flows.
electromagnetismThe connected description of electric and magnetic effects, including magnetic fields made by moving charge.

Summary

  • Moving electric charge produces a magnetic field.
  • A straight current-carrying wire produces a circular magnetic-field pattern.
  • Compasses reveal field direction and reverse when current direction reverses.
  • Bending wire into a loop concentrates the field inside the loop.
  • Many loops form a coil or solenoid whose individual field contributions add.
  • The current-produced field is direct evidence that electricity and magnetism are connected.
Summary notes / questions / connections

Teacher summary cue: Emphasize the evidence chain: current → field, compass maps direction, geometry changes overlap, many turns concentrate the field.

Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy it is temptingCheck instead
Thinking the wire must be a permanent magnetA compass turns near it when current flows.Check whether moving charge is present; current itself creates the magnetic field.
Drawing field lines as straight along the wireCurrent flows along the wire, so the two directions are easy to confuse.Around a straight wire, the magnetic field circles the wire.
Assuming more loops create more chargeA stronger field appears with more turns.The same moving charges contribute fields that overlap and add.
Thinking current reversal changes only current, not fieldThe wire shape is unchanged.Compass reversal shows the magnetic-field direction reverses too.
