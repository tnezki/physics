Section 1.4
Equilibrium and Balanced Motion
Balanced forces can describe both rest and steady straight-line motion.

Mathematical idea: Mechanical equilibrium is summarized by the equilibrium rule: the vector sum of the forces is zero.

The scaffold, gymnast, book, and scale images show that equilibrium does not mean “no forces.” Instead, forces can be large and distributed in different ways while their vector sum remains zero.

Learning Target

Students will determine whether an object is in mechanical equilibrium and explain how balanced forces relate to rest or constant straight-line motion.

Reading 1: Mechanical equilibrium means the net force is zero

Consider a hanging object supported by a string and spring scale. Gravity pulls downward on the object while tension pulls upward. If the object remains at rest, those two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, so their vector sum is zero. The object is not force-free; it is balanced.

When the net force on an object is zero, the condition is called mechanical equilibrium. The equilibrium rule is written as $\Sigma F=0$. The symbol $\Sigma$ means to take the vector sum, so direction is included. If upward is chosen as positive, downward forces count in the opposite direction. Equal upward and downward totals cancel.

The scaffold force diagram extends the idea to several forces. Two ropes pull upward, while the people and scaffold contribute downward weights. No single rope has to equal the entire downward weight. What matters for vertical equilibrium is that the total upward force equals the total downward force. The multiple vectors can still sum to zero.

Image read: Add the upward arrow magnitudes conceptually and compare them with the downward arrows. Mark the evidence that the vector sum is zero.

An object at rest with zero net force is in static equilibrium. Static does not mean no interaction. A hanging sign, a person on a scaffold, or a book on a table can have several forces acting on it continuously. The word equilibrium describes the combined effect of those forces and the unchanged motion, not the absence of forces.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why does $\Sigma F=0$ not mean that every individual force is zero?
  2. Use the scaffold figure to explain what must be true about the total upward force and total downward force.

Teacher answer / support: Mechanical/static equilibrium: forces can be nonzero individually, but their vector sum is zero. Scaffold: total upward tension equals total downward weight.

Math NotesThe equilibrium rule
$$\Sigma F=0$$

In equilibrium, opposite-direction forces balance when all forces on the chosen object are added as vectors. Zero net force means the object’s motion is not changing.

Example 1: A hanging sign

A hanging sign has 120 N of weight downward and remains at rest. If one vertical support provides all of the upward force, what upward force is required? Explain with the equilibrium rule.

Teacher answer / support: 120 N upward. Then +120 N and −120 N sum to 0 N, so the sign can remain at rest.

You Try It 1: Balanced horizontal forces

A cart is held motionless by a 35-N pull to the right. What leftward force is required for horizontal equilibrium? State the net horizontal force.

Teacher answer / support: 35 N left is required. The horizontal net force is 0 N.

Reading 2: Equilibrium can redistribute support without making every force equal

A balanced system does not require identical support forces. The scaffold examples are built around this idea. When a person stands near the middle, the two supporting ropes can share the load more evenly. As the person moves toward one rope, that rope supports more of the total load while the other supports less. The individual tensions change even though the scaffold remains in equilibrium.

The gymnast-on-rings example gives the same pattern. If the gymnast hangs with her weight shared evenly, the two scale readings can be equal and each accounts for part of the weight. If she shifts toward one support, that side can carry more while the other carries less. The equilibrium requirement applies to the total vector sum, not to equality between each pair of forces.

Image read: Compare the two positions. Annotate which support carries more of the load in each and why both situations can still have zero net force.

The scaffold practice image makes the redistribution numerical. In one position, the two supports can each read 500 N for a total upward support of 1000 N. In another, one support can read 170 N while the other must provide the remaining 830 N. In an extreme position, one rope can go slack while the other provides essentially all of the upward support. In all three cases, equilibrium requires the upward total to match the downward total.

Image read: For each position, focus on the sum of the two upward supports rather than on one rope by itself.

This is a useful reasoning habit: keep track of what must stay fixed and what is allowed to change. For a motionless object with the same total weight, the required total upward support stays fixed. The way that total is divided among ropes or surfaces may change with position and geometry.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why can two support forces be unequal even while the object remains in equilibrium?
  2. In the scaffold positions, what quantity stays the same as the load moves, and what quantities change?

Teacher answer / support: Fixed: total upward support must still equal total downward weight. Variable: how that upward total is shared. Unequal support forces are compatible with equilibrium.

Example 2: Sharing a 500-N load

Two vertical ropes support a 500-N load in equilibrium. One rope provides 170 N upward. What upward force must the other rope provide? Explain why the rope forces do not have to be equal.

Teacher answer / support: 330 N upward from the other rope because 170 N + 330 N = 500 N upward, balancing 500 N downward.

You Try It 2: Weight shifted toward one support

A gymnast is at rest while supported by two vertical scale ropes. Explain how the two readings could change if she shifts more of her weight toward the left rope while remaining in equilibrium.

Teacher answer / support: Left reading increases and right reading decreases, while their sum remains equal to her weight if she stays at rest.

Reading 3: Support force and steady motion are both equilibrium ideas

A book at rest on a table is another equilibrium system. Gravity pulls downward with the book’s weight, while the table pushes upward with a support force. This support force is called the normal force when it acts perpendicular to the surface. If there are no other vertical forces and the book remains at rest, the support force and weight have equal magnitudes.

The spring comparison helps explain how a rigid-looking surface can push. Pressing on a spring compresses it and produces an upward push on your hand. The book also compresses the table’s atoms by a tiny amount, and those atoms push back. The support force is therefore an interaction between the book and table, not a mysterious force that appears simply because the book is motionless.

Image read: Mark the surface interaction that supplies the upward force. Explain why the table can behave like a very stiff spring at the microscopic level.

A bathroom scale measures this support interaction. When a person stands still, the scale pushes upward while gravity pulls downward. The scale can show a large force at the same time the net force on the person is zero. The reading is connected to the support force, while net force must be found by combining all forces on the chosen object.

Image read: Label support force and weight on the person. Then write “net force = 0” only after you have combined the two forces.

The Unit 1 learning target extends this same equilibrium idea to steady motion: an object moving at constant speed in a straight line has unchanged motion and can therefore be in dynamic equilibrium. Static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium have different motion states, but both correspond to zero net force.

This gives a clean test. First describe the motion. If the object is at rest and remains at rest, it may be in static equilibrium. If it moves with constant speed in a straight line, it may be in dynamic equilibrium. If it speeds up, slows down, or turns, its motion is changing and the net force is not zero.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why can a bathroom scale show a nonzero force while the person’s net force is zero?
  2. What is the one force relationship shared by static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium?

Teacher answer / support: Scale reads support force; weight balances it in the standing-still case. Dynamic-equilibrium statement comes from the Unit 1 assessment plan because extracted Section 2.7 is missing.

Math NotesSimple support-force balance

For an object at rest on a level surface with no other vertical forces:

$$F_N=W$$

This is a special equilibrium relationship, not a rule that the normal force always equals weight in every situation.

Example 3: Scale reading versus net force

A person stands still on a bathroom scale that reads 600 N. Explain why the net force on the person is 0 N even though the scale reading is 600 N.

Teacher answer / support: Scale/support force = 600 N upward and weight = 600 N downward, so the vector sum is 0 N. The scale reading is one force, not the net force.

You Try It 3: Dynamic equilibrium

A cart moves at constant speed in a straight line across a level floor. Classify the motion as static equilibrium, dynamic equilibrium, or not equilibrium. State what must be true about the net force.

Teacher answer / support: Dynamic equilibrium. Constant speed in a straight line means unchanged velocity, so net force is 0 N.

Vocabulary: Define the Terms

You have now seen these words in the reading, images, and practice. Use this table to consolidate the physics meaning of each term.

Vocabulary termDefinition
mechanical equilibriumA condition in which the net force on an object is zero, so its motion is not changing.
static equilibriumMechanical equilibrium for an object that remains at rest.
dynamic equilibriumMechanical equilibrium for an object moving at constant speed in a straight line.
tensionA pulling force transmitted through a stretched rope, string, or cable.
weightThe gravitational force acting on an object.
support forceA force exerted by a supporting surface on an object.
normal forceA support force that acts perpendicular to the supporting surface.
net forceThe vector sum of all forces acting on one object.

Summary

  • Mechanical equilibrium means the vector sum of all forces on the object is zero: $\Sigma F=0$.
  • Static equilibrium describes an object at rest with zero net force.
  • Dynamic equilibrium describes constant-speed straight-line motion with zero net force; this relationship is required by the Unit 1 assessment plan.
  • Equilibrium does not require every individual force to be equal. Several supports can share the total load in unequal amounts.
  • For a level supported object at rest with no other vertical forces, the support/normal force equals the object’s weight: $F_N=W$.
  • A scale reading can show the magnitude of a support force even while the net force is zero.
Summary notes / questions / connections

Teaching note: Listen for “zero net force” as the common condition connecting rest and constant straight-line motion. Guard against the shortcut that equilibrium means equal individual forces.

Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy it is temptingCheck instead
Saying equilibrium means no forces actThe object may appear completely still.List the actual forces, then check whether their vector sum is zero.
Assuming all support forces must be equalSymmetric examples make equal sharing common.Use the total equilibrium requirement; individual supports may share the load unequally.
Thinking equilibrium only applies to restThe word “balance” is often associated with being still.Check for constant-speed straight-line motion as dynamic equilibrium.
Treating a scale reading as net forceThe displayed number is labeled as a force.The scale reports a support-force magnitude; combine it with weight and other forces to find net force.
Using $F_N=W$ in every situationIt works for the common book-on-level-table example.Use it only when the object is at rest on a level surface and no other vertical forces alter the balance.
