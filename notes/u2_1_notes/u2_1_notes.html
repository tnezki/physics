Motion and Reference Frames

Section 2.1

Motion only makes sense after you decide what you are measuring it relative to.

Designed for about 20–30 minutes of reading, reflection, and practice.

Learning Target

Students will explain that motion is relative and describe an object’s motion using an appropriate frame of reference.

  • I can explain why an object can be moving relative to one reference frame but not another.
  • I can identify the reference frame used in a motion description.
  • I can compare motion from two different points of view and explain why everyday motion is usually described relative to Earth’s surface.

Motion sounds simple until two observers disagree. A student sitting on a bus may say a backpack on the seat is not moving. A person standing on the sidewalk may say that same backpack is moving east at the speed of the bus. Physics does not fix this disagreement by choosing one observer as “correct.” Instead, physics asks a more careful question: moving relative to what?

A reference frame is the object, place, or coordinate system used to judge position and motion. Once a frame is chosen, you can describe whether an object changes position in that frame. If its position stays the same, the object is at rest in that frame. If its position changes, the object is moving in that frame. The frame is part of the measurement, even when the sentence does not say so out loud.

Motion is relative. “Moving” and “at rest” are incomplete ideas unless the reference frame is known.

Position Is Always Measured Relative to Something

Position tells where an object is compared with a reference. In a classroom, you might describe a chair as “two meters from the door.” On a road, you might describe a car as “one kilometer east of school.” Both descriptions use a reference. The door and the school help define where the object is.

This matters because a change in position also depends on the reference. A passenger can stay in the same seat for ten minutes, so the passenger’s position relative to the seat does not change. During those same ten minutes, the bus may travel several kilometers, so the passenger’s position relative to Earth changes continuously. One object can therefore have more than one valid motion description at the same moment.

Good physics language makes the frame visible. Compare “the phone is moving” with “the phone is moving east relative to the road.” The second statement is stronger because another person could use the same frame and check the description.

Stop and Reflect

Rewrite the sentence “The cup is not moving” so that it names a reference frame and cannot be misunderstood.

Different Viewpoints Can Both Be Correct

Relative motion becomes easiest to see when two observers move differently. Suppose a student walks toward the front of a train. Relative to the train, the student may be moving at only 1 m/s. Relative to the ground, the student also shares the train’s motion. If the train moves east at 9 m/s and the student walks east at 1 m/s relative to the train, the student moves east at 10 m/s relative to the ground.

The important reasoning step is not the arithmetic; it is keeping the frames straight. “1 m/s east” and “10 m/s east” can both describe the same student because they answer different measurement questions. A common error is to switch frames halfway through an explanation without noticing. When that happens, quantities that belong to different frames get mixed together.

Worked Example 1

Situation: Kai walks toward the front of a train moving east at 9 m/s. Kai walks at 1 m/s relative to the train. Explain the two valid motion descriptions.

  1. Relative to the train, Kai moves east at 1 m/s.
  2. Relative to the ground, Kai shares the train's 9 m/s eastward motion and walks another 1 m/s east.
  3. So Kai moves east at 10 m/s relative to the ground.
  4. The values differ because the reference frames differ.
You Try It 1

Zoe walks toward the front of a train moving east at 9 m/s. Zoe walks at 3 m/s relative to the train. Describe Zoe’s motion relative to the train and relative to the ground.

Why Earth Is the Everyday Default

Most everyday motion is described relative to Earth’s surface. Roads, buildings, fields, addresses, and classroom walls are approximately fixed in this frame, so Earth gives people a convenient shared reference. A speed limit sign, for example, is intended to describe vehicle motion relative to the road, not relative to another car.

Earth is useful, but it is not the only valid frame. The source-reference image below reminds us that Earth itself is moving relative to the Sun. Describing motion relative to Earth is a practical choice for ordinary situations, not a claim that Earth is absolutely at rest.

Finding the Frame from Clues

Sometimes the frame is stated directly: “relative to the train,” “relative to the road,” or “as measured from the shore.” Other times you must infer it from clues. If a backpack “stays beside Sam on the seat,” the fixed seat and passenger identify the vehicle frame. If trees “move backward past the window,” the observer is using the moving vehicle as a frame to describe the trees.

When comparing two viewpoints, make a simple two-column mental model: observer/frame and motion description. Do not judge the descriptions until you know which frame belongs to each statement.

Worked Example 2

Situation: “The backpack remains beside Sam on the seat while trees move backward past the window.” Identify the frame used when the backpack is described as remaining beside Sam.

  1. The backpack stays at a fixed position relative to Sam and the seat.
  2. That cue identifies the vehicle/passenger frame.
  3. In that frame the backpack is at rest.
You Try It 2

Comparing Two Descriptions Without Creating a Contradiction

When two motion descriptions seem to conflict, check three things: the object being described, the reference frame, and the direction/magnitude information. If the frames differ, the descriptions may both be correct. If the frames are supposed to be the same, then a real contradiction may exist.

This habit is more important than memorizing examples. Later in physics you will compare velocities, forces, and other quantities in different situations. Naming what each measurement is relative to prevents many errors before calculation begins.

Worked Example 3

Situation: A passenger says a phone on the seat is at rest. A sidewalk observer says the phone is moving east with the bus. Compare the statements.

  1. The passenger uses the bus frame, so the phone is at rest relative to the seat.
  2. The sidewalk observer uses the Earth frame, so the phone moves east with the bus.
  3. There is no contradiction because the frames are different.
  4. Earth is convenient for ordinary travel because roads and locations are fixed in that frame.
You Try It 3

A rider says a bag is at rest on the train floor while a station observer says the bag is moving west. Explain how both statements can be correct and name both frames.

Stop and Reflect

In one or two sentences, explain the single most important rule you should use whenever two people disagree about whether something is moving.
