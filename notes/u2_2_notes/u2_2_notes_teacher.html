Speed and Velocity
How fast is not the same question as how fast and in which direction.

Mathematical idea: Average speed is a distance-to-time ratio; velocity adds direction to that motion description.

You will calculate average speed, compare momentary and whole-trip speed, and use the circular-track source figure to decide when velocity changes.

Reading 1: Measuring How Fast

Before Galileo, words such as “slow” and “fast” were often used without measurement. The textbook describes Galileo’s improvement: compare the distance traveled with the time required. Speed is the distance traveled per unit of time.

The units tell the same story as the idea. Meters per second means meters divided by seconds; kilometers per hour means kilometers divided by hours. The slash in m/s is read “per,” so it reminds us that speed is a distance-to-time comparison.

For an entire trip, average speed is total distance divided by the total time interval. It does not say that the object moved at that same speed during every moment. A car can stop at a light, move quickly on an open road, and still have one average speed for the whole trip.

The textbook’s speed table helps show that a numerical value only makes sense with its units. A speed of “20” is incomplete; 20 m/s and 20 km/h describe very different motions.

When distance and time are known, average speed can be calculated. When speed is treated as constant, the same relationship can also be used to find distance or time.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why is “fast” less useful scientifically than a speed such as 8 m/s?
  2. A cyclist covers 16 m in 2 s. Explain in words what the quotient 16 m ÷ 2 s tells you about the motion.

Teacher answer: A measured speed gives distance per time; 16 m ÷ 2 s means the cyclist covers 8 m each second on average.

Math Notes Average Speed, Distance, and Time

Average speed compares total distance with total time:

$$v=\frac{d}{t}$$

When the motion is treated as constant speed, the same relationship can be rearranged:

$$d=vt$$ $$t=\frac{d}{v}$$

Use matching units. If distance is in meters and time is in seconds, speed is in meters per second (m/s).

Example 1: Average Speed of a Cart

A cart travels 150 meters in 12 seconds.

  1. Calculate the cart’s average speed.
  2. Write the answer with units.
  3. Explain what the value means in the context of the trip.

Teacher answer: 150 m ÷ 12 s = 12.5 m/s. Listen for an interpretation such as “the cart covered 12.5 m per second on average.”

You Try It 1: Scooter Trip

A scooter travels 240 meters in 20 seconds.

  1. Calculate the average speed.
  2. If the scooter kept that speed for 15 seconds, how far would it travel?
  3. Explain which relationship you used for each result.

Teacher answer: 240 m ÷ 20 s = 12 m/s; at that constant speed for 15 s, d = vt = 180 m.

Reading 2: A Moment Versus a Whole Trip

Things in motion often speed up, slow down, or stop. The instantaneous speed is the speed at a particular instant. A car speedometer gives this kind of reading: it tells what the car’s speed is at that moment, not the average for the entire trip.

The textbook contrasts that momentary reading with average speed. A driver might travel 320 km in 4 h and have an average speed of 80 km/h even though the speedometer was sometimes below 80 km/h and sometimes above it.

This difference matters whenever motion varies. If a trip begins from rest, then some instantaneous speeds are smaller than the final average. To end with a high average, other parts of the trip may need instantaneous speeds larger than that average.

Distance and time data can describe the whole trip without telling every moment of the motion. That is why two trips can have the same average speed but very different patterns of stopping, speeding up, or slowing down.

A speeding ticket is based on the speed measured at the moment of observation, not on the driver’s average speed since leaving home. The measurement question determines which kind of speed matters.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Explain why a speedometer reading of 70 km/h does not tell you the average speed for the whole trip.
  2. Can two trips have the same average speed but different instantaneous speeds during the trips? Use an example to support your answer.

Teacher answer: A speedometer is instantaneous; an average summarizes total distance and time. Same average does not require the same speed history.

Example 2: Same Average, Different Motion

Trip A covers 60 km in 1 h at a nearly steady speed. Trip B also covers 60 km in 1 h but includes a 10-minute stop.

  1. Compare the two trips’ average speeds.
  2. Which trip must include a higher instantaneous speed at some time? Explain.
  3. Why does the average alone not describe the whole motion story?

Teacher answer: Both trips average 60 km/h. Trip B must exceed 60 km/h at some time to make up for the stop.

You Try It 2: Dashboard and Trip Computer

A car’s dashboard reads 50 km/h at one moment. The trip computer reports an average of 35 km/h since the trip began.

  1. Which value is instantaneous speed?
  2. Which value is average speed?
  3. Give one reasonable motion history that could produce both readings.

Teacher answer: 50 km/h is instantaneous; 35 km/h is the trip average. Any reasonable history with slower earlier motion/stops can fit.

Reading 3: Direction Turns Speed into Velocity

Velocity includes both speed and direction. “60 km/h” is a speed; “60 km/h north” is a velocity. Direction is not an extra decoration—it is part of the physical quantity.

A scalar quantity has magnitude only. Speed is scalar because one number with a unit is enough to describe it. A vector quantity has both magnitude and direction. Velocity is vector because both pieces are needed.

Constant speed means the speed does not increase or decrease. Constant velocity is stricter: both the speed and the direction must stay unchanged. In ordinary motion, constant direction means a straight path.

If either speed or direction changes, velocity changes. The textbook emphasizes this with a car traveling around a curved track. Even if the speedometer reading stays the same, the car points in a new direction from moment to moment.

Image read: Trace the path with your finger and mark two places where the car could have the same speed but different directions. Explain what that means for velocity.

This distinction prepares us for acceleration: a change in direction is a change in velocity, even when the speed stays constant.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Use the circular-track image to explain why constant speed does not always mean constant velocity.
  2. Two cars both travel at 100 km/h, one east and one west. Compare their speeds and their velocities.

Teacher answer: On the curved track the car’s direction changes, so velocity changes even if speed is constant. East and west cars have equal speed but opposite velocities.

Example 3: Straight Road or Curved Road?

Car A travels east at 20 m/s on a straight road. Car B travels around a circular track at a steady 20 m/s.

  1. Which car has constant speed?
  2. Which car has constant velocity?
  3. Use speed and direction to justify your answers.

Teacher answer: Both cars can have constant speed; only Car A has constant velocity because its direction stays east.

You Try It 3: Runner Changes Direction

A runner moves north at 6 m/s, turns a corner, and continues east at 6 m/s.

  1. Did the runner’s speed change?
  2. Did the runner’s velocity change?
  3. Explain why direction matters even when the speed value is unchanged.

Teacher answer: Speed stays 6 m/s, but velocity changes because the direction changes from north to east.

Summary

  • Average speed is total distance divided by total time: $v=\frac{{d}}{{t}}$.
  • Instantaneous speed describes one moment; average speed describes an entire time interval.
  • Velocity includes both speed and direction.
  • Constant velocity requires constant speed and constant direction; a turn changes velocity even if speed stays the same.
  • For constant-speed situations, $d=vt$ and $t=\frac{{d}}{{v}}$ are rearrangements of the same distance–time relationship.
Summary notes / questions / connections

Teaching note: Summary should preserve the hierarchy: speed → instantaneous/average; velocity = speed + direction; constant velocity requires both pieces constant.
