Speed and Velocity

Section 2.2

Speed tells how fast; velocity tells how fast and which way.

Designed for about 20–30 minutes of reading, reflection, and practice.

Learning Target

Students will distinguish between speed and velocity and use distance, time, and direction to describe motion.

  • I can calculate average speed from distance and time.
  • I can distinguish instantaneous speed from average speed.
  • I can explain that velocity includes both speed and direction and compare examples of constant speed, constant velocity, and changing velocity.

Physics needs a precise way to describe how fast motion occurs. Words such as “fast” and “slow” depend on opinion unless they are tied to measurements. Speed compares the distance traveled with the time taken. If two runners cover the same distance, the runner who takes less time has the greater average speed. If two runners move for the same time, the runner who covers more distance has the greater average speed.

Average Speed

\(\text{average speed}=\dfrac{\text{total distance traveled}}{\text{total elapsed time}}\). Use the whole trip for both the distance and the time.

Units matter because speed is a rate. Meters per second (m/s), kilometers per hour (km/h), and miles per hour (mi/h) all communicate “distance per time.” A number without a unit is not a complete physical speed.

A student writes “The average speed is 12.” What information is missing, and why does it matter?

Average Speed Describes an Interval

Average speed uses the total distance and the total elapsed time over an interval. The object does not need to move at that speed every moment. A cyclist might move quickly, stop at a light, and then move quickly again. The stop still counts in the elapsed time, so the trip’s average speed can be lower than the cyclist’s speed while actually moving.

The approved distance–time graphs below are useful because the same time interval can contain different motion patterns. When distance increases steadily, the object covers equal distances in equal times. When the graph becomes steeper, distance is being added more quickly. A flat part means the distance is not changing during that time interval.

Worked Example 1

Situation: A cart travels a total distance of 120 m in 10 s. Calculate its average speed.

  1. Identify total distance: 120 m.
  2. Identify total time: 10 s.
  3. Compute \(120\text{{ m}}/10\text{{ s}}=12\text{{ m/s}}\).
  4. Include the unit because the answer is a rate.
You Try It 1

A cart travels a total distance of 60 m in 6 s. Calculate its average speed and include units.

Instantaneous Speed Describes One Moment

A speedometer is designed to show instantaneous speed: the speed at a particular instant. This is different from an average taken over an entire trip. If your car’s speedometer reads 25 m/s right now, that does not mean the average speed for the last twenty minutes was 25 m/s. You may have accelerated, slowed down, or stopped during that interval.

A useful way to separate the ideas is to ask, “Is this value about one moment or an interval?” A dashboard reading is about one moment. Total distance divided by total time is about an interval.

Stop and Reflect

During a 30-minute trip, a driver’s speedometer reads 20 m/s at one instant. Give one reason the trip’s average speed could be less than 20 m/s.

Worked Example 2

Situation: A rider’s speedometer reads 12 m/s at one instant. The rider’s average speed over the entire trip is 10 m/s. Explain both values.

  1. 12 m/s is the instantaneous speed at that moment.
  2. 10 m/s comes from total distance divided by total elapsed time.
  3. The values need not match because one describes a moment and the other describes the whole interval.
You Try It 2

On a moving walkway, a rider’s speedometer reads 13 m/s at one instant. The average speed for the whole trip is 10 m/s. Explain what each value means.

Velocity Adds Direction

Velocity includes both speed and direction. “5 m/s” is a speed. “5 m/s east” is a velocity. Because direction is part of velocity, two objects can have the same speed but different velocities. A car traveling north at 15 m/s and a car traveling south at 15 m/s have equal speeds but different velocities.

The selected course figure below highlights a second important point: an object can maintain constant speed while its velocity changes if its direction changes. Going around a curve is an example. The speedometer might remain steady, but the direction of the velocity is continually changing.

Stop and Reflect

A car moves around a circular track at a steady 12 m/s. Is its speed constant? Is its velocity constant? Explain both answers.

Constant speed is not automatically constant velocity. Constant velocity requires both constant speed and constant direction.

Worked Example 3

Situation: A robot moves at a constant speed of 5 m/s. It first moves east, then turns north while keeping the same speed. Compare speed and velocity.

  1. The speed remains 5 m/s before and after the turn.
  2. The direction changes from east to north.
  3. Because velocity includes direction, the velocity changes.

Reading Motion Descriptions Carefully

When a problem gives distance and time, first decide whether it is asking for a rate over an interval. When it gives a direction, ask whether the quantity is speed or velocity. When a graph is shown, connect its shape to what the axes actually measure rather than treating the graph as a picture of the path.

These habits keep vocabulary connected to evidence. Speed answers “how fast?” Velocity answers “how fast and which direction?” Average speed summarizes an interval; instantaneous speed describes a moment.
