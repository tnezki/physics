Friction and Real-World Forces
Real surfaces and gravity add forces that change the net force you actually get.

Mathematical idea: Net force is found by combining forces with direction; mass and weight describe different physical quantities.

You will connect microscopic friction, everyday pushing, support forces, and mass-versus-weight evidence to real motion.

Reading 1: Friction Comes from Contact

The force called friction acts when surfaces slide or tend to slide across one another. The source explains that even surfaces that look smooth have microscopic irregularities, and atoms at contact points can attract one another. Moving one surface past another means climbing over irregularities or breaking tiny contact bonds.

Image read: Trace the contact boundary. Mark two places where microscopic irregularities would resist sliding.

The direction of friction opposes the relative sliding or attempted sliding. If a crate is pushed right, friction from the floor acts left. Friction therefore reduces the net force when it opposes an applied force.

Image read: On each panel, label which force opposes the motion or attempted motion. Compare the arrow directions.

An object can remain at rest even while you push it. If friction grows to match the push, the horizontal forces balance, net force is zero, and acceleration is zero.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Use the microscopic image to explain why friction is a contact phenomenon rather than a force that appears from nowhere.
  2. A box is pushed with 60 N right while friction is 45 N left. Find the net force and predict the acceleration direction.

Reading 2: Starting to Slide Is Different from Sliding

Before an object begins sliding, static friction adjusts to oppose the attempted motion up to a maximum value. After the surfaces start sliding, sliding friction acts. For the same pair of surfaces, the source notes that maximum static friction is usually somewhat greater than sliding friction.

This explains why it can take a larger push to start a crate moving than to keep it moving. It also helps explain why locked tires are less effective for stopping than tires that maintain rolling contact; the road-tire interaction can use stronger static friction before sliding begins.

Image read: Identify the tire-road contact. Explain why tread is especially useful for moving water away rather than simply “creating more friction” on dry pavement.

The textbook also points out that ordinary sliding friction between solid surfaces is approximately independent of speed and contact area in the simple model. Wider tires distribute load and reduce heating and wear, but a larger contact patch is not automatically more friction in this model.

Liquids and gases are fluids, so resistance in them is called fluid friction. The familiar example is air resistance, which becomes more important at higher speeds. Unlike the simple sliding-friction model, air resistance increases with speed.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why can an object require more force to start moving than to keep sliding?
  2. Compare ordinary sliding friction with air resistance: how does speed affect each in the source model?

Reading 3: Mass Is Not Weight

An object’s mass is a measure of the amount of matter and of inertia, its resistance to acceleration. Mass does not disappear in space and does not change simply because the strength of gravity changes.

An object’s weight is different: it is the gravitational force acting on the object. Near Earth, weight points downward. A surface can provide an upward support force on an object resting on it.

Image read: Compare the two vertical arrows. If the foot is at rest and no other vertical forces matter, what must be true about their magnitudes?

The astronaut illustration emphasizes the mass-weight distinction. An object can be nearly weightless in a drifting spacecraft but still be difficult to shake because its mass and inertia remain.

Image read: Circle the evidence that the object still resists acceleration even though ordinary scale weight is absent.

Image read: In the string demonstration, compare a slow pull with a sudden jerk. Which case gives the ball more time to accelerate, and which case most clearly reveals its inertia?

Near Earth, weight is about 10 newtons for each kilogram of mass. Moving the same object to the Moon changes its weight because gravity is weaker, but its mass—and therefore its resistance to acceleration—stays the same.

Stop and Discuss

  1. How does the astronaut image show that mass and weight are not the same quantity?
  2. For an object resting on a level surface, explain how weight and support force can balance without being a third-law pair.
Math NotesWeight and net force with friction
$$W=mg$$

Near Earth, use $g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2$ when a simple estimate is intended.

$$F_{net}=F_{applied}-F_f$$

This one-dimensional form applies when the applied force and friction point in opposite directions.

Vocabulary: Define the Terms

You have now seen these words in the reading, images, and practice. Use this table to consolidate the physics meaning of each term.

Vocabulary termDefinition
frictionA force that opposes sliding or attempted sliding between surfaces.
static frictionFriction that acts while surfaces are not sliding relative to each other; it adjusts up to a maximum.
sliding frictionFriction acting while surfaces slide; for the same surfaces it is usually less than the maximum static friction.
fluid frictionResistance experienced by an object moving through a fluid such as water or air.
air resistanceFluid friction from air; it generally increases as speed increases.
massA measure of matter and inertia; it does not depend on location.
inertiaThe resistance of an object to a change in its motion.
weightThe gravitational force acting on an object.
support forceThe upward contact force a surface exerts on an object it supports.

Summary

  • Friction opposes sliding or attempted sliding and changes the net force.
  • Static friction acts before sliding and can grow to a maximum; sliding friction acts during sliding and is usually smaller than the maximum static friction.
  • Air resistance is fluid friction and increases with speed.
  • Mass measures inertia and remains the same with location; weight is the gravitational force and can change with location.
  • $W=mg$ models weight, and $F_{net}=F_{applied}-F_f$ models a simple push-against-friction situation.
Summary notes / questions / connections

Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy it is temptingCheck instead
Friction always equals the applied force.That is true while static friction is matching a push below its maximum.Once forces are unbalanced, friction and applied force need not be equal.
Static and sliding friction are identical.Both oppose relative motion.Static friction adjusts up to a maximum; sliding friction applies after sliding begins and is usually smaller.
Mass and weight are interchangeable.Everyday language often uses “weight” for amount of matter.Mass measures inertia; weight is a force due to gravity.
More contact area always means more friction.A larger contact patch looks like it should grip more.Use the source’s simple model: dry sliding friction is not directly determined by contact area.
