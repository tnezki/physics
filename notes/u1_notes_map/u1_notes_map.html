  • U1-S1-IC3 — I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

Summary Targets

  • Measurements and observations provide evidence; inferences and models interpret that evidence.
  • Hypotheses, laws, and theories have different roles and remain accountable to evidence.
  • Scientific claims must be testable in principle.
  • Equations describe relationships among physical quantities and should guide reasoning before calculation.

Common Mistakes Targets

  • Treating an inference as a direct observation.
  • Assuming a plausible-sounding claim is scientific without a possible test.
  • Using law and theory as if one simply “graduates” into the other.
  • Treating a formula as a calculation recipe with no physical meaning.

1.2 Forces and Newton’s First Law

Summary Targets

  • Newton’s First Law says rest or constant straight-line motion continues when net force is zero.
  • Inertia is a property of matter that describes resistance to changes in motion; it is not a force.
  • An object can have several forces acting on it while the net force is zero.
  • Support force and weight are common forces that can balance on supported objects.

Common Mistakes Targets

  • Saying a moving object needs a net forward force to keep moving at constant velocity.
  • Calling inertia a force.
  • Equating zero net force with no forces acting.
  • Forgetting weight or support force in simple supported-object force models.

Summary Targets

  • A force is a vector because a complete description requires magnitude and direction.
  • Forces in the same direction add; opposite-direction forces combine by direction-aware subtraction.
  • The net force/resultant describes the combined effect of all forces on the object.
  • Scaled arrow length and arrow direction carry physical meaning; a simple 2-D resultant can be represented as a diagonal.

Common Mistakes Targets

  • Adding magnitudes while ignoring direction.
  • Treating arrow length as decoration.
  • Reporting a net force magnitude without a direction.
  • Reversing the resultant direction or choosing the direction of the smaller force.

Summary Targets

  • Mechanical equilibrium means the net force is zero.
  • Static equilibrium describes an object at rest with zero net force.
  • Dynamic equilibrium describes constant straight-line motion with zero net force.
  • Balanced forces can be nonzero individually; their vector sum is zero.
  • The equilibrium rule can be used to infer missing upward/downward or left/right forces.

Common Mistakes Targets

  • Equating equilibrium with rest only.
  • Saying forces disappear when an object is in equilibrium.
  • Assuming constant speed always means equilibrium even when direction changes.
  • Balancing magnitudes without checking opposite directions.

Summary Targets

  • A force model begins by defining the system and including only forces acting on that object.
  • Force arrows represent interactions with direction and relative magnitude; together they determine net force.
  • Balanced forces mean zero net force and unchanged motion; unbalanced forces mean motion changes.
  • A good force model must agree with the observed motion/evidence and can be revised when it does not.

Common Mistakes Targets

  • Including a force the chosen object exerts on something else.
  • Omitting support force or weight from supported-object models.
  • Matching a constant-velocity description to an unbalanced force diagram.
  • Critiquing a model without naming the physical evidence or rule that makes it inconsistent.
