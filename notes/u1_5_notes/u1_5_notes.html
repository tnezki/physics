Modeling Forces in the Real World
Build force diagrams that agree with the interactions, the net force, and the observed motion.

Mathematical idea: A force model is internally consistent when its vector sum agrees with the object’s described motion.

Reading 1: A force model begins by choosing one object and its interactions

A useful force diagram is not a drawing of everything happening in a scene. It is a model of the forces acting on one chosen object. Before drawing arrows, name the object being modeled. Then ask what other objects interact with it: Earth, a floor or table, a rope, a spring, another person, or another surface. Each force arrow should be connected to one of those interactions.

Force arrows carry specific information. The arrow points in the direction of the force, and its length can show relative magnitude when the diagram is scaled or when comparison matters. A motion arrow and a force arrow are not the same thing. An object can move to the right while its net force is zero, and an object can have a leftward net force while it is still moving right but slowing down.

The tablecloth demonstration is a good modeling test because the scene contains several objects. If the chosen object is one dish, Earth pulls downward, the tablecloth provides an upward support interaction while contact remains, and brief friction can act horizontally. The cloth’s large horizontal motion should not be copied as a large forward force arrow on the dish unless the interaction actually supplies that force.

Image read: Choose one dish as the object. On the image, identify which other objects can actually exert forces on that dish.

A bathroom-scale situation highlights the same modeling discipline. If the chosen object is the person, draw the person’s weight downward and the scale’s support force upward. If the chosen object were the scale instead, the relevant forces would be different. A force model becomes much clearer once the “object of interest” is fixed.

Image read: Put a box around the person as the modeled object, then name the source of each force that acts on that person.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why must a force diagram identify one object before arrows are drawn?
  2. Using either image, name each force and the interacting object that produces it.

Example 1: Model a backpack on the floor

A backpack rests on the floor. Choose the backpack as the object, identify the interacting objects that produce its main forces, describe the force arrows you would draw, and decide whether the forces are balanced.

You Try It 1: Model one dish during the quick pull

Choose one dish in the tablecloth scene. Describe the vertical forces and the brief horizontal interaction on the dish. Explain why you should not draw an “inertia force.”

Reading 2: A correct force model must agree with the motion description

A force model and a motion description are two representations of the same physical situation. Once all force vectors on the object are identified, combine them to find the net force. Then compare that result with the motion. If the net force is zero, Newton’s First Law predicts unchanged motion: the object can remain at rest or continue at constant speed in a straight line.

If the net force is nonzero, the motion must be changing. The object may speed up, slow down, or turn depending on how the net force relates to its current motion. Unit 1 does not require a numerical acceleration formula, but it does require the cause-and-effect statement that nonzero net force means motion changes.

Figure 2.6 is a useful consistency check because the force vectors are simple. Equal opposite arrows give zero net force, so they can match rest or constant straight-line motion. Unequal opposite arrows give a nonzero net force toward the larger force, so they cannot match a description of perfectly constant straight-line motion unless the force diagram or motion description is incomplete.

Image read: For each row, write a motion description that could be consistent with the net force shown. Remember that zero net force allows both rest and constant straight-line motion.

This kind of comparison is one of the strongest ways to critique a model. Instead of saying “the drawing looks wrong,” identify the exact conflict: perhaps an arrow is missing, the relative arrow lengths are inconsistent with the stated forces, the net force is nonzero while the motion is described as unchanged, or a student has drawn a force simply because the object is moving.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What motion descriptions are possible when a force model has zero net force?
  2. Why is a diagram with 60 N right and 40 N left inconsistent with a claim of unchanged constant-speed straight-line motion?

Example 2: Find the representation mismatch

A cart’s force diagram shows 60 N right and 40 N left, but the written description says “the cart moves at constant speed in a straight line.” Explain the mismatch and give one revision to the force diagram or motion description that would make the representations agree.

You Try It 2: Build a consistent pair

Create a one-dimensional force description with zero net force and pair it with a motion description that is consistent with Newton’s First Law. Explain why the two representations agree.

Reading 3: Real situations require several force ideas at the same time

The hanging-person and scaffold figures combine the unit’s central modeling ideas. Weight acts downward, while tension forces follow the directions of ropes. Because force is a vector, two angled tensions can combine to create an upward resultant even though neither force points straight up. The geometry and arrow lengths matter to the model.

Image read: Trace the two tension vectors and their combined effect. Note that the force arrows follow the ropes rather than pointing in the direction of motion.

Equilibrium adds another layer. If the hanging person or scaffold is at rest, the vector sum of all forces must be zero. That does not require the individual tensions to be equal. Different rope angles or different positions of the load can change the separate tension magnitudes while the total force still balances the weight.

Image read: Check the model by grouping all upward forces and all downward forces. Write the net-force conclusion only after the vectors are accounted for.

A strong explanation cites evidence from the model. Instead of saying “the ropes hold her up,” name the tension vectors and explain how their combined effect opposes weight. Instead of saying “the scale measures net force,” identify the scale’s support force and combine it with weight. Physics vocabulary is most useful when it points to a specific relationship in the diagram or situation.

When you critique an incorrect force model, use a repeatable process. First identify the chosen object. Second check whether every arrow comes from a real interaction and points in a physically reasonable direction. Third combine the vectors to determine the net force. Fourth compare the net force with the motion description. Finally, revise the model and explain exactly why the revision is better supported by the evidence.

Stop and Discuss

  1. How can multiple angled tension vectors support a downward weight?
  2. List the steps you would use to critique a force diagram that does not agree with a motion description.

Example 3: Critique a missing-force model

A student draws only a downward weight arrow for a person standing still on a bathroom scale. Identify the missing interaction, revise the model in words or a sketch, and explain why the revised model matches the observed motion.

You Try It 3: Defend a hanging-object model

A hanging object is supported by two angled ropes and remains at rest. Describe the three main force vectors and explain how the two tension forces can combine to balance the weight.

Vocabulary: Define the Terms

You have now seen these words in the reading, images, and practice. Use this table to consolidate the physics meaning of each term.

Vocabulary termDefinition
force modelA simplified representation of the forces and relationships relevant to a chosen physical situation.
force diagramA diagram that uses labeled force vectors to show forces acting on one chosen object.
net forceThe vector sum of all forces acting on the chosen object.
vectorA quantity with both magnitude and direction.
inertiaThe tendency of matter to resist a change in motion.
support forceA force from a surface that supports an object, usually perpendicular to the surface.
weightThe gravitational force on an object.
tensionA pulling force transmitted by a stretched rope, string, or cable.
equilibriumA condition of zero net force and therefore no change in motion.
evidenceObservations, measurements, or model features used to support or challenge an explanation.

Summary

  • A force diagram models the forces acting on one chosen object, not every object or motion in the scene.
  • Every force arrow should come from an identifiable interaction and should communicate direction and relative magnitude.
  • Combine the force vectors to determine net force, then compare the net force with the motion description.
  • Zero net force is consistent with rest or constant-speed straight-line motion; nonzero net force means the motion changes.
  • A strong critique identifies a specific modeling error, revises it, and uses evidence, vectors, Newton’s First Law, and equilibrium vocabulary to justify the revision.
Summary notes / questions / connections

Common Mistakes

MistakeWhy it is temptingCheck instead
Drawing arrows for motion instead of forcesThe object’s direction of travel is visually obvious.Ask what interaction causes each arrow. Motion itself is not a force.
Drawing forces from every object in the sceneThe whole picture feels like the system being modeled.Choose one object first; include only forces acting on that object.
Adding an inertia forceInertia helps explain why motion resists change.Treat inertia as a property; force arrows require interactions.
Accepting a force diagram that conflicts with the motionEach representation may look reasonable by itself.Find net force, then check whether rest/constant motion/changing motion agrees.
Saying a model is wrong without evidenceIt is easier than naming the precise problem.Identify the missing/extra force, wrong direction, unsupported magnitude, or net-force/motion mismatch, then revise it.
