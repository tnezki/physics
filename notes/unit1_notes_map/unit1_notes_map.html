  • U1-S1-IC3 — I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

S1-B1 · Measurement comes before explanation

I-can routing: U1-S1-IC1, U1-S1-IC3 · Reading depth: substantial

Reading goal: Develop a phenomenon-centered opening around Eratosthenes: separate what was observed/measured from what the geometric model allowed scientists to infer. Use the image to drive the reading rather than decorating it.

Essential ideas
  • Measurements are records of observable quantities, while inferences are interpretations built from them.
  • A scientific model can connect local measurements to a larger system that cannot be measured directly in the same way.
  • Evidence becomes powerful when the reasoning chain from observation to inference is explicit.

Vocabulary definition targets

observation
Directly recorded information from senses or measuring tools; the target definition must separate what was detected from what is concluded.
inference
An interpretation or conclusion drawn from observations rather than a directly measured fact.
hypothesis
A proposed explanation or prediction framed so evidence can test it.
scientific law
A concise description of a repeatedly observed pattern or relationship; it describes what happens without serving as a complete explanation of why.
scientific theory
A broad, evidence-supported explanatory framework that connects many observations and tested ideas.
evidence
Relevant observations or measurements used to support, challenge, or compare claims and explanations.
model
A purposeful representation of a system or relationship used to describe, explain, or predict physical behavior.
testable claim
A claim for which possible observations or measurements could count for or against it.

Summary targets

  • Physics begins with observations and measurements, but explanations require careful inference.
  • Scientific claims must be testable with possible evidence.
  • Laws, theories, hypotheses, observations, and inferences serve different scientific roles.
  • Models and formulas represent physical relationships and should be interpreted before they are calculated with.
  • Evidence supports some explanations better than others; plausibility alone is not enough.

Common mistakes

  • Treating an inference as an observation.
  • Assuming an explanation is scientific because it sounds plausible.
  • Treating a formula as a rule with no physical meaning.

S2-B1 · Objects do not need a forward force to keep moving

I-can routing: U1-S2-IC1, U1-S2-IC2 · Reading depth: substantial

Reading goal: Open with inertia phenomena and prediction. Let students notice that the object tends to keep its current state while a nearby support/card/tablecloth changes motion, then name the Newton First Law idea after the phenomenon.

Essential ideas
  • Objects resist changes in velocity rather than “wanting” to stop or move.
  • Inertia belongs to matter; it is not a hidden force arrow.
  • A change in motion requires a nonzero net force, while unchanged motion does not require a forward net force.

Vocabulary definition targets

force
A push or pull arising from an interaction that can contribute to a change in motion.
inertia
The property of matter that resists changes in velocity; it is not an additional force.
Newton’s First Law
When the net force is zero, an object maintains its velocity: rest stays rest and constant straight-line motion stays constant straight-line motion.
net force
The vector sum of all external forces acting on the chosen object or system.
support force
The contact force a supporting surface exerts on an object, directed away from the surface in simple level-support cases.
weight
The gravitational force exerted on an object by a massive body such as Earth.

Summary targets

  • Newton’s First Law connects zero net force with unchanged velocity, not necessarily with rest.
  • Inertia is a property of matter; it does not appear as a force arrow.
  • An object can move while the net force is zero.
  • A supported object can have weight downward and a support force upward at the same time.
  • “No forces” and “balanced forces” are different physical situations even though both can have zero net force.

Common mistakes

  • Calling inertia a force.
  • Assuming motion requires a net forward force.
  • Confusing no force with zero net force.

S3-B1 · Force arrows encode magnitude and direction

I-can routing: U1-S3-IC1, U1-S3-IC3 · Reading depth: substantial

Reading goal: Start from actual force-arrow cases. Make students read arrow direction and relative length as information, then develop why a scalar list of force sizes would lose essential physics.

Essential ideas

  • Net force depends on how directed forces combine, not on magnitudes alone.

Vocabulary definition targets

vector
A quantity represented with both magnitude and direction.
magnitude
The size or amount of a vector quantity, represented by arrow length when a diagram is scaled.
direction
The orientation in which a vector points; direction is part of the quantity, not a decoration.
resultant
A single vector that has the same combined effect as two or more vectors.
net force
The resultant of all forces acting on the chosen object or system.

Summary targets

  • Force is a vector, so both magnitude and direction matter.
  • Scaled arrow length represents relative or numerical magnitude.
  • Forces in the same direction reinforce; opposite-direction forces partially or completely cancel.
  • The resultant/net-force arrow points in the direction of the combined force effect.
  • Vector diagrams can be read forward to find a resultant or backward to infer a missing force.

Common mistakes
