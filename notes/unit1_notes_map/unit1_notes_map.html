1.1 · Thinking Like a Physicist

Learning Target: Students will explain how physics uses observation, measurement, evidence, models, and testable claims to understand the natural world.

Exact I-cans

U1-S1-IC1 I can distinguish between an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, and theory.
U1-S1-IC2 I can explain why a claim must be testable to be scientific.
U1-S1-IC3 I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

Vocabulary Preview

observationinferencehypothesisscientific lawscientific theoryevidencemodeltestable claim

Planned Story Sequence

U1-S1-B1 · developed

Observations before explanations

Reading goal: Establish the difference between what is directly observed or measured and what is inferred from those observations.

  • Physics begins with observable events and measurements, not with labels attached after the fact.
  • An observation records what can be detected or measured; an inference interprets what those observations might mean.
  • Good models and explanations keep the evidence/interpretation boundary visible.

Scientific ideas have different jobs

Reading goal: Distinguish hypothesis, law, and theory by their role and connect scientific status to testability rather than certainty.

1.2 · Forces and Newton’s First Law

Learning Target: Students will explain Newton’s First Law and use the idea of inertia to predict whether an object’s motion will stay the same or change.

Exact I-cans

U1-S2-IC1 I can state Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
U1-S2-IC2 I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
U1-S2-IC3 I can distinguish between “no forces acting” and “no net force acting.” and identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.

Vocabulary Preview

forceinertiaNewton’s First Lawnet forcesupport forceweight

Planned Story Sequence

U1-S2-B1 · developed

Motion does not need a continuing net push

Reading goal: Build Newton’s First Law from the contrast between motion changing and motion remaining unchanged.

  • An object keeps its state of rest or constant straight-line motion unless a net external force changes that motion.
  • Force is connected to change in motion, not to motion itself.
  • Predictions should begin by asking whether the net force is zero.

U1-S2-B2 · developed

Inertia is a property, not a force

Reading goal: Use familiar inertia demonstrations to separate an object’s resistance to changes in motion from an interaction force.

  • Inertia describes the tendency of matter to resist changes in velocity.
  • Inertia is not an extra force that pushes an object forward.
  • Objects with matter have inertia whether they are moving or at rest.

1.3 · Net Force and Vectors

Learning Target: Students will represent forces as vectors and determine the direction and relative size of the net force in one-dimensional and simple two-dimensional situations.

Exact I-cans

U1-S3-IC1 I can explain why force is a vector quantity.
U1-S3-IC2 I can combine forces acting in the same direction or opposite directions.
U1-S3-IC3 I can identify the resultant force from a simple vector diagram and use arrow length and direction to compare force strength and direction.

Vocabulary Preview

vectormagnitudedirectionresultantnet force

Planned Story Sequence

U1-S3-B1 · developed

A force vector needs magnitude and direction

Reading goal: Make arrow length and arrow direction carry explicit physical meaning.

  • Force is a vector because both magnitude and direction are required to describe it.
  • A vector arrow is not decoration: its orientation encodes direction and its length can encode relative magnitude when a scale is defined.
  • Two forces with equal magnitudes can have different effects when directions differ.

Same direction adds; opposite direction competes

1.4 · Equilibrium and Balanced Motion

Learning Target: Students will determine whether an object is in mechanical equilibrium and explain how balanced forces relate to rest or constant straight-line motion.

Exact I-cans

U1-S4-IC1 I can recognize static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium.
U1-S4-IC2 I can explain why an object at rest can still have forces acting on it.
U1-S4-IC3 I can explain why an object moving at constant speed in a straight line can be in equilibrium and use the equilibrium rule to reason about upward/downward and left/right forces.

Vocabulary Preview

mechanical equilibriumstatic equilibriumdynamic equilibriumbalanced forcesconstant velocity

Planned Story Sequence

U1-S4-B1 · developed

Equilibrium is a net-force condition

Reading goal: Define mechanical equilibrium through zero net force, then distinguish static and dynamic cases by motion evidence.

  • Mechanical equilibrium means the vector sum of forces is zero.
  • Static equilibrium describes an object at rest with zero net force.
  • Dynamic equilibrium describes constant straight-line motion with zero net force.

Rest can include real forces

Reading goal: Use a balanced support situation to reject the idea that forces disappear when an object is at rest.

1.5 · Modeling Forces in the Real World

Learning Target: Students will create and critique force models for real-world situations involving inertia, support force, net force, and equilibrium.

Exact I-cans

U1-S5-IC1 I can draw a force diagram for an object in a real-world situation.
U1-S5-IC2 I can decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.
U1-S5-IC3 I can explain how the net force affects the object’s motion and critique an incorrect force explanation and revise it using physics vocabulary.

Vocabulary Preview

force modelsysteminteractionnet forceequilibriumevidence

Planned Story Sequence

U1-S5-B1 · developed

Define the system before drawing forces

Reading goal: Make object/system selection the first move in building a real-world force diagram.

  • A force diagram represents forces acting on one selected object/system.
  • Every arrow should correspond to an external interaction with that object.
  • Forces the chosen object exerts on other objects do not belong on its force diagram.

Summary targets:

  • Define the modeled object/system before drawing a force diagram.
  • Include only forces acting on the selected object.
  • Classify balanced versus unbalanced force models.
  • Connect net-force direction to motion change.
  • Critique and revise models so arrows, net force, vocabulary, and motion evidence agree.

Common mistakes:

  • Including forces that act on other objects.
  • Omitting support force or weight.
  • Matching a force diagram to the wrong motion description.
