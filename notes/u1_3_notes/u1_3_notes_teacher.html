Net Force and Vectors

Section 1.3

A force arrow carries two ideas at once: how much and which way.

Learning Target

Students will represent forces as vectors and determine resultant/net force in simple force situations.

  • I can use vector arrow direction and length to represent a force’s direction and relative magnitude.
  • I can combine forces acting in the same or opposite directions to determine net force.
  • I can determine the direction and relative magnitude of the resultant/net force from a simple force-vector diagram.

Vocabulary / Key Notation Preview

vector, magnitude, direction, resultant, net force, \(F_{net}\)

A force is a push or a pull, but a number by itself is not enough to describe a force. A 10 N push to the right and a 10 N push to the left have the same size but point in opposite directions. Because both the size and the direction matter, force is a vector quantity.

A force vector tells both magnitude and direction. The arrow points in the force direction, and its length represents the force’s relative magnitude.

When force arrows are drawn to scale, longer arrows represent larger forces. Two arrows can be the same length but point in different directions, or they can point the same direction but have different lengths. Reading both features is essential. The arrow is not decoration; it is part of the physics information.

A quantity such as speed can be described with a number and unit alone. A vector cannot. Saying “12 N” leaves the force description incomplete; saying “12 N to the right” gives both parts of the vector.

Stop and Reflect

Two force arrows point to the right. One arrow is twice as long as the other. What is the same about the two forces? What is different?

Acceptable reasoning: They have the same direction. The longer arrow represents twice the force magnitude.

Teacher move: Ask students to point to the visual feature that communicates each part of the answer.

Worked Example 1

Situation: Force A is 8 N to the left. Force B is 4 N to the left. Describe how their vector arrows should compare.

  1. Both arrows point left because both forces have the same direction.
  2. Force A has twice the magnitude of Force B.
  3. Answer: A’s arrow should be twice as long as B’s arrow, and both arrows should point left.
You Try It 1

Force C is 3 N upward. Force D is 9 N downward. Describe the direction of each arrow and how their lengths should compare if they are drawn to the same scale.

Answer: C points up and D points down. D’s arrow should be three times as long as C’s.

Listen for: students comparing magnitude from the sign/direction instead of from arrow length.

Combining Forces Along One Line

Most objects have more than one force acting on them. The single force that represents the combined effect of all the forces is the net force, written \(F_{net}\). When forces point in the same direction, their magnitudes add. When forces point in opposite directions, their magnitudes subtract, and the net force points toward the larger force.

Formula / Relationship

Choose a positive direction and add the signed forces: \(F_{net}=\sum F\). In one dimension, forces in the same direction reinforce each other; forces in opposite directions oppose each other.

For example, 5 N right plus 5 N right gives 10 N right. A 5 N force right and a 5 N force left cancel to 0 N. A 10 N force right opposed by 5 N left gives a net force of 5 N right. Zero net force does not mean no forces are present; it means the forces combine to zero.

Stop and Reflect

A box has 7 N pulling left and 7 N pulling right. What is the net force? What would be wrong with saying the net force is 14 N?

Acceptable reasoning: Net force is 0 N because equal opposite vectors cancel. Reporting 14 N ignores direction and treats both forces as if they act the same way.

Worked Example 2

Situation: A cart is pushed with 12 N to the right while friction pushes with 7 N to the left. Determine the net force.

  1. The forces are opposite, so subtract their magnitudes.
  2. \(12\text{ N}-7\text{ N}=5\text{ N}\).
  3. The larger force points right.
  4. Answer: \(F_{net}=5\text{ N}\) to the right.
You Try It 2

A sled has an 8 N pull to the right and a 3 N pull to the right while friction acts with 5 N to the left. Determine the net force and its direction.

Answer: \((8+3)-5=6\text{ N}\) to the right.

Teacher move: Have students combine the two rightward forces first, then compare that total with the leftward force.

Resultant Force in Two Dimensions

For forces that do not lie along the same line, simply adding or subtracting the magnitudes loses the directional information. Instead, the vectors must be added geometrically. The single vector that represents their combined effect is called the resultant.

One way to see the resultant is the parallelogram rule. Draw the two force vectors from the same starting point, complete the parallelogram, and draw the diagonal from the shared starting point. That diagonal points in the direction of the resultant. Its length represents the magnitude of the resultant on the same scale.

When the two component forces are perpendicular, the geometry is a right triangle. If an exact magnitude is needed, the Pythagorean relationship can be used: \(R=\sqrt{X^2+Y^2}\). More important for this section is what the picture tells you before any calculation: the resultant must point between the component vectors and, for nonzero perpendicular components, its arrow is longer than either single component arrow.

Stop and Reflect

Why would adding 30 N + 40 N and calling the result 70 N be incorrect for the perpendicular forces shown above? Use the direction of the two arrows in your explanation.

Acceptable reasoning: The forces are perpendicular, not in the same direction, so ordinary magnitude addition discards direction. The resultant is the diagonal vector; for 30 N and 40 N perpendicular components its magnitude is 50 N.

Worked Example 3

Situation: A 30 N force acts to the right while a 40 N force acts upward. Describe the resultant shown by the vector model.

  1. The resultant points between the two component directions: up and to the right.
  2. The diagonal is longer than either component arrow, so the resultant is larger than 30 N and larger than 40 N.
  3. For these perpendicular components, \(R=\sqrt{30^2+40^2}=50\text{ N}\).
  4. Answer: the resultant is 50 N directed up and to the right.
You Try It 3

Two equal force vectors act at right angles, one to the right and one upward. Where must the resultant point? How should its arrow length compare with either component arrow?

Answer: The resultant points diagonally up-right, halfway between the equal perpendicular components (45°). Its arrow is longer than either component; for equal components its magnitude is \(\sqrt{2}\) times one component.

Listen for: students saying the resultant is a third independent force rather than a vector representing the combined effect.

A force-vector diagram is therefore a compact model of several pieces of information at once. Arrow direction tells which way each force acts, arrow length compares magnitude, and the resultant summarizes the combined effect. Reading those features together prevents one of the most common mistakes in force problems: treating forces as numbers with no direction.
