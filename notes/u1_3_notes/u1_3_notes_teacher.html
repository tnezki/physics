Two students pull a low-friction sled. The force arrows are drawn to the same scale:

6 N ←──── [sled] ──────────→ 14 N

(a) Which force is larger, and what feature of the diagram shows that?
(b) Find the resultant force, including direction.
(c) A classmate says the two forces cancel because they point in opposite directions. Evaluate that claim.
(d) Without calculating acceleration, predict the direction in which the sled’s velocity will begin to change and explain which earlier result your prediction uses.
Canonical solution: (a) The 14 N rightward force is larger; its arrow is longer. (b) 8 N right. (c) Incorrect; opposite directions cancel only equal amounts, leaving 8 N right. (d) The velocity changes toward the right; this conclusion depends on the rightward resultant from part (b).
Because vector direction matters, subtract the opposite force magnitudes: 14 N − 6 N = 8 N to the right. The nonzero resultant points right, so the motion changes in that direction.

Net Force and Vectors

A force is a push or a pull. When several forces act on the same object, we combine them to find the net force. Forces acting in the same direction reinforce one another; equal forces in opposite directions cancel; unequal opposite forces leave a net force toward the larger one.

Force diagrams use arrows because force has both size and direction. When arrow lengths are drawn to a common scale, a longer arrow represents a greater magnitude. The diagram is not decoration—the direction and length carry physical information.

The three cases in the figure show the basic one-dimensional patterns: same-direction forces add, equal opposite forces produce zero net force, and unequal opposite forces produce a resultant in the direction of the larger force.

Stop & Discuss
  1. Which case has the largest resultant and why?
  2. When do opposite forces cancel completely?
  3. How does the arrow direction tell you the resultant direction?
Listen for: Students combine signed/directed forces rather than adding all magnitudes blindly.

Example 2

Two students push a crate to the right with 18 N and 27 N while friction pushes 15 N left. Use F_net = sum of rightward forces − sum of leftward forces to find the net force and direction.
Answer: 30 N right.
F_net = (18 + 27) − 15 = 30 N. The positive/rightward side is larger, so the net force is 30 N right.

You Try It 2

A sled is pulled 42 N left while another person pulls 17 N right. Find the net force and direction.
Answer: 25 N left.
Opposite forces subtract. 42 − 17 = 25 N, toward the larger leftward force.

Vector Quantities: Magnitude and Direction

A vector quantity needs both magnitude and direction for a complete description. Force is a vector. Saying “8 N” gives a magnitude, but not a complete force vector until a direction is included.

A scalar can be described by magnitude alone. Mass, volume, and speed are examples. For vectors, arrow direction shows direction and scaled arrow length shows relative magnitude. Changing either feature changes the represented vector.

Stop & Discuss
  1. What information is missing if only “8 N” is written?
  2. If two arrows use one scale, what must their lengths communicate?
  3. Can equal magnitudes in opposite directions be the same vector?
Listen for: Students name both magnitude and direction and treat arrow length as quantitative/relative evidence when scaled.

Example 1

Two diagrams both label a force ‘8 N.’ Diagram A shows a long arrow right; Diagram B shows a short arrow left. A student says they represent the same vector because both say 8 N. What is wrong with the representation/reasoning?
Answer: A force vector requires both magnitude and direction, and a scaled arrow should use length consistently; opposite directions are different vectors and inconsistent lengths cannot both represent the same 8 N on one scale.
Force is a vector. Arrow direction carries direction information and arrow length carries relative magnitude when the diagram is scaled.

You Try It 1

A student draws a 5 N eastward force and a 5 N westward force using arrows that both point east. Critique the drawing.
Answer: One arrow must point west; equal magnitudes do not erase the direction requirement.
The magnitude 5 N is only part of the vector. The arrows must point in the actual force directions.

Simple Two-Dimensional Resultants

When two vectors are not along the same line, they cannot be combined by ordinary one-dimensional addition or subtraction. A geometric method is the parallelogram rule: draw the two vectors as adjacent sides of a parallelogram, then use the diagonal from their common starting point as the resultant.

For perpendicular vectors, the parallelogram is a rectangle. The diagonal shows both the direction and relative magnitude of the combined effect. The key idea is the same as in one dimension: the resultant must preserve the directional information carried by the original vectors.

Stop & Discuss
  1. Why can’t perpendicular vectors be handled by ordinary add/subtract along one line?
  2. Where is the resultant drawn in the rectangle/parallelogram?
  3. How do direction and relative length survive the construction?
Listen for: Students identify the diagonal as the resultant and preserve both magnitude and direction meaning.

Example 3

Scaled vector diagram: 4 N ← [cart] ─────────→ 11 N. Read the diagram and determine the resultant force, including direction.
Answer: 7 N right.
The arrows are opposite, so subtract their magnitudes: 11 − 4 = 7 N. The longer/larger arrow points right.

You Try It 3

Scaled vector diagram: 13 N ←──────── [sled] ───→ 5 N. Determine the resultant force and direction.
Answer: 8 N left.
13 − 5 = 8 N, in the direction of the larger leftward vector.
Math NoteOne-Dimensional Force Sums

Choose a positive direction for bookkeeping, combine forces with their directions, and then report the physical result as a magnitude plus direction. A negative sign tells you the resultant points opposite your chosen positive direction; the sign is not the final physical answer.

Example 4

A tug-of-war team pulls 70 N right. What leftward force is needed for the resultant to be 15 N right? Use F_net = F_right − F_left.
Answer: 55 N left.
15 = 70 − F_left, so F_left = 55 N.

You Try It 4

A cart is pushed 48 N left. What rightward force would make the net force 12 N left?
Answer: 36 N right.
For 12 N left, the leftward side must exceed the rightward side by 12 N: 48 − F_right = 12, so F_right = 36 N.

Vocabulary

vectorA quantity represented by both magnitude and direction.
magnitudeThe size or amount of a quantity.
directionThe orientation in which a vector points or an effect acts.
resultantThe single vector that has the same combined effect as two or more vectors.
net forceThe resultant vector obtained by adding all forces acting on an object.

Summary

  • A force is a vector because it has magnitude and direction.
  • Same-direction forces add; opposite-direction forces subtract by direction.
  • The resultant/net force keeps direction information.
  • Scaled arrow length is part of the representation, not decoration.
  • Simple 2D resultants can be represented geometrically with a parallelogram/rectangle.

Common Mistakes

Common mistakeBetter physics thinking
Adding vector magnitudes without directionKeep direction attached to each force before combining them.
