Velocity Vectors and Motion Modeling

Section 2.5

A strong motion model tells the same story in words, diagrams, vectors, tables, and graphs.

Designed for about 20–30 minutes of reading, reflection, and practice.

Learning Target

Students will use velocity vectors, motion diagrams, and simple graphs to model and explain the motion of objects.

  • I can represent velocity with arrows that show both speed and direction.
  • I can compare velocity vectors to determine whether an object’s velocity is changing.
  • I can connect motion diagrams, data tables, and graphs to written descriptions of motion and critique an incorrect motion model and revise it using evidence.

A useful motion model must preserve the quantities that matter. For velocity, two pieces of information matter at the same time: magnitude (speed) and direction. A velocity vector represents both with an arrow. The arrow points in the motion direction, and its length represents the velocity magnitude when a scale is being used.

A velocity vector is not just an arrow showing direction. Its direction and relative length together communicate the velocity.

If two vectors point the same direction but have different lengths, the object has different speeds. If two vectors have the same length but point different directions, the speed can be the same while velocity changes. Both features must match for two velocity vectors to represent the same velocity.

Stop and Reflect

Two velocity arrows have equal length, but one points east and the other north. What stayed the same, what changed, and what does that mean for velocity?

Worked Example 1

Situation: Construct a velocity vector for an object moving left at 5 m/s.

  1. The arrow must point left because direction is left.
  2. The arrow length must represent 5 m/s on the chosen scale.
  3. Both direction and magnitude are required for a complete velocity vector.
You Try It 1

Construct a velocity vector for an object moving downward at 6 m/s. Explain what the arrow direction and length represent.

Comparing Velocity Vectors

Comparing vectors is one way to decide whether velocity changes. Look first at length, then at direction. A length change means the speed changed. A direction change means the direction of motion changed. Either one is enough to make the velocity different.

This connects directly to acceleration. If successive velocity vectors differ, the object’s velocity is changing, so the motion includes acceleration. The vectors do not have to get longer; turning at constant speed also changes velocity.

Worked Example 2

Situation: Vector A is 4 cm long and points east. Vector B is 4 cm long and points north. Did velocity change?

  1. The vector lengths are equal, so the represented speed can be the same.
  2. The direction changes from east to north.
  3. Because direction is part of velocity, the velocity changed.
You Try It 2

Vector A is 3 cm long and points south. Vector B is 5 cm long and points south. Compare the two velocities and identify what changed.

Motion Diagrams Add Time Structure

A motion diagram shows positions at equal time intervals. Dot spacing tells how much distance is covered in each interval. Equal spacing represents constant speed. Increasing spacing shows speeding up. Decreasing spacing shows slowing down. When direction changes, the sequence of positions bends or reverses.

Velocity vectors can be added to the motion diagram to show both speed and direction at each moment. This creates a richer model: the dots show where the object is, while the arrows show how it is moving.

Stop and Reflect

For a motion diagram with dots that get farther apart to the right, describe the velocity vectors you would draw at successive dots.

Graphs Are Representations, Not Pictures of the Path

A common graph mistake is to treat the graph line as a picture of the object’s path. On a position–time graph, the vertical axis is position and the horizontal axis is time. A rising straight line means position increases at a constant rate; it does not mean the object is climbing a hill. A steeper straight line means a larger constant velocity in the positive direction.

On a velocity–time graph, the vertical value is velocity. A horizontal line above zero means constant positive velocity. A line that slopes upward means velocity is increasing; its slope represents acceleration.

Stop and Reflect

Why is the statement “the object goes uphill because the graph slopes upward” usually a bad interpretation of a position–time graph?

Translating Between Representations

Strong motion reasoning connects several representations without changing the physical story. A written description, a motion diagram, a data table, a graph, and a velocity-vector sketch should agree. If the written description says the object moves right at constant velocity, then equal-time dots should be equally spaced to the right, velocity arrows should be equal and rightward, and a position–time graph should have a constant positive slope.

Translation is not copying surface features. It means preserving the same physical relationships. When two representations disagree, that mismatch is evidence that at least one part of the model needs revision.
