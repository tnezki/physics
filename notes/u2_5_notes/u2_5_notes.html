Velocity Vectors and Motion Modeling

Section 2.5

A good motion model tells the same story in arrows, dots, data, graphs, and words.

Learning Target

Students will use velocity vectors, motion diagrams, and simple graphs to model and explain the motion of objects.

  • I can represent velocity with arrows that show both speed and direction.
  • I can compare velocity vectors to determine whether an object’s velocity is changing.
  • I can connect motion diagrams, data tables, and graphs to written descriptions of motion and critique an incorrect motion model and revise it using evidence.

A velocity vector is an arrow that stores two pieces of information at once. The arrow direction shows the direction of motion, and its length represents speed. Two velocity vectors are the same only when both their lengths and directions match.

Motion models become more useful when several representations agree. A written description, motion diagram, data table, graph, and set of velocity vectors should all tell a consistent story about where an object is, how fast it moves, and whether its velocity is changing.

When comparing velocity vectors, check both arrow length and arrow direction. A change in either one means velocity changed.
Stop and Reflect

Two velocity arrows are the same length. One points east and the other north. Has the velocity changed? What evidence supports your answer?

Representation Connection

For a position–time graph, slope represents velocity. For a velocity vector, arrow length represents speed and arrow direction represents motion direction.

Comparing Velocity Vectors

If two vectors have the same direction but different lengths, speed changed. If they have the same length but different directions, direction changed. If both length and direction change, both parts of velocity changed. Any of these cases means the object accelerated over the interval.

Worked Example 1

Situation: Construct a velocity vector for an object moving left at 5 m/s. Use arrow direction for motion direction and arrow length to represent speed.

  1. Point the arrow left to show direction.
  2. Choose an arrow length that represents 5 m/s on the chosen scale.
  3. Both direction and length are required for a complete velocity vector.
You Try It 1

Construct a velocity vector for an object moving down at 6 m/s. Use arrow direction for motion direction and arrow length to represent speed.

Connecting Representations

A motion diagram uses positions at equal time intervals. Larger spacing between successive positions indicates greater speed. A position–time graph uses vertical height to represent position, not how high the object is in the real world. The slope of a position–time graph describes velocity.

Worked Example 2

Situation: Vector A is 4 cm long and points east. Vector B is 4 cm long and points north. Determine whether the object’s velocity changed and explain what feature of the vectors supports your answer.

  1. The arrows have equal length, so the speed magnitude can be the same.
  2. The direction changes from east to north.
  3. Because direction is part of velocity, the velocity changed.
You Try It 2

Vector A is 5 cm long and points south. Vector B is 5 cm long and points west. Determine whether the velocity changed and explain what feature supports your answer.

Critiquing a Motion Model

A common mistake is reading a graph as a picture of the path. A line that slopes upward on a position–time graph does not mean the object climbed a hill; it means position increased as time passed. A strong critique names the incorrect interpretation and replaces it with one supported by the axes, slope, vectors, or data.

  1. The line is straight with a positive slope, so the object moves in the positive direction at constant velocity.
  2. Graph height represents position, not physical height above the ground.
  3. The graph should not be read as a picture of a hill.
