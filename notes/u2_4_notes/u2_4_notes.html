Free Fall Motion

Section 2.4

Gravity changes velocity in a predictable way when air resistance can be ignored.

Designed for about 20–30 minutes of reading, reflection, and practice.

Learning Target

Students will describe free fall as motion under the influence of gravity alone and use simple free-fall relationships to determine speed or distance fallen.

  • I can explain why objects in free fall accelerate at the same rate when air resistance is ignored.
  • I can use \(g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2\) to determine speed gained during free fall.
  • I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.

Free fall is motion in which gravity is the only significant force acting on an object. Near Earth’s surface, an object in ideal free fall has nearly constant downward acceleration. In this course we use \(g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2\) as a useful model unless a problem states otherwise.

The word “free” does not mean the object is moving upward or downward at a particular speed. It means the motion is being modeled with gravity alone. An object dropped from rest, an object thrown downward, and an object thrown upward can all be in free fall after release if air resistance is ignored.

In ideal free fall near Earth, all objects have the same downward acceleration \(g\), independent of their mass.

Why Mass Does Not Change Ideal Free-Fall Acceleration

It is tempting to think a heavier object must fall faster because gravity pulls more strongly on it. The missing idea is that the heavier object also has more inertia. In the ideal model, the increased gravitational force and increased resistance to acceleration scale together, so different masses share the same gravitational acceleration.

Real falling objects can behave differently because air resistance is not always negligible. A flat sheet of paper and a compact ball may fall differently in air, but that difference is evidence about air resistance, not evidence that gravity gives heavier objects a fundamentally larger free-fall acceleration.

Worked Example 1

Situation: Two compact objects of different masses are released together in a vacuum chamber near Earth. Predict what happens.

  1. Air resistance is absent, so both objects are in ideal free fall.
  2. Both have the same downward acceleration \(g\).
  3. If released from the same height with the same initial velocity, they reach the floor together.

Speed Gained During Free Fall

An acceleration of about \(10\text{ m/s}^2\) means the velocity changes by about 10 m/s every second. For an object dropped from rest, the speed after 1 s is about 10 m/s downward, after 2 s about 20 m/s downward, after 3 s about 30 m/s downward, and so on while the ideal model remains reasonable.

Worked Example 2

Situation: An object is dropped from rest. Using \(g\approx10\text{{ m/s}}^2\), determine the speed gained after 3 s.

  1. Use the constant acceleration model: speed gained = \(gt\).
  2. \((10\text{{ m/s}}^2)(3\text{{ s}})=30\text{{ m/s}}\).
  3. The velocity change is downward, so the object has gained 30 m/s of downward speed.
You Try It 2

Distance Does Not Increase Linearly with Time

Because a falling object speeds up, it covers more distance during each new second than during the previous second. For an object dropped from rest under constant gravitational acceleration, the total distance is approximated by \(d=\tfrac12gt^2\). With \(g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2\), this becomes \(d\approx5t^2\).

Notice the square on time. If time doubles, total distance becomes four times as large, not twice as large. This is a direct consequence of the object continuously gaining speed.

Ideal Free Fall from Rest

Speed gained: \(v\approx gt\).    Distance fallen: \(d\approx\tfrac12gt^2\). These are model relationships for constant downward acceleration with air resistance ignored.

Stop and Reflect

Compare the total distance fallen after 1 s and after 2 s. Why is the second distance not simply double the first?

Worked Example 3

Situation: Compare the total distance fallen after 3 s and 4 s using \(d=\tfrac12gt^2\) and \(g\approx10\text{{ m/s}}^2\).

  1. After 3 s: \(d=5(3^2)=45\text{{ m}}\).
  2. After 4 s: \(d=5(4^2)=80\text{{ m}}\).
  3. The distance grows with \(t^2\), so equal additional seconds do not add equal distances.

When Air Resistance Matters

Air resistance grows in importance when an object presents a large area to the air or reaches high speed. In that situation the object is not in ideal free fall because gravity is no longer the only significant force. The acceleration can become smaller than \(g\), and if air resistance eventually balances weight, the object can approach a nearly constant terminal speed.

This is why the ideal free-fall model must be chosen deliberately. It is powerful because it is simple, but it has conditions. Physics models are useful when their assumptions match the situation.
