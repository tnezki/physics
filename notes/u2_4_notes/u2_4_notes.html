Conceptual Physics • Unit 2

Free Fall Motion

Section 2.4

In ideal free fall, gravity changes velocity by the same amount each second.

Learning Target

Students will describe free fall as motion under the influence of gravity alone and use simple free-fall relationships to determine speed or distance fallen.

  • I can explain why objects in free fall accelerate at the same rate when air resistance is ignored.
  • I can use \(g \approx 10\text{ m/s}^2\) to determine speed gained during free fall.
  • I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.

An object is in free fall when gravity is the only significant force affecting its motion. Near Earth’s surface, objects in ideal free fall have approximately the same downward acceleration even when their masses are different.

For this course, the convenient model \(g \approx 10\text{ m/s}^2\) means that a freely falling object’s downward velocity changes by about 10 m/s during each second. This model assumes air resistance is small enough to ignore.

Ignoring air resistance, all objects near Earth share the same free-fall acceleration. Mass does not make one compact object accelerate faster than another.
Stop and Reflect

Two compact objects of different masses are dropped together in a vacuum. Which one reaches the floor first? Explain without using “heavier falls faster.”

Free-Fall Relationships

\[\Delta v \approx gt \qquad d \approx \tfrac12gt^2\]

Speed Gained in Free Fall

If an object is dropped from rest and air resistance is ignored, the speed gained after time t is approximately \(v=gt\). With \(g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2\), the object gains about 10 m/s of downward speed each second.

Worked Example 1

Situation: Two compact objects of different masses are released together in a vacuum chamber near Earth. Predict which reaches the floor first and explain using the meaning of free fall.

  1. In a vacuum, air resistance is absent.
  2. Gravity is the only significant force, so both objects have the same downward acceleration g.
  3. If released from the same height with the same initial velocity, they reach the floor together.
You Try It 1

Two compact objects of different masses are dropped together from the same height in a vacuum. Predict which has the greater acceleration and explain.

Distance Does Not Increase by Equal Amounts

Because a freely falling object speeds up, it covers more distance during each new second than during the previous second. For an object dropped from rest, a useful model is \(d=\tfrac12gt^2\). With \(g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2\), this becomes \(d\approx5t^2\).

Worked Example 2

Situation: Ignoring air resistance, an object is dropped from rest. Using g ≈ 10 m/s², determine the speed it has gained after 3 s.

  1. Use speed gained = gt.
  2. (10 m/s²)(3 s) = 30 m/s.
  3. Answer: 30 m/s downward.
You Try It 2

Ignoring air resistance, an object is dropped from rest. Using g ≈ 10 m/s², determine the speed it has gained after 5 s.

When the Ideal Model Stops Working

A coffee filter or parachute can experience large air resistance. When air resistance is significant, gravity is not the only important force, so the object is not in ideal free fall. The acceleration can decrease and the motion can approach nearly constant terminal speed.

Worked Example 3

Situation: For ideal free fall from rest, use d = ½gt² with g ≈ 10 m/s². Compare the total distance fallen after 3 s and 4 s. Then explain why a coffee filter falling at terminal speed would not follow this same pattern.

  1. After 3 s: d = 5(3²) = 45 m.
  2. After 4 s: d = 5(4²) = 80 m.
  3. Distance grows with t² in ideal free fall.
  4. A coffee filter with significant air resistance is not in ideal free fall and can approach nearly constant speed.
You Try It 3

For ideal free fall from rest, compare the distance fallen after 2 s and 3 s using d = ½gt² and g ≈ 10 m/s². Then explain why a coffee filter at terminal speed would not follow the same pattern.
