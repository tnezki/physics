Reading 1: Charge, Atoms, and Electrical Forces

Matter contains particles with electric charge. Protons carry positive charge, electrons carry negative charge, and a neutral object has equal amounts of positive and negative charge. A neutral object is not “uncharged matter”; it contains enormous amounts of both kinds of charge that balance.

The basic electric force has two possible directions. Like charges repel, while opposite charges attract. This differs from gravity, which only attracts. In ordinary matter, positive and negative charges are usually mixed so evenly that large electrical attractions and repulsions cancel at the scale we normally notice.

The atomic model helps explain why electrons matter so much in charging. Protons are locked inside atomic nuclei, while electrons occupy the region around the nucleus. In solids, charging usually involves electrons moving from one object to another; the protons stay with their nuclei.

When a material gains or loses only a tiny fraction of its electrons, the imbalance can still produce noticeable forces. The diagrams below connect the rule about attraction and repulsion to the particles inside matter.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Use the charge-interaction figure as evidence: what pattern determines attraction versus repulsion?
  2. How can an object be neutral even though every atom in it contains charged particles?

Teacher answer: Like charges repel; opposite charges attract. Neutral means equal positive and negative charge, not an absence of charge.

Example 1: Charge Interaction Sort

Two small objects are known to have net charges: object A is positive and object B is negative.

  1. Predict whether A and B attract or repel.
  2. If B were changed to positive, predict the new interaction and explain the rule you used.

Teacher answer: A and B attract. If both are positive, they repel. Listen for students using the sign rule rather than “charged things always attract.”

You Try It 1: Neutral Does Not Mean Empty

A metal sphere is neutral before an experiment begins.

  1. Explain what “neutral” says about its positive and negative charges.
  2. Would removing some electrons make the sphere positive or negative? Explain.

Teacher answer: Neutral means balanced charge. Removing electrons leaves a positive net charge.

Reading 2: Electron Transfer and Conservation of Charge

Charging does not require making new charge. The conservation of charge principle says that charge can move from place to place, but the total charge of an isolated system remains the same. If one object gains electrons, another part of the system must lose the same number of electrons.

The textbook example shows a rod rubbed with fur. Electrons move from the fur to the rod. The rod becomes negatively charged because it has an excess of electrons. The fur becomes equally positive because it has lost those electrons.

A useful accounting habit is to track electrons before and after an interaction. Do not imagine positive charge jumping onto an object when electrons leave. The object becomes positive because it now has fewer electrons than protons.

Charge is also quantized: ordinary charged objects gain or lose whole electrons. For this course, the main consequence is simple—charging changes the balance of electrons and protons; it does not create or destroy the underlying charge.

Image read: Draw an arrow showing the electron-transfer direction. Label the final sign of the fur and of the rod.

Stop and Discuss

  1. In the fur-and-rod image, where did the rod’s extra electrons come from?
  2. Explain why conservation of charge requires the fur and rod to end with opposite net charges.

Teacher answer: Electrons moved from the fur to the rod. The rod’s gain equals the fur’s loss, so total charge is conserved.

Example 2: Track the Electrons

A plastic strip and a cloth start neutral. After rubbing, 8 electrons have moved from the cloth to the strip.

  1. State the final sign of each object.
  2. Explain how the total charge of the two-object system can still be conserved.

Teacher answer: The strip is negative and the cloth positive. No charge was created; electrons were transferred.

You Try It 2: Charge Accounting

Two neutral materials are rubbed together. Object X loses electrons to object Y.

  1. State the sign of X and Y after the transfer.
  2. Write one sentence correcting the claim “positive charge was created on X.”

Teacher answer: X is positive and Y negative. Correct idea: X became positive because it lost electrons.

Reading 3: What Controls Electric Force—and What Lets Charge Move?

The amount of electrical interaction depends on both charge amount and separation. Coulomb’s law summarizes this pattern: larger charge amounts produce a larger force, while increasing distance weakens the force very quickly because distance appears squared in the denominator.

The force acts along the line joining the charged objects. The equation gives the size relationship; the signs of the charges tell whether the interaction is attractive or repulsive. In this unit, the most important skill is often proportional reasoning before plugging in numbers.

Whether transferred electrons stay near one spot or spread out depends on the material. A conductor contains charge carriers that can move readily; metals such as copper and aluminum are common examples. An insulator, such as glass, rubber, or many plastics, holds electrons much more tightly to particular atoms.

Wire resistance will matter later, but this conductor–insulator distinction begins here: conductors allow charge redistribution, while insulators strongly restrict it. That difference is why induction works especially clearly with metal objects and why plastic coatings can keep charge from easily reaching your hand.

Stop and Discuss

  1. If the distance between two charges increases from r to 2r, what feature of Coulomb’s law tells you the force becomes much smaller?
  2. Why can charge spread across a metal sphere more easily than across a rubber object?

Teacher answer: Distance is squared in the denominator; doubling r makes force one-fourth. Metals redistribute charge because mobile electrons can move.

Math NotesCoulomb’s Law — Charge Amount and Distance
$$F=k\frac{q_1q_2}{r^2}$$

$F$ is electric-force magnitude.

$q_1$ and $q_2$ are the charge amounts.

$r$ is separation distance.

$k$ is the proportionality constant.

Read the relationship first: multiplying one charge by 2 multiplies the force by 2, while doubling distance makes the force one-fourth as large.

Example 3: Distance Matters Twice

Two charged objects exert force F on each other at separation r. The charges stay the same, but the separation becomes 2r.

  1. Use the squared distance in Coulomb’s law to compare the new force with F.
  2. Would the attraction/repulsion type change just because distance changed? Explain.

Teacher answer: New force is F/4. Attraction or repulsion does not change unless charge signs change.

You Try It 3: Charge Amount Matters

Two charged objects are held at the same separation. The amount of charge on one object is tripled while everything else stays the same.

  1. Compare the new force with the original force.
  2. Name one material choice that would help charge spread across an object and explain why.

Teacher answer: Tripling one charge triples force at fixed r. A metal such as copper is a conductor, so charge can spread.

Reading 4: Charging by Friction, Contact, and Induction

When different materials rub together, electrons can transfer from one surface to the other. This is charging by friction. The rubbing does not manufacture electrons; it creates conditions that favor electron transfer between the materials.

Electrons can also move through direct touching. In charging by contact, a charged object touches another object and some charge transfers. On a conductor, the added electrons then spread across the surface because they repel one another.

A third process can happen without touching the charged object. Charging by induction begins when a nearby charge causes mobile electrons in a conductor to redistribute. If parts of the conductor are separated, or if a path to Earth is provided at the right time, the object can be left with a net charge even though the inducing object never touched it.

Grounding means connecting an object to Earth through a conducting path. Earth is so large that it can accept or supply electrons without a noticeable change in its own potential. The induction diagrams show how grounding and separation can “freeze in” a charge imbalance.

The same charge-redistribution idea appears on a much larger scale in storms. Charge in a cloud can induce opposite charge on the ground below. The textbook uses lightning as a dramatic example of electric forces and charge movement acting across large separations.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What is the key difference between charging by contact and charging by induction?
  2. Use one induction figure to explain how an object can end charged even though the charged rod never touches it.
  3. How does grounding change what electrons are able to do?

Teacher answer: Contact requires touching and charge transfer; induction uses redistribution without touching the charged object. Grounding provides a path to Earth.

Example 4: Name the Charging Process

A negatively charged rod touches a neutral metal sphere. Some electrons move onto the sphere.

  1. Identify the charging process.
  2. Predict the sphere’s final sign and explain the electron movement.

Teacher answer: Charging by contact; electrons move onto the sphere, leaving it negatively charged.

You Try It 4: Induction Without Touching

A negatively charged rod is brought near two touching neutral metal spheres. The spheres are separated while the rod is still nearby.

  1. Identify the charging process.
  2. Explain why the two spheres can end with opposite charges even though the rod never touched them.

Teacher answer: Charging by induction. The rod repels mobile electrons across the touching conductors; separating the spheres preserves opposite imbalances.

Summary

  • Positive and negative charges can attract or repel; neutral matter contains balanced positive and negative charge.
  • Charging normally transfers electrons; conservation of charge means total charge is not created or destroyed.
  • Conductors allow charge to move readily, while insulators strongly restrict charge motion.
  • Coulomb’s law shows that force grows with charge amount and falls with the square of separation distance.
  • Friction, contact, and induction are different ways to produce charge imbalance; induction can occur without touching the charged object.
