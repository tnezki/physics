Combining forces

The net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on an object. Forces pointing in the same direction reinforce one another. Forces pointing in opposite directions compete, so direction-aware subtraction determines the resultant. A zero net force does not require every individual force to be zero.

Example

A crate is pulled 11 N right and 4 N left. Determine the net force, including direction.

You Try It

A wagon is pulled 6 N left and 14 N right. Determine the net force.

Example

An object already has an 8 N force to the right. What additional horizontal force would produce a resultant of 3 N left?

You Try It

An object has a 5 N force left. What additional horizontal force would produce a resultant of 7 N right?

Force is a vector

A complete force description needs both magnitude and direction. A scaled arrow can show both at once: the arrow points in the force direction, and its length represents the force magnitude. Two arrows can have the same magnitude but opposite directions, or the same direction but different magnitudes.

Example

Two force arrows point right, but one is twice as long as the other. What two pieces of information make each arrow a vector, and what does the length difference mean?

You Try It

Two force arrows have equal lengths, one pointing left and one pointing right. Compare their magnitudes and directions.

Example

A scaled diagram shows a 12 N force right and a 5 N force left on the same object. What is the resultant magnitude and direction?

You Try It

A scaled diagram shows a 9 N force left and a 3 N force right. What is the resultant magnitude and direction?

Simple two-dimensional resultants

For two forces that are not along the same line, the resultant can be represented geometrically. Place the force vectors as sides of a parallelogram; the diagonal from their shared starting point shows the direction and relative size of the resultant. For this lesson, the goal is to read the geometry of the vectors, not to turn the idea into a trigonometry procedure.

Resultants summarize the combined effect

A two-dimensional resultant replaces several component vectors with one vector that has the same combined effect on the object. The resultant must point in a direction consistent with the component arrows and have a magnitude consistent with their scale.

Vocabulary

vectora quantity represented with both magnitude and direction
magnitudethe size or amount of a quantity
directionthe orientation in which a vector points
resultantthe single vector equal to the vector sum of two or more vectors
net forcethe resultant of all forces acting on an object

Summary

  • A force is a vector because a complete description requires magnitude and direction.
  • Forces in the same direction add; opposite-direction forces combine by direction-aware subtraction.
  • The net force/resultant describes the combined effect of all forces on the object.
  • Scaled arrow length and arrow direction carry physical meaning; a simple 2-D resultant can be represented as a diagonal.
