Physics | Unit 2 | Section 2 | Teacher guide

Same Speed, Different Motion

18-minute whole-class demonstration | teacher is the mover | every student times and calculates

Duration18 minutes total
Class rolesTeacher moves; every student times and calculates
Equipment2 tape marks 6 m apart; individual timers; board; direction arrow

Learning targets addressed

  • I can calculate average speed from distance and time.
  • I can distinguish instantaneous speed from average speed.
  • I can explain that velocity includes both speed and direction and compare examples of constant speed, constant velocity, and changing velocity.

Before class: simple setup

  1. Put tape or cones at A and B, 6 m apart in a safe, clear walking lane. Remove loose objects from the path.
  2. Ask every student to have a stopwatch (phone, Chromebook timer, or another available timer) and paper / Notability. No student walker or student timer is required.
  3. Have a paper arrow ready. Hold it in the direction you are traveling; turn it when you turn.
  4. On the board, leave space for the formula and quick comparison of times. Students will each use their own readings, not one official class time.
  5. Project the demo slide deck. Use Next/Back or arrow keys to advance.
Timing agreement: Everyone starts their stopwatch when the teacher first moves and stops when the teacher reaches the destination. For walk 3, stop only when the teacher is back at A. Reaction-time differences are normal; small disagreement in answers is useful measurement evidence.

18-minute demonstration flow

WhenSlideTeacher does / students do
0-2 min1-2: Setup & tableShow A and B are 6 m apart. Students prepare stopwatches and copy a 3-row table: walk, distance, time, average speed. Establish when to start/stop timing. Ask the big prediction.
2-5 min3: Walk 1Walk steadily A to B. All students time, record their own elapsed time, and calculate 6 m / time. Ask whether an average describes every instant. Note you had to start and stop, so strictly constant velocity applies only to the steady middle portion.
5-9 min4: Walk 2Cover 6 m again: about 2 m slow, 2 m quick, pause for about 2 s, then finish the final 2 m quickly. Try to make total elapsed time similar to walk 1, but a precise match is not required. Everyone times and computes 6 m / time. Compare the two averages; ask about the pause.
9-13 min5: Walk 3Walk A to B and return directly to A, holding the arrow toward the direction of travel. All students time the whole trip. Use 12 m / time for average speed. Ask about returning to the same position and reversing direction.
13-15 min6: Walk 4Walk a smooth curve at an approximately steady pace, continuously aiming the arrow forward (tangent to your path). No timing/calculation here because the curve's distance has not been measured. Observe the changing direction.
15-18 min7: DiscussionStudents think briefly, compare with a neighbor, then share evidence for the three questions. Close with speed: how fast; velocity: how fast and in which direction.

Teacher questions and expected ideas

Walk 1: steady straight trip

Ask: What do distance and time allow us to calculate? Expected: Average speed = total distance divided by elapsed time. If a student records 4.0 s, the result is 6 m / 4.0 s = 1.5 m/s. Use actual student times, not this example as a scripted answer.

Ask: Does that prove the instantaneous speed was 1.5 m/s at every instant? Expected: No. Average speed describes the full trip. You accelerated at the start and slowed to a stop at the end; the steady middle can have approximately constant speed and velocity.

Walk 2: varied pace, possibly similar average

Ask: Could the two average speeds be close, even though this trip included speeding up, slowing down, and stopping? Expected: Yes. Average speed summarizes total distance and total time, not what happened every second. At the 2-second pause your instantaneous speed was 0 m/s. A car's speedometer is an example of a readout of (approximately) instantaneous speed.

If the times differ appreciably: Keep both real measurements. Students can still show that a whole-trip average does not reveal the details of the motion. Do not claim the two trips had exactly equal speed unless the observed data support it.

Walk 3: 6 m out and 6 m back

Ask: Did you move? What were distance and change in position? Expected: The distance was 12 m, so average speed = 12 m / elapsed time, which is positive. The final position equals the initial position, so net displacement = 0 m and average velocity over the whole round trip = 0 m/s. Instantaneous velocity was not zero throughout; its direction reversed on the return leg.

Walk 4: steady speed around a curve

Ask: Does the arrow turn even when the pace remains roughly steady? Expected: Yes. Speed can be approximately constant while velocity changes because velocity has both a magnitude (speed) and a direction. If the pace is steady and the line of travel straight, velocity is approximately constant; if direction changes, velocity changes.

Follow-up discussion: choose student reasoning, not a vote

  1. Compare walks 1 and 2: How can the average speeds be similar when instantaneous speeds were different? Look for total distance / elapsed time versus moment-to-moment changes; pause = 0 m/s.
  2. Explain walk 3: Why is the average speed not zero when the teacher returns to A? What changed in velocity? Look for 12 m traveled, zero net displacement, reversal of direction; average velocity over trip zero.
  3. Explain walk 4: Can speed remain constant while velocity changes? Look for curved path and constantly changing direction, demonstrated by the arrow.
Important physics language: Walk 1 is not literally constant velocity from the moment you start until the moment you stop. Walk 3 does not have zero velocity throughout just because average velocity is zero. The curved walk is an approximation of constant speed, not a precision measurement.

Board summary / transition to notes

Average speed = total distance / total elapsed time. Instantaneous speed = speed at a particular instant. Velocity = speed with direction. A change in speed or direction changes velocity.

Use the opening question to transition: When does describing how fast someone moves leave out important information?
