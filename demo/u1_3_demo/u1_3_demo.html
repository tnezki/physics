You need a whiteboard and marker. No force numbers today — use arrow direction and relative arrow length.

How we will model the ball
Every force arrow starts at the ball.

Read each arrow two ways.

  • Direction = which way the force acts.
  • Length = relative force strength.
  • The resultant is one arrow for the combined effect.
Whiteboard setupCopy the point and force arrows. Add a different arrow for your predicted resultant.
Round 1 · Opposite & balanced
Equal length. Opposite directions.

Predict the resultant.

On your whiteboardCopy the force diagram. What resultant vector belongs at the point? Draw it — or show clearly if the resultant is zero.

Do not run the demo yet.

Round 1 · Test it

Two students. Two straws. Opposite directions.

Run the trial
Place the ball at center. Blow at the same time and try to make the two pushes as equal as possible.
After drawing
On the same whiteboard, add a dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. If it drifted, what does that suggest about the real forces?
Resultant ≈ 0
If the opposite force arrows are truly equal, they cancel. Human blowing may not be perfectly balanced.
Round 2 · 90° apart
The forces are perpendicular.

Where should the resultant point?

Before the demoCopy the two force arrows. Draw the resultant from the point. Use the arrow length to show how its size compares with the two force arrows.

No numbers. Your diagram should communicate the prediction.

Round 2 · Test & revise

Run the 90° trial.

Demo
Keep the same two students. Move one straw so the two blowing directions are about 90° apart. Blow together.
After drawing
Add a dashed motion arrow showing what the ball actually did. Then revise your resultant arrow if the observation gives you a better model.
Did the ball move in the direction of either single force — or somewhere between them?
Round 3 · A different angle
Different angle. Different arrow lengths.

Predict before anyone blows.

On your whiteboardDraw the resultant. Which force should the resultant lean toward? How should its length compare with the force arrows?

For the demo, one student should blow noticeably harder than the other.

Round 3 · Test & revise

Run the angled, unequal-force trial.

Demo
Set the straws at a non-90° angle. One blower uses a stronger steady breath; the other uses a gentler breath.
After drawing
Add the dashed motion arrow. Compare it to your predicted resultant. Revise the resultant arrow if needed.
What changed when one force arrow became longer?
Round 4 · Three straws
Three forces can still be replaced by one resultant.

Final prediction.

Before the demoCopy all three force arrows. Draw one resultant arrow for their combined effect. No calculation — reason from direction and relative length.

Add a third student and straw. Match the relative strengths approximately: strongest rightward, medium upper-left, gentler lower-left.

Round 4 · Test & revise

Three students blow at the same time.

Run it
Start the ball at the point. Count down so all three students blow together.
After drawing
Add the dashed motion arrow. Compare the observed direction with the single resultant arrow you predicted.
Even with three forces, the goal is still the same: replace the combined effect with one resultant vector.
What should your diagrams show?

Three patterns to carry into the notes

Opposite forces

Equal opposite arrows can give a zero resultant. If one is longer, the resultant points toward the longer arrow.

Forces at an angle

The resultant usually points between the force directions and leans toward the stronger force.

Any number of forces

One resultant vector can represent their combined effect.
