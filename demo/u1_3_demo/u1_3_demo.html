The dot is the ping-pong ball. Arrow direction shows force direction. Arrow length shows relative force strength. No numbers are needed.

Round 1 · Opposite & balanced

Predict the resultant.

On your whiteboardCopy the force diagram. Add the resultant vector at the point — or show clearly if the resultant is zero.

Do not run the demo yet.

Round 1 · Test it

Two students. Two straws. Opposite directions.

Run the trial
Place the ball at center. Blow at the same time and try to make the two pushes as equal as possible.
After drawing
On the same whiteboard, add a dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. If it drifted, what does that suggest about the real forces?
Round 2 · Right angle

Predict the resultant.

On your whiteboardCopy the force diagram. Add the resultant vector. Use its direction and length to show your prediction.
Round 2 · Test it

Two students. Two straws. About 90° apart.

Run the trial
Move one student so the two blowing directions are about a right angle. Try to use similar steady pushes.
After drawing
Add a dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. Compare it with your predicted resultant.
Round 3 · Right angle, unequal forces

Predict the resultant.

On your whiteboardCopy the force diagram. Add the resultant vector. Pay attention to which force arrow is longer.
Round 3 · Test it

Same right angle. One stronger push.

Run the trial
Keep the students about 90° apart. Ask one student to use a clearly stronger steady push than the other.
After drawing
Add a dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. Did the motion lean toward the stronger force?
Round 4 · Not a right angle

Predict the resultant.

On your whiteboardCopy the force diagram. Add the resultant vector. Decide where it should point when the angle is no longer 90°.
Round 4 · Test it

Two students. New angle.

Run the trial
Move the two students so the blowing directions make a clear angle that is not 90°. Try to use similar steady pushes.
After drawing
Add a dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. Compare its direction with the resultant you drew.
Round 5 · Three forces

Predict one resultant.

On your whiteboardCopy the force diagram. Add one resultant vector that represents all three forces together.
Round 5 · Test it

Three students. Three straws.

Run the trial
Place three students around the ball to match the three force directions as closely as practical. Count down and blow together.
After drawing
Add a dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. Compare that observation with your one predicted resultant.
Discuss before the notes

What did the force diagrams help us see?

1. How did the direction of the resultant change when the force directions changed?
2. What happened to the resultant when one force arrow became much longer than the other?
3. How can several force arrows be replaced by one resultant vector without losing the important information?
