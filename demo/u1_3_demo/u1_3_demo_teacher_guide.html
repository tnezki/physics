Two Blows, One Motion

5-8 minute phenomenon hook for Net Force and Vectors

Time5-8 minutes
GroupingWhole class + 2 volunteers
Student fileProjection only

Purpose

This demo makes the vector nature of force visible before students formalize it. With opposite air forces, students see that direction matters when forces combine. With forces at right angles, the ping-pong ball can move in a direction different from either individual push, creating a natural need for the ideas of vector, resultant, and net force.

Use imperfect air forces as a feature, not a flaw. The demo is qualitative. Students should notice the direction of the combined effect, not expect laboratory-grade force equality from two people blowing through straws.

Materials & Setup

  • 1 ping-pong ball
  • 2 drinking straws; a third is optional
  • Masking tape to mark a center point and two or three blower locations
  • Smooth tabletop or floor area
  • Optional protractor for quickly marking a 90° position
  • Project u1_3_demo.html full screen

Mark a center spot for the ball. Mark two positions directly opposite each other for Round 1. For Round 2, move one blower so the two straw directions are approximately 90° apart.

Run of Show

TimeProjectionTeacher MoveWhat to Listen For
0:00-1:00Slide 1Frame the puzzle: “Can the ball move in a direction that neither person blows?” Choose two volunteers.Most students will initially expect the ball to follow one of the two pushes.
1:00-2:30Slide 2Put volunteers opposite each other. Count down and have both blow. Repeat once with one person intentionally stronger.Near-equal opposite forces produce little motion; unequal forces produce motion toward the weaker side.
2:30-4:30Slide 3Move the volunteers to approximately 90°. Require a class prediction, then run the trial.The ball drifts diagonally rather than directly along either straw.
4:30-6:30Slide 4Ask how the direction would change if one force were stronger. If time permits, test one student prediction.Students should begin describing the result as a combination rather than a competition with one “winning.”
6:30-8:00Slide 5Transition into notes with the question: “How can an arrow preserve both how strong a force is and which way it acts?”Magnitude and direction.

Source Connection

This hook is a shortened version of the legacy Vector Force Balance Challenge. The old activity used opposite forces, right-angle forces, and three-way balance with straws and a ping-pong ball. Here, only the first two ideas are used so the demo stays in the 5-8 minute hook window; the deeper equilibrium challenge is reserved for later investigation work.

Suggested Teacher Language

“Neither student blew diagonally, yet the ball can move diagonally. If our force model only records ‘how hard,’ we lose something essential. Direction has to be part of the quantity.”

Misconceptions to Surface, Not Fix Yet

  • “The stronger force always completely cancels the weaker one.” Opposite forces partially cancel; perpendicular forces combine differently.
  • “Net force is whichever individual force is biggest.” Net force is the combined vector effect of all forces on the object.
  • “Arrow direction shows where the object is moving.” A force arrow represents a force, not automatically the motion direction.
  • “Equal-sized forces always make zero net force.” Their directions matter.

Transition Into Guided Notes

Move into Section 1.3 by referring back to the ball’s diagonal motion. The notes develop force arrows, same/opposite-direction addition, perpendicular vectors, resultants, and the role of arrow length and direction in representing force magnitude and direction.
