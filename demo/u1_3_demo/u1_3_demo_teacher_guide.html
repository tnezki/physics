Physics · Unit 1 · Section 1.3 · Demo Teacher Guide

Draw It. Predict It. Blow It.

12-18 minute whiteboard + ping-pong-ball vector demo

Time12-18 minutes
GroupingWhole class + 2-3 volunteers
Student toolsWhiteboards + markers

Purpose

Students repeatedly move through the same reasoning cycle: copy a force diagram, add one predicted resultant vector, test the situation with straws and a ping-pong ball, then add a separate dashed arrow showing what the ball actually did. The sequence stays qualitative and directly supports Section 1.3 work with vector direction, relative magnitude, and resultant/net force.

Important visual rule: The projected force diagrams contain only the point and force arrows. Do not add labels, numbers, captions, or explanatory words to the diagrams themselves. All interpretation should come from arrow direction and relative length.

Materials & Setup

  • 1 ping-pong ball
  • 3 drinking straws
  • Student whiteboards and markers
  • Smooth tabletop or floor area
  • Masking tape for the ball's start point and approximate student positions
  • Project u1_3_demo.html full screen

Use one marked start point for the ball. Reposition the student blowers between rounds rather than trying to keep permanent floor marks for every geometry.

Direction reminder: A student blows air toward the ball, but the force arrow on the ball points in the direction the air pushes the ball — away from the blower.

Whiteboard Routine

  1. Students copy only the point and shown force arrows.
  2. Students add one resultant vector from the point. If the ideal resultant is zero, they should mark that clearly rather than inventing an arrow.
  3. Run the physical straw trial.
  4. Students keep their prediction and add a dashed arrow for the observed motion.

Do not require numerical force values. Arrow length represents relative force strength only.

Suggested Teacher Language

“Copy exactly what you see: a point and the force arrows. Then add one resultant vector. Do not calculate — use direction and relative arrow length.”
“After we test it, keep your prediction. Add a dashed arrow for what the ball actually did. The solid arrows are force vectors; the dashed arrow is our observation of the ball's motion.”

What to Listen For

  • “The arrows cancel.” Ask what must be true about both direction and length for that to happen.
  • “It goes halfway between.” Ask whether that still makes sense when one arrow is much longer.
  • “The longer arrow wins.” Push students to draw a combined vector that includes both influences.
  • “The force arrow is the path.” Reinforce that force vectors and the dashed observed-motion arrow are different representations.
  • “Three forces need three answers.” Reinforce that one resultant can represent the combined vector effect.

Likely Messiness — Use It Productively

Human breath is not calibrated. Students may blow at slightly different strengths or directions, and the ball may wobble because of spin or surface imperfections. Use disagreement between the ideal diagram and the observed motion as evidence about the actual forces. Do not change the vector model into a claim that the human trial was exact.

Section Boundary

Keep the language in Section 1.3: vector, magnitude, direction, resultant, and net force. Round 1 can show a zero resultant, but do not turn the demo into the later Section 1.4 equilibrium lesson.

Final Discussion Questions

  1. How did the direction of the resultant change when the force directions changed?
  2. What happened to the resultant when one force arrow became much longer than the other?
  3. How can several force arrows be replaced by one resultant vector without losing the important information?

Transition Into Notes

End with: “We were able to make useful predictions without force numbers because the arrows carried two pieces of information at once: direction and relative magnitude. Now we will formalize how those vectors combine.”
