Physics · Unit 1 · Section 1.3 · Demo Teacher Guide

Draw It. Predict It. Blow It.

10-15 minute whiteboard + ping-pong-ball vector demo

Time10-15 minutes
GroupingWhole class + 2-3 volunteers
Student toolsWhiteboards + markers

Purpose

This demo makes Section 1.3 visible before students formalize vector addition. Students model the ping-pong ball as a point, draw force vectors from that point, predict a single resultant vector, test the prediction with air forces from straws, and then make a separate sketch of the ball's observed motion.

The instructional emphasis is qualitative: direction and relative arrow length. No force numbers are needed. The sequence directly supports the Section 1.3 goals of reading force-arrow direction and length, combining same/opposite-direction forces, and determining the direction and relative magnitude of a resultant from a simple force-vector diagram.

Force model
Solid arrows start at the ball. Arrow direction shows force direction; arrow length shows relative force strength.
After-demo motion sketch
Students add a dashed arrow for what the ball actually did. This is deliberately separate from the force vector so students do not treat a force arrow as a path arrow.

Materials & Setup

  • 1 ping-pong ball
  • 3 drinking straws
  • Student whiteboards and markers
  • Smooth tabletop or floor area
  • Masking tape to mark the starting point and approximate blower locations
  • Project u1_3_demo.html full screen

Mark one start point for the ball. Leave enough open space around it to place students on opposite sides, at about 90°, at another angle, and finally in three different directions.

Important: A straw blows air toward the ball, but the force arrow on the ball points in the direction the air pushes the ball — away from the blower. Keep every force arrow's tail at the ball.

Run of Show

RoundProjection / Student MovePhysical DemoTeacher Focus
Setup Slides 1-2. Students learn the point-and-arrow model and prepare whiteboards. No demo yet. Establish that arrow direction and arrow length both carry meaning. The resultant is one vector representing the combined effect.
1 · Opposite Slide 3: students copy equal opposite force arrows and predict the resultant. Slide 4: after the trial they add a dashed motion arrow. Two students blow from opposite directions and try to use equal effort. The ideal model has a zero resultant. If the ball drifts, use that as evidence that the actual forces were not perfectly equal. Avoid calling the human trial perfectly balanced.
2 · 90° Slide 5: students draw a resultant from two perpendicular force arrows. Slide 6: they add the observed-motion arrow and revise if needed. Move one student so the two forces are about 90° apart. Blow together. The resultant should point between the force directions. With roughly equal forces, it should be approximately diagonal and longer than either component arrow.
3 · Other angle Slide 7: students predict from an angled diagram with unequal arrow lengths. Slide 8: observe and revise. Use a non-90° angle. Ask one student to blow noticeably harder and the other more gently. The resultant should lean toward the longer force arrow. Emphasize relative length, not numerical calculation.
4 · Three straws Slide 9: students combine three force arrows into one resultant. Slide 10: run the trial and add the dashed motion arrow. Add a third student. Strongest push roughly rightward, medium upper-left, gentler lower-left. Students should reason that several forces can still be represented by one resultant. Precision is not the goal; coherent direction/relative magnitude is.
Close Slide 11: students summarize patterns. No demo. Bridge directly to vector arrows, net force, and resultants in the notes.

Suggested Teacher Language

“Your force diagram is a model, not a photograph. The point is the ball. Every solid arrow begins at the ball and tells us which way a force acts and how strong it is relative to the others. Before we test it, replace all of those forces with one resultant arrow.”
“After we test it, do not erase your prediction. Add a dashed arrow for what the ball actually did. Now compare the observation with the resultant you predicted.”

What to Listen For

  • “The arrows cancel.” Good for equal opposite forces; ask what has to be true about both direction and relative length.
  • “The ball should go between them.” Ask whether it should be exactly halfway or lean toward a stronger/longer force.
  • “The longest force wins.” Push students to describe a combined effect rather than selecting a single force.
  • “The force arrow is where it moved.” Redirect: solid arrows are forces; the dashed arrow is the separate observed-motion sketch.
  • “Three forces are too many to combine.” Reinforce that the resultant is still one vector for the total effect.

Likely Messiness — Use It Productively

Two students will not create perfectly equal air forces, and the ball may spin, wobble, or respond to table imperfections. Do not treat those imperfections as failure. Ask whether the observed drift suggests the actual force arrows should have been slightly different in length or direction. The purpose is to use a model to interpret evidence, not to make human breath into a calibrated force source.

Section Boundary

Keep the language in Section 1.3: vector, magnitude, direction, resultant, and net force. The first round may be described as equal opposite forces giving a zero resultant, but avoid turning the demo into a full lesson on static/dynamic equilibrium; that belongs in Section 1.4.

Transition Into Notes

End with: “We just predicted several force situations without using numbers. Now we need a reliable way to read and combine force arrows so the resultant keeps both magnitude and direction.” Then move into Section 1.3 notes.
