The Hidden Bounce

5-8 minute phenomenon hook for Thinking Like a Physicist

Time5-8 minutes
GroupingWhole class
Student fileProjection only

Purpose

This demo creates an immediate need for the Section 1.1 distinctions among observation, inference, model, evidence, and testable claim. Students experience how quickly people turn limited observations into explanations, then see why competing explanations require new evidence.

Do not resolve everything for students. The goal is curiosity and a need for the language in the guided notes, not mastery before the notes begin.

Materials & Setup

  • 1 bouncy ball or racquetball
  • Opaque visual barrier: tri-fold board, large book/bin, lab table front, or another setup that hides the ball from student view
  • Optional: meter stick and phone timer for a second round if students request quantitative evidence
  • Project u1_1_demo.html full screen

Before students enter, choose a spot where you can drop the ball several times behind the barrier without students seeing the object or your hand. A hard floor gives a clear sequence of impacts.

Run of Show

TimeProjectionTeacher MoveWhat to Listen For
0:00-1:00Slide 1Tell students they may report only what they directly observe. Drop the hidden ball once from a moderate height. Do not identify the object.Students will immediately say “a ball bounced.” Treat that as useful evidence of how naturally inference gets mixed with observation.
1:00-2:30Slide 2Collect 3-5 statements. Ask: “Could a microphone record that statement directly?” Sort statements aloud into observation and inference.Good observations: repeated impacts, impacts became closer together, sound changed, event stopped. Inference: it was a ball, it lost energy, it was dropped.
2:30-4:00Slide 3Require at least two models that could explain the observations. Accept imperfect models if they genuinely fit the evidence.Possible alternatives: bouncing ball; several objects dropped in sequence; object hitting steps; person tapping a surface with changing timing.
4:00-6:30Slide 4Ask what new evidence would distinguish the models. Run one student-proposed test if it is quick and safe: reveal the area, repeat at a visible height, measure timing, change drop height, etc.Students should move from “I think…” toward “If that model is right, then we should observe…”
6:30-8:00Slide 5Reveal the ball if it has not already been revealed. Transition directly into notes.The important takeaway is not “we guessed ball correctly”; it is that claims become stronger when they can be tested against evidence.

Suggested Teacher Language

“A camera can record the impacts and a microphone can record the sounds. Neither device can record ‘a ball was bouncing.’ That part is our explanation. Physics lives in the connection between what we can observe and the model we use to explain it.”

Misconceptions to Surface, Not Fix Yet

  • “If my inference is correct, it counts as an observation.” Correctness does not change the category.
  • “The best explanation is the one most people vote for.” Agreement is not evidence.
  • “A model must be a drawing.” A verbal explanation or mathematical relationship can also function as a model.
  • “A scientific claim is one that cannot be disproved.” Section 1.1 will reverse this idea: testability requires that evidence could count against the claim.

Transition Into Guided Notes

Use the final slide and move immediately to the Section 1.1 notes. The notes begin with measurements turning observations into evidence, then use models to connect measurements to explanations and finally address testability. This demo provides a concrete class experience to reference throughout those pages.
