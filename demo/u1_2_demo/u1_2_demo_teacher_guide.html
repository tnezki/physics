The Coin That Refuses to Follow

5-8 minute phenomenon hook for Forces and Newton’s First Law

Time5-8 minutes
GroupingWhole class
Student fileProjection only

Purpose

This demo creates a concrete need for Newton’s First Law and the idea of inertia. Students see the card undergo a dramatic sideways change while the coin does not immediately share that motion. The key question is not “What force keeps the coin still?” but “What interaction could give the coin enough sideways net force to change its horizontal motion?”

Keep the explanation incomplete. The goal is to make students dissatisfied with the idea that motion itself requires a force, then move into the guided notes where the law and vocabulary give them a better model.

Materials & Setup

  • 1 clear cup or glass with a reasonably wide opening
  • 1 index card or stiff piece of cardstock
  • 1 coin
  • Optional extension: tablecloth, smooth tabletop, and lightweight plastic dishes

Place the card flat across the cup opening and center the coin above the opening. Practice a quick horizontal flick of the card so the card clears the cup while the coin drops into it.

Run of Show

TimeProjectionTeacher MoveWhat to Listen For
0:00-1:30Slides 1-2Show the setup without running it. Require a prediction and ask students to name what would have to push the coin sideways if they think it will follow the card.“The card carries it,” “the coin wants to stay,” “friction pulls it,” or “inertia pushes back.” Do not correct yet.
1:30-2:30Slide 3Flick the card sharply sideways. If needed, repeat once. Ask for observations before explanations.The card moves sideways; the coin moves very little sideways and falls into the cup.
2:30-4:30Slide 4Ask what real interaction changed the card’s motion and what real interactions act on the coin. Emphasize that “inertia” is not the name of an extra force arrow.Hand on card, brief friction between card and coin, gravity on coin, support while contact remains.
4:30-6:30Slide 5Ask: “If the sideways net force on an object is zero, what should happen to its sideways motion?” Take predictions but stop before formalizing the law.Students may say “it stops” or “it stays still.” Preserve both rest and moving cases for the notes.
6:30-8:00OptionalIf time allows, do the tablecloth version as a second example of the same pattern.Students should recognize the same underlying idea in a different setup.

Suggested Teacher Language

“The card has a dramatic sideways change in motion because my hand interacts with it. The coin does not automatically inherit that motion just because it is touching the card. Before we draw a force, we should be able to name the interaction that produces it.”

Misconceptions to Surface, Not Fix Yet

  • “Inertia is a force that holds the coin in place.” Inertia is a property of matter, not another interaction.
  • “An object needs a forward force to keep moving.” Newton’s First Law will directly challenge this.
  • “No motion means no forces.” The coin and cup setup will soon connect to support force and weight.
  • “A bigger object has inertia only when it is moving.” Inertia describes resistance to a change in motion whether the object is moving or at rest.

Transition Into Guided Notes

Move into Section 1.2 with the question from the final slide: What should happen when net force is zero? The notes use the same coin-card demonstration, the tablecloth trick, the ball-between-strings demonstration, and support-force examples to develop Newton’s First Law, inertia, and the distinction between “no forces” and “no net force.”
