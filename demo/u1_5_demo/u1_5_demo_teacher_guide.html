The Diagram on Trial

5-8 minute phenomenon hook for Modeling Forces in the Real World

Time5-8 minutes
GroupingWhole class
Student fileProjection only

Purpose

This demo shifts students from knowing individual force ideas to building and critiquing a force model. A real cart provides the evidence. Competing diagrams force students to choose one object, distinguish motion from force, identify real interactions, determine the direction of net force, and check whether the force model agrees with the observed motion.

The argument matters more than the letter choice. Students should defend or reject a model by naming interactions and comparing net force with motion, not by guessing which diagram “looks right.”

Materials & Setup

  • Dynamics cart or other smooth rolling cart
  • Level track or tabletop
  • Small rough strip, towel, felt, or other surface that makes the cart visibly slow after release
  • Optional: Vernier motion detector / Go Direct Motion and Vernier Graphical Analysis
  • Project u1_5_demo.html full screen

Place the rough strip where the cart can roll across it after a brief push. The goal is a clearly visible rightward motion that slows after the teacher’s hand is no longer touching the cart.

Optional Vernier Evidence Upgrade

If the motion detector is already connected, collect a quick velocity-time graph in Vernier Graphical Analysis during the rough-strip trial. Do not turn this into a graph-analysis lesson. Simply use the graph as a second representation showing that the cart is still moving right while its velocity is decreasing. Then ask which force model is consistent with that change.

Suggested Teacher Language

“A force diagram is not a picture of where the object is going. It is a claim about the interactions acting on one chosen object. If the arrows predict a net force that does not match the observed change in motion, the model needs revision.”

Misconceptions to Surface, Not Fix Yet

  • “Moving right means there must be a rightward force.” Motion direction and net-force direction are different pieces of information.
  • “The teacher’s push keeps acting after contact ends.” Force arrows require ongoing interactions.
  • “If vertical forces balance, the whole object is in equilibrium.” All force vectors must be included before deciding net force.
  • “A force diagram is correct if its arrows are labeled.” The diagram must also agree with the physical motion and evidence.

Transition Into Guided Notes

Move directly into Section 1.5. The notes formalize the same model-checking process: choose one object, identify interactions, represent the forces as vectors, combine them into net force, compare the result with the motion description, and revise inconsistent models using evidence.
