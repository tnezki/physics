Unit at a Glance

What ties Unit 4 together?

Unit 4 follows two conservation stories. Momentum tracks motion through interactions such as pushes, collisions, recoil, and explosions. Energy tracks the ability to cause change as work transfers energy and energy shifts among kinetic, potential, thermal, chemical, and other forms.

Formula & Relationship Limit

The eight relationships Unit 4 expects

These are the complete symbolic relationships allowed by the Unit 4 assessment plan.

Momentum

$p=mv$

Momentum depends on mass and velocity, so it also has direction.

Impulse

$J=Ft$

Use for a force acting over a time interval in the simple impulse model.

Impulse–Momentum

$J=\Delta p$

Impulse equals the change in momentum produced by an interaction.

Momentum Conservation

$p_{\text{before}}=p_{\text{after}}$

Valid for the chosen system when no net external impulse acts.

Work

$W=Fd$

Use when the force acts in the direction of the displacement in the simple model.

Power

$P=\frac{W}{t}$

Power is how quickly work is done or energy is transferred.

Gravitational Potential Energy

$PE=mgh$

Use for changes in gravitational potential energy near Earth with a chosen reference height.

Kinetic Energy

$KE=\frac{1}{2}mv^2$

Kinetic energy depends on mass and the square of speed.

Keep the conditions straight: momentum conservation depends on the chosen system and external impulse; work requires displacement in the force direction; kinetic energy depends on speed squared, not velocity direction.
Section-by-Section Review
Section 4.1

Momentum and Impulse

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will explain momentum as mass in motion and use impulse to describe how forces change momentum.

I can…

  • I can define momentum as a quantity that depends on mass and velocity.
  • I can explain why a large mass, high speed, or both can produce large momentum.
  • I can explain impulse as force acting over a time interval.
  • I can connect impulse to change in momentum.
  • I can explain why increasing contact time can reduce force during a collision.

Summary

  • Momentum combines mass and velocity: $p=mv$.
  • Impulse combines force and interaction time: $J=Ft$.
  • Impulse equals momentum change: $J=\Delta p$.
  • A moving object has momentum; an interaction delivers impulse.
  • For the same momentum change, increasing contact time reduces the average force required.

DOK 2

DOK 3

DOK 4

Defend an unfamiliar momentum–impulse model using data, equations, and evidence.

Common traps to avoid

Momentum is just speed

Momentum depends on both mass and velocity.

Impulse is something an object has

An object has momentum; an interaction delivers impulse.

Padding reduces momentum change

Padding can produce the same momentum change over more time, reducing average force.

Ignore direction

Velocity gives momentum direction; signs matter in one-dimensional comparisons.

Section 4.2

Conservation of Momentum

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will use conservation of momentum to analyze collisions, explosions, recoil, and interacting systems.

I can…

  • I can explain what it means for momentum to be conserved.
  • I can identify the system being analyzed in a momentum situation.
  • I can distinguish internal forces from external forces.
  • I can explain why momentum is conserved in both elastic and inelastic collisions when no net external impulse acts.
  • I can compare before-and-after momentum in simple collision and recoil situations.

Summary

  • Total momentum stays constant for a chosen system when no net external impulse acts.
  • Internal forces redistribute momentum within the system; external impulse changes the system total.
  • Both elastic and inelastic collisions conserve momentum under the same isolation condition.
  • Objects can stop, stick together, recoil, or move apart while the system’s total momentum remains conserved.
  • Momentum is a vector, so direction must be included in before-and-after accounting.

Common traps to avoid

Cars stop, so momentum vanished

Check the total momentum of the full chosen system, not one object alone.

Internal forces destroy momentum

Internal forces transfer momentum between system parts.

Only elastic collisions conserve momentum

Inelastic collisions also conserve momentum when external impulse is negligible.

System choice does not matter

Conservation claims only make sense after defining what is inside the system.

Section 4.3

Work and Energy

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will explain work as energy transfer and distinguish kinetic energy, potential energy, mechanical energy, and power.

I can…

  • I can determine whether a force does work on an object.
  • I can explain that work requires force and motion in the direction of the force.
  • I can distinguish kinetic energy from potential energy.
  • I can calculate simple work, power, gravitational potential energy, and kinetic energy.
  • I can explain how energy changes form in everyday situations.

Summary

  • Work is energy transfer caused by force acting through displacement in the force direction: $W=Fd$.
  • Power describes the rate of doing work: $P=\frac{W}{t}$.
  • Gravitational potential energy depends on mass and height: $PE=mgh$.
  • Kinetic energy depends on mass and speed squared: $KE=\frac{1}{2}mv^2$.
  • Mechanical energy includes energy associated with motion and position.

Common traps to avoid

Feeling tired means physics work occurred

Work on the object requires displacement in the direction of the force.

Power and work are the same

Power measures how quickly work is done.

Potential energy belongs to motion

Kinetic energy is motion energy; gravitational potential energy depends on position.

Double speed means double $KE$

Because $KE\propto v^2$, doubling speed makes kinetic energy four times as large for the same mass.

Section 4.4

Conservation of Energy

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will apply the work-energy theorem and conservation of energy to explain motion, stopping distance, energy transformations, and mechanical systems.

I can…

  • I can explain that work changes energy.
  • I can use the work-energy theorem to connect net work and change in kinetic energy.
  • I can explain how potential energy transforms into kinetic energy and thermal energy.
  • I can explain why energy is conserved even when mechanical energy seems to “disappear.”
  • I can analyze energy bar charts or diagrams for roller coasters, pendulums, ramps, and collisions.

Summary

  • Net work changes an object’s kinetic energy.
  • Energy can transform among kinetic, gravitational potential, thermal, and other forms.
  • Mechanical energy can decrease while total energy remains conserved because energy has shifted into thermal or other forms.
  • Friction usually transfers organized mechanical energy into thermal energy.
  • System boundaries determine whether energy is described as transformed within the system or transferred across its boundary.

Common traps to avoid

Energy disappears when motion stops

Track energy transferred to thermal energy, sound, deformation, or other forms.

Mechanical energy is always conserved

Total energy is conserved; mechanical energy can transform into thermal energy.

Ignore the system boundary

Whether energy is “inside” or “transferred” depends on the chosen system.

Bar charts are separate from equations

Both are accounting tools for the same energy changes.

Section 4.5

Energy in Society

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will evaluate energy sources, energy transformations, efficiency, and real-world energy decisions using conservation of energy.

I can…

  • I can explain that energy is not created or destroyed but can be transferred or transformed.
  • I can distinguish energy source, energy carrier, and energy transformation.
  • I can explain why useful energy often becomes thermal energy.
  • I can compare energy sources using evidence such as availability, efficiency, environmental impact, and reliability.
  • I can critique claims about “free energy” or impossible energy-producing machines.

Summary

  • Energy sources provide usable energy; carriers store or transport energy supplied from sources.
  • Real technologies transform energy from one form to another.
  • Some input energy usually spreads into thermal energy and becomes less useful for the intended task.
  • Energy-source decisions require multiple evidence criteria, including availability, efficiency, environmental impact, and reliability.
  • “Free energy” or perpetual-motion claims fail unless a real energy input has been overlooked or incorrectly measured.

Common traps to avoid

Hydrogen automatically counts as a source

Ask where the energy used to produce the hydrogen came from.

Thermal energy means energy was destroyed

The energy still exists but may be less useful for the intended task.

One criterion decides the best source

Real decisions depend on several evidence categories and context.

New technology can bypass conservation

Any claim still needs complete, reproducible energy measurements.
