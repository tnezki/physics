Rules & structures

Keep these relationships ready

Net force

$\vec F_{net}=\sum \vec F$

Net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on the chosen object.

Equilibrium

$\sum \vec F=0$

Balanced forces mean zero net force and no change in velocity.

One dimension

$F_{net}=F_{right}-F_{left}$

This form works only after a consistent positive direction is chosen.

Representation check: Force diagrams, motion descriptions, and equations must describe the same physical situation. Do not calculate before deciding what the arrows and signs mean.
Mastery Goal 1

Evidence, Models & Testable Explanations

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Use evidence and models to distinguish observations, inferences, and testable physics explanations, and communicate what a representation or relationship means.

I can...

  • I can distinguish between an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, and theory.
  • I can explain why a claim must be testable to be scientific.
  • I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

What to remember

  • An observation is a direct description or measurement; an inference is an interpretation based on observations.
  • A scientific claim must connect to observations or measurements that could count for or against it.
  • A model or formula represents a physical relationship. Read what the quantities mean before calculating.
  • Strong explanations connect a claim to evidence and explain why that evidence supports the claim.
Key words:observationinferencehypothesisscientific lawscientific theoryevidencemodeltestable claim

Evidence check

Ask: What was measured or observed? What conclusion was inferred? What result would support or challenge the claim?

Common traps

Inference as observation

Do not report an interpretation as though it were directly measured.

Formula as answer machine

A relationship is useful only when its variables and physical meaning match the situation.

DOK 1

Classify a statement as an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, or theory.

DOK 2

Judge whether a claim is testable and identify evidence that could test it.

DOK 3

Critique an explanation or model and defend a revision using evidence and physical meaning.

Mastery Goal 2

Newton's First Law & Equilibrium

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Explain Newton’s First Law and inertia to predict when an object’s motion remains unchanged or changes.

I can...

  • I can state Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
  • I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • I can distinguish between “no forces acting” and “no net force acting.” and identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.
  • I can recognize static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium.
  • I can explain why an object at rest can still have forces acting on it.

What to remember

  • Newton's First Law connects zero net force with no change in velocity.
  • An object may remain at rest or move at constant velocity while several forces act, as long as those forces balance.
  • Inertia is a property of matter that resists changes in velocity; it is not an extra force arrow.
  • Static equilibrium means rest with zero net force. Dynamic equilibrium means constant straight-line velocity with zero net force.
Key words:forceinertiaNewton's First Lawnet forcesupport forceweightstatic equilibriumdynamic equilibrium

First-Law checkpoint

$\sum \vec F = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; \Delta \vec v = 0$

Zero net force means velocity does not change.

Common traps

Motion requires a forward force

Constant velocity does not require a nonzero net force.

Zero net force means no forces

Individual forces can be present and cancel to a vector sum of zero.

DOK 1

State Newton's First Law or identify an equilibrium type.

DOK 2

Connect a force state to rest or constant straight-line motion.

DOK 3

Defend a motion prediction while resolving an inertia or equilibrium misconception.

Mastery Goal 3

Net Force & Vector Balance

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Represent and determine net force using vectors, then use the result to classify balanced and unbalanced situations.

I can...

  • I can explain why force is a vector quantity.
  • I can combine forces acting in the same direction or opposite directions.
  • I can identify the resultant force from a simple vector diagram and use arrow length and direction to compare force strength and direction.
  • I can explain why an object moving at constant speed in a straight line can be in equilibrium and use the equilibrium rule to reason about upward/downward and left/right forces.

What to remember

  • A force vector needs both magnitude and direction. Arrow direction shows direction; arrow length represents relative magnitude.
  • Forces in the same direction add. Opposite forces subtract, and the resultant points toward the larger force.
  • Balanced forces have zero net force. Unbalanced forces have a nonzero net force.
  • Choose a positive direction before using signs so the mathematics matches the physical diagram.
Key words:vectormagnitudedirectionresultantnet forcebalanced forcesunbalanced forcesequilibrium

Common traps

Ignoring direction

Adding magnitudes without direction can produce the wrong resultant.

Treating arrow length as decoration

Relative arrow length carries information about force strength.

DOK 1

Read a force arrow's direction or relative magnitude.

DOK 2

Determine a resultant and classify the force situation as balanced or unbalanced.

DOK 3

Use a vector model and motion evidence together to justify whether the system is in equilibrium.

Mastery Goal 4

Build and Critique Force Models

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Create, critique, and defend force models for real-world systems using evidence about interactions and motion.

I can...

  • I can draw a force diagram for an object in a real-world situation.
  • I can decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.
  • I can explain how the net force affects the object’s motion and critique an incorrect force explanation and revise it using physics vocabulary.

What to remember

  • Choose one object as the system, then draw only forces acting on that object.
  • Label force directions and make relative arrow lengths consistent with the model.
  • Check whether the vector sum is zero before deciding whether the motion should remain unchanged or change.
  • A strong critique identifies the mismatch, states the physics rule, and revises the model or explanation so all representations agree.
Key words:force modelsysteminteractionforce diagramnet forceequilibriumevidencemotion

Common traps

Forces on the wrong object

Do not draw a force the chosen object exerts on something else as a force acting on the chosen object.

Diagram and motion disagree

If the force model is unbalanced, constant straight-line velocity is not consistent with that model.

DOK 1

Identify a common force or a basic feature of a force diagram.

DOK 2

Construct a force diagram and decide whether its forces are balanced.

DOK 3

Critique and revise a model, then defend the revision with net-force and motion evidence.

DOK perspective

Depth comes from the thinking

DOK 1

Recall or recognize a term, force, equilibrium type, or vector feature.

DOK 2

Use a model, combine forces, classify balance, or connect force state to motion.

DOK 3

Critique competing explanations or representations and justify a revision with evidence.

Remember: DOK describes the complexity of the thinking, not whether a question feels easy or hard.
