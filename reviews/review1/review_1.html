Review focus

Rules & structures

Keep these relationships ready

Net force

F_net = vector sum of forces

Net force is a vector sum, so both magnitude and direction matter.

Equilibrium

ΣF = 0

Balanced forces can describe rest or constant straight-line motion.

Newton’s First Law

F_net = 0 → velocity is constant

If velocity changes in speed or direction, the forces are not balanced.

Physics check: Do not invent a forward force just because an object is moving. Do not draw inertia as a force. Identify real interactions, add them as vectors, and compare the net force with the motion evidence.
Mastery Goal 1

Testable Physics Explanations

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Use evidence and models to distinguish observations, inferences, and testable physics explanations, and communicate what a representation or relationship means.

I can...

  • I can distinguish between an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, and theory.
  • I can explain why a claim must be testable to be scientific.
  • I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

What to remember

  • An observation is directly recorded evidence; an inference is an interpretation drawn from evidence.
  • A scientific claim must connect to observations or measurements that could support or challenge it.
  • A model or formula represents a relationship among physical quantities; interpret what the relationship means before treating it as a calculation rule.
  • Good explanations connect a claim to specific evidence and explain why that evidence matters.
Key words:observationinferencehypothesisscientific lawscientific theoryevidencemodeltestable claim

Evidence → explanation

Ask three questions: What was measured or observed? What conclusion is being drawn? What evidence could test the claim?

evidence → claim → reasoning

Common traps

Inference as observation

A conclusion drawn from evidence is not the same thing as the evidence itself.

Formula without meaning

Substituting numbers is not enough if the relationship between quantities is never interpreted.

DOK 2

DOK 3

Design evidence or critique a model-based explanation and justify the decision.

Mastery Goal 2

Newton’s First Law & Inertia

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Explain Newton’s First Law and inertia to predict when an object’s motion remains unchanged or changes.

I can...

  • I can state Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
  • I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • I can distinguish between “no forces acting” and “no net force acting.” and identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.
  • I can recognize static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium.
  • I can explain why an object at rest can still have forces acting on it.
  • I can explain why an object moving at constant speed in a straight line can be in equilibrium and use the equilibrium rule to reason about upward/downward and left/right forces.
  • I can explain how the net force affects the object’s motion and critique an incorrect force explanation and revise it using physics vocabulary.

What to remember

  • Newton’s First Law links zero net force to unchanged velocity: an object can remain at rest or continue at constant speed in a straight line.
  • Inertia is a property of matter that resists changes in velocity; inertia is not an additional force acting on the object.
  • Zero net force does not mean no forces act. Several forces can cancel so their vector sum is zero.
  • Equilibrium can be static (at rest) or dynamic (moving with constant velocity).
Key words:Newton’s First Lawinertiaforcenet forcesupport forceweightstatic equilibriumdynamic equilibrium

First-Law test

$\vec F_{\text{net}}=0 \Rightarrow \vec v=\text{constant}$

Constant velocity includes zero velocity. If the velocity changes in speed or direction, the net force is not zero.

Common traps

Motion requires force

A moving object does not need a forward net force to keep moving at constant velocity.

Inertia as a force

Do not add an “inertia force” arrow to a free-body diagram. Inertia is a property, not an interaction.

DOK 1

State Newton’s First Law or identify an equilibrium state.

DOK 2

Use balanced-force information to predict motion or determine an unknown force.

DOK 3

Critique a motion explanation and justify a revised force-based explanation.

Mastery Goal 3

Net Force & Vectors

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Represent and determine net force using vectors, then use the result to classify balanced and unbalanced situations.

I can...

  • I can explain why force is a vector quantity.
  • I can combine forces acting in the same direction or opposite directions.
  • I can identify the resultant force from a simple vector diagram and use arrow length and direction to compare force strength and direction.
  • I can decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.

What to remember

  • A force is a vector because it has both magnitude and direction.
  • Net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on the selected object.
  • Collinear forces in the same direction add; opposing forces subtract, and the resultant points toward the larger total force.
  • Balanced forces produce zero net force. Unbalanced forces produce a nonzero net force.
Key words:vectormagnitudedirectionresultantnet forcebalanced forcesunbalanced forces

Vector structure

$\vec F_{\text{net}}=\sum \vec F$

Arrow direction carries sign/direction information; relative arrow length communicates relative magnitude.

Common traps

Ignoring direction

Adding magnitudes without directions can produce the wrong resultant.

Decorative arrows

Arrow length and orientation are evidence in a vector diagram, not decoration.

DOK 1

Identify magnitude or direction from a vector representation.

DOK 2

Combine forces and classify the result as balanced or unbalanced.

DOK 3

Use a vector model to defend a resultant or critique an incorrect interpretation.

Mastery Goal 4

Build and Defend Force Models

Back to top
Mastery Goal

Create, critique, and defend force models for real-world systems using evidence about interactions and motion.

I can...

  • I can draw a force diagram for an object in a real-world situation.

What to remember

  • Choose one object as the system before drawing the diagram.
  • Include only forces acting on that object, such as weight, support/normal force, tension, friction, or an applied interaction when the situation supports them.
  • Draw force arrows in the correct directions and use relative arrow length consistently when magnitude matters.
  • Use the net-force result and motion evidence to decide whether the model is physically consistent, then revise incorrect models.
Key words:force diagramsysteminteractionweightsupport forcetensionfrictionnet forceevidence

Force-model routine

1. Select the object. 2. Identify interactions. 3. Draw only forces on that object. 4. Check vector balance. 5. Compare the model with the motion evidence.

Common traps

Forces on the wrong object

A free-body diagram contains forces acting on the selected object, not forces that object exerts on something else.

Model-motion mismatch

A force diagram must agree with the object’s observed or stated motion change.

DOK 1

Identify a force that acts on a selected object.

DOK 2

Construct a force diagram for a familiar situation.

DOK 3

Critique or defend a force model using interactions, net force, and motion evidence.

DOK perspective

How the thinking becomes more demanding

DOK 1 · Recall & identify

  • Identify vocabulary, forces, vector features, or equilibrium states.
  • State Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
DOK 2 · Apply & connect

Coordinate representations

  • Combine forces and interpret the net force.
  • Connect a motion description with balanced or unbalanced forces.
DOK 3 · Justify & critique

Defend the reasoning

  • Evaluate whether evidence supports an explanation.
  • Critique a flawed force model and justify a revision.
DOK 4 · Extend

Sustain a model-based argument

  • Design, defend, or revise a force model for an unfamiliar situation using multiple pieces of evidence.
