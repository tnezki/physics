Rules, Properties & Structures

The algebra moves Unit 0A expects

Unit 0A is not a formula-heavy unit. These are the core rules and equivalence structures students repeatedly use across the five sections.

Subtracting a number

$a-b=a+(-b)$

Subtraction can be rewritten as addition of the opposite; this is especially useful with signed numbers.

Product / quotient signs

$(+)(+)=+,\qquad (-)(-)=+,\qquad (+)(-)=-$

Same signs give a positive product/quotient; different signs give a negative result.

Distance on a number line

$\text{distance}=|a-b|$

Distance is nonnegative and ignores direction after the difference is found.

Fraction division

$\frac{a}{b}\div\frac{c}{d}=\frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{d}{c}$

Multiply by the reciprocal of the divisor; the divisor cannot be zero.

Order of operations

$\text{grouping}\rightarrow\text{exponents}\rightarrow\times,\div\rightarrow +,-$

At the same priority level, work from left to right.

Distributive property

$a(b+c)=ab+ac$

Expanding and factoring are reverse uses of the same property.

Combine like terms

$ax+bx=(a+b)x$

Only coefficients combine when the variable/exponent part matches exactly.

Equation balance

$A=B\Rightarrow A+c=B+c$

Equivalent operations on both sides preserve equality; similar reasoning applies to other inverse operations.

Inequality sign reversal

$a\lt b\rightarrow -a\gt -b$

Multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative reverses the order relation.

Boundary rule

$\lt,\gt:\text{ open}\qquad \le,\ge:\text{ closed}$

The endpoint is excluded for strict inequalities and included for inclusive inequalities.

Use structure, not memorization alone: a rule is useful only when its conditions match the expression, equation, or inequality in front of you. Always check the original notation and the meaning of the result.
Section-by-Section Review
Section 0A.1

Rational Number Operations

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will accurately perform operations with integers, fractions, and decimals while interpreting results in mathematical and contextual situations.

I can…

  • I can construct accurate rational number computations using integers, fractions, and decimals in mathematical and contextual situations.
  • I can apply operation rules to calculate sums, differences, products, and quotients with rational numbers.
  • I can assess whether rational number results are reasonable for the situation they represent.

Summary

  • Rational numbers include integers, fractions, terminating decimals, and repeating decimals; the same operation rules apply across representations.
  • For addition and subtraction, track direction/sign carefully. Subtracting a number is equivalent to adding its opposite.
  • For multiplication and division, equal signs give a positive result and different signs give a negative result.
  • Fraction addition/subtraction requires a common denominator; fraction division uses the reciprocal of the divisor.
  • Absolute value describes distance from zero, and $|a-b|$ gives the distance between two numbers on a number line.
  • In context, the sign and size of the result must match the situation, not just the arithmetic.
Key terms:rational numberintegerfractiondecimaloppositeabsolute valuereciprocaldistance
DOK 1

Compute signed-number, fraction, and decimal operations and use absolute value for distance.

DOK 2

Model multi-step changes or scaling situations and check whether the sign and size are reasonable.

DOK 3

Critique an incorrect rational-number claim or compare two representations of the same change.

DOK 4

Design a short rational-number model that combines representations and justify that they agree.

Common traps to avoid

Adding denominators

For fraction addition/subtraction, rewrite with a common denominator; do not add denominators.

Losing the sign

Predict the sign before multiplying/dividing so the arithmetic result has a built-in reasonableness check.

Absolute value as “make positive”

Absolute value is distance from zero; use that meaning when interpreting a context.

Ignoring units/context

A correct number can still be an incorrect answer if its sign or meaning conflicts with the situation.

Section 0A.2

Order of Operations and Evaluating Expressions

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will evaluate numerical and algebraic expressions correctly using substitution and order of operations.

I can…

  • I can formulate a clear sequence of steps for evaluating numerical and algebraic expressions.
  • I can use substitution and order of operations to calculate expression values accurately.
  • I can determine whether an expression value makes sense after substitution.

Summary

  • Substitution means replacing each variable with its given value before simplifying; parentheses around negative substituted values prevent sign errors.
  • Grouping symbols and exponents are handled before multiplication/division, then addition/subtraction, with equal-priority operations completed left to right.
  • An exponent applies only to the base indicated by notation: $(-3)^2$ and $-3^2$ do not mean the same thing.
  • Absolute value bars act as grouping symbols: simplify inside first, then take the absolute value.
  • A rational expression is undefined when substitution makes its denominator equal to zero.
  • After evaluating, interpret the output in the context and decide whether its magnitude and sign are reasonable.
Key terms:expressionvariablesubstitutionexponentbasegrouping symbolsorder of operationsundefined
DOK 1

Substitute values and evaluate expressions using exponents, absolute value, and operation order.

DOK 2

Evaluate a contextual expression or table and interpret what the output means.

DOK 3

Analyze an evaluation error and explain how notation or operation order changes the value.

DOK 4 · Extension

Design an expression with required structural features and a target output after expression structure is developed in 0A.3.

Common traps to avoid

Substituting a negative without parentheses

Replace $x=-3$ with $(-3)$ before applying exponents or multiplication.

Doing addition before multiplication

Follow the operation hierarchy and left-to-right rules within the same priority level.

Treating $-3^2$ as $(-3)^2$

The exponent belongs to the base shown by the notation.

Dividing by zero

If substitution makes the denominator zero, the expression is undefined.

Section 0A.3

Expressions and Properties

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will analyze and rewrite algebraic expressions using properties of operations and algebraic structure.

I can…

  • I can build equivalent algebraic expressions using properties of operations and structure.
  • I can extend expression work by rewriting terms, factors, and sums into useful equivalent forms.
  • I can critique whether two rewritten expressions preserve the same value for all valid inputs.

Summary

  • Equivalent expressions have the same value for every valid input, even when their forms look different.
  • Terms are separated by addition/subtraction; coefficients are numerical factors attached to variable terms.
  • Like terms have the same variable part and exponent structure, so their coefficients can be combined.
  • The distributive property $a(b+c)=ab+ac$ connects factored and expanded forms.
  • Factoring out a greatest common factor reverses distribution and can reveal useful structure.
  • Testing one input can find a counterexample, but one matching input alone does not prove two expressions are equivalent for all inputs.
Key terms:termcoefficientfactorlike termsequivalent expressionsdistributive propertygreatest common factorexpanded formfactored form
DOK 1

Identify terms, coefficients, factors, and like terms.

DOK 2

Distribute, combine like terms, factor a GCF, and move between equivalent forms.

DOK 3

Verify equivalence structurally, compare two rewrite strategies, or explain why a claimed rewrite fails.

DOK 4

Design an expression or context with specified structure and justify how different forms preserve meaning.

Common traps to avoid

Combining unlike terms

Only combine terms with identical variable/exponent parts.

Distributing to one term only

The outside factor multiplies every term inside the grouping symbols.

Changing signs during distribution

A negative factor changes the sign of each product it creates.

One matching input proves equivalence

A single test value is evidence, not a proof that two expressions match for all inputs.

Section 0A.4

Solving Equations

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will solve one-variable equations and interpret solutions within mathematical and contextual situations.

I can…

  • I can design a balanced equation-solving pathway that keeps both sides equivalent.
  • I can use inverse operations to solve one-variable equations accurately.
  • I can assess whether a solution satisfies the original equation and fits the context.

Summary

  • An equation states that two expressions have equal value; solving finds the value(s) that make that statement true.
  • Keep an equation balanced by performing equivalent operations on both sides.
  • Use inverse operations strategically to undo addition/subtraction, multiplication/division, and distributed structure.
  • After solving, substitute the proposed solution into the original equation to verify both sides match.
  • Structure can reveal one solution, no solution, or all real-number solutions when variable terms cancel.
  • In contextual equations, the numerical solution must also make sense for the quantity being modeled.
Key terms:equationsolutioninverse operationsequivalent equationsbalancesubstitution checkone solutionno solutionall real numbers
DOK 1

Solve one- and two-step equations, check proposed solutions, and classify solution types.

DOK 2

Solve multi-step/context equations and show a balanced pathway including distribution when needed.

DOK 3

Critique an incorrect pathway or use structure to explain why an equation has a particular solution type.

DOK 4

Create an equation with specified features/solution or build a context and verify the model.

Common traps to avoid

Undoing the wrong number

Inverse operations undo operations applied to the variable term, not simply any visible number.

Changing only one side

Any operation used to preserve equivalence must be applied to both sides.

Checking only the final simplified line

Verify in the original equation; that is the claim the solution must satisfy.

Forcing a numerical answer

If variable terms cancel, inspect the remaining statement to decide no solution vs. all real numbers.

Section 0A.5

Solving and Graphing Inequalities

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will solve inequalities, represent solution sets graphically, and interpret solutions in context.

I can…

  • I can construct inequality models for situations with more than one possible solution.
  • I can apply inverse operations to solve inequalities and account for direction changes when needed.
  • I can extend solutions by representing and interpreting solution sets on a number line.

Summary

  • An inequality describes a set of possible values rather than requiring two expressions to be equal.
  • Solve with inverse operations as with equations, but multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number reverses the inequality direction.
  • Use an open boundary for $\lt$ or $\gt$ because the boundary value is excluded; use a closed boundary for $\le$ or $\ge$ because it is included.
  • Shade toward values that satisfy the inequality, and test a value if the direction is uncertain.
  • Context words matter: “at least” means $\ge$, “at most/no more than” means $\le$, “fewer than” means $\lt$, and “greater than” means $\gt$.
  • Compound inequalities represent simultaneous lower/upper constraints and can be checked with boundary tests.
Key terms:inequalitysolution setboundary valueopen circleclosed circleconstraintcompound inequalityfeasible values
DOK 1

Solve one-step inequalities and identify boundary type and shading direction.

DOK 2

Solve, graph, and interpret two-step or contextual inequalities, including negative coefficients.

DOK 3

Compare methods, critique a boundary claim, or explain why a sign reversal is required.

DOK 4

Design a context or compound inequality model and justify the feasible values/bounds.

Common traps to avoid

Forgetting the sign reversal

Reverse the inequality only when multiplying or dividing both sides by a negative number.

Open/closed boundary mix-up

Strict inequalities exclude the endpoint; inclusive inequalities include it.

Shading by memorized direction

Use the inequality meaning or test a value to confirm the correct side.

Ignoring the context boundary

Words such as “fewer than” and “no more than” decide whether the endpoint belongs in the solution set.

Unit Mastery by DOK

A second way to organize the review

Sections tell you which algebra topic you are reviewing. DOK tells you how deeply you need to use it.

DOK 1 · Recognize & Calculate

Control notation and basic procedures

  • Compute with signed integers, fractions, and decimals.
  • Evaluate expressions after substitution.
  • Identify terms, coefficients, factors, equation/inequality symbols, and boundary types.
  • Solve routine equations and inequalities.
DOK 2 · Apply & Represent

Connect procedures to meaning

  • Model multi-step rational-number contexts.
  • Rewrite expressions using distribution, like terms, and factoring.
  • Solve multi-step/context equations and inequalities.
  • Connect symbolic solutions to tables, number lines, and feasible values.
DOK 3 · Reason & Critique

Explain why the algebra works

  • Diagnose sign, exponent, operation-order, distribution, or boundary errors.
  • Compare methods and explain why they produce equivalent results.
  • Use structure to classify equations or verify expression equivalence.
  • Revise flawed mathematical claims with precise justification.
DOK 4 · Design & Synthesize

Build compact algebra models

  • Create rational-number logs or contexts with required features.
  • Design expressions/equations with specified structure and outputs.
  • Build inequality contexts with defensible minimum/maximum constraints.
  • Connect tables, symbols, number lines, and written interpretations in one model.
Before the Summative

Can you do these without notes?

Vocabulary & Structure

  • Identify terms, coefficients, factors, variables, and like terms.
  • Explain equivalent expressions and equivalent equations.
  • Tell an equation from an inequality and interpret its symbol.
  • Explain open vs. closed boundaries.

Procedures & Representations

  • Compute accurately with integers, fractions, and decimals.
  • Substitute and use order of operations correctly.
  • Distribute, combine like terms, and factor a GCF.
  • Solve and check equations.
  • Solve and graph inequalities on a number line.

Reasoning

  • Predict/check the sign and reasonableness of a rational-number result.
  • Explain why two expression forms are or are not equivalent.
  • Critique a flawed equation/inequality pathway.
  • Explain when and why an inequality sign reverses.
  • Connect a symbolic answer back to the context.

Strong Unit 0A work is not just getting an answer. It is controlling notation, preserving equivalence, choosing a useful representation, and checking that the result still makes sense.
