Formula & Relationship Limit

The six relationships Unit 1 expects

This unit is mostly conceptual. These relationships are the complete symbolic limit stated in the assessment plan.

Net force

$\Sigma F = F_{\text{net}}$

Add all forces as vectors. The result is not an extra force; it is the combined effect.

Equilibrium rule

$\Sigma F = 0$

Zero net force means no change in motion: rest can remain rest, and constant straight-line motion can continue.

Weight near Earth

$W=mg$

Weight is a force. In Unit 1, g is commonly taken as about 10 N/kg when a calculation is required.

Level support balance

$F_N=W$

Use only when the object is at rest on a level surface and no other vertical force changes the balance.

Same-direction forces

$F_{\text{net}}=F_1+F_2$

Add magnitudes when the force vectors point in the same direction.

Opposite-direction forces

$F_{\text{net}}=F_{\text{larger}}-F_{\text{smaller}}$

Subtract magnitudes and point the resultant toward the larger force.

Important: Do not use $F_N=W$ automatically. First check that the object is supported on a level surface, is at rest, and has no other vertical forces changing the balance.

Section 1.1

Thinking Like a Physicist

↑ Top
Learning Target

Explain how physics uses observation, measurement, evidence, models, and testable claims to understand the natural world.

I can…

  • Distinguish between an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, and theory.
  • Explain why a claim must be testable to be scientific.
  • Use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported.
  • Explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

Summary

  • Physics uses measurements to make observations more precise and shareable.
  • An observation is directly noticed or measured; an inference is a conclusion built from evidence and a model.
  • Models and formulas organize relationships so scientists can reason from accessible measurements to quantities that may be difficult to measure directly.
  • A scientific hypothesis or claim must be testable in a way that could show it to be wrong.
  • Scientific laws, principles, and theories remain accountable to evidence; changing an explanation when strong evidence requires it is a strength of science.

Common traps to avoid

Observation vs. inference

Ask whether a camera or measuring tool could record it directly. If you are explaining what the evidence means, it is an inference.

Model = perfect copy

A model is useful when it preserves the relationships that matter, even if details are simplified.

Any guess = hypothesis

A scientific hypothesis needs a test that could produce evidence against it.

Formula substitution = proof

State what each quantity means, where the values came from, and what assumptions connect them.

Section 1.2

Forces and Newton’s First Law

↑ Top
Learning Target

Explain Newton’s First Law and use the idea of inertia to predict whether an object’s motion will stay the same or change.

I can…

  • State Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
  • Explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • Distinguish between “no forces acting” and “no net force acting.”
  • Identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.

Summary

  • Newton’s First Law says an object continues at rest or at constant speed in a straight line unless a nonzero net force acts on it.
  • Inertia is a property of matter that describes resistance to a change in motion; inertia is not a force.
  • A change in speed or direction requires a nonzero net force, but continued motion does not require a continuing forward net force.
  • Weight is a downward gravitational force near Earth, while a supporting surface can exert an upward support or normal force.
  • “No forces acting” and “no net force acting” are different: balanced forces can be present while the net force is zero.

Common traps to avoid

Drawing an “inertia force”

Only draw forces that come from identifiable interactions. Inertia is a property of matter.

Moving object needs a forward net force

Ask whether the motion is changing. Constant straight-line motion can occur with zero net force.

At rest means no forces

Gravity and support forces can be present and cancel.

Scale reading = net force

A scale reports a support-force magnitude; combine it with all other forces to find net force.

Section 1.3

Net Force and Vectors

↑ Top
Learning Target

Represent forces as vectors and determine the direction and relative size of the net force in one-dimensional and simple two-dimensional situations.

I can…

  • Explain why force is a vector quantity.
  • Combine forces acting in the same direction or opposite directions.
  • Identify the resultant force from a simple vector diagram.
  • Use arrow length and direction to compare force strength and direction.

Summary

  • Force is a vector quantity because a complete force description requires both magnitude and direction.
  • Net force is the vector sum of all forces acting on one object; it is not an extra interaction force.
  • For one-dimensional forces, same-direction vectors add and opposite-direction vectors subtract; equal opposite forces give zero net force.
  • Arrow direction represents vector direction, and arrow length represents relative magnitude when a common scale is used.
  • The resultant is the single vector representing the combined effect of two or more vectors; the parallelogram rule can show the resultant for vectors at different angles.

Common traps to avoid

Adding before checking direction

Read every arrow direction first: same direction adds; opposite directions subtract.

Net force is an extra force

List the real interaction forces first. Net force is their vector sum.

Ignoring arrow length

On a scaled diagram, arrow length communicates relative magnitude.

Every number is a vector

A vector requires direction as well as magnitude.

Section 1.4

Equilibrium and Balanced Motion

↑ Top
Learning Target

Determine whether an object is in mechanical equilibrium and explain how balanced forces relate to rest or constant straight-line motion.

I can…

  • Recognize static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium.
  • Explain why an object at rest can still have forces acting on it.
  • Explain why an object moving at constant speed in a straight line can be in equilibrium.
  • Use the equilibrium rule to reason about upward/downward and left/right forces.

Summary

  • Mechanical equilibrium means the vector sum of all forces on the object is zero: $\Sigma F=0$.
  • Static equilibrium describes an object at rest with zero net force.
  • Dynamic equilibrium describes constant-speed straight-line motion with zero net force.
  • Equilibrium does not require every individual force to be equal. Several supports can share the total load in unequal amounts.
  • For a level supported object at rest with no other vertical forces, the support/normal force equals the object’s weight: $F_N=W$.
  • A scale reading can show the magnitude of a support force even while the net force is zero.

Common traps to avoid

Equilibrium means no forces

List the actual forces, then check whether their vector sum is zero.

Equilibrium only applies to rest

Constant-speed straight-line motion can be dynamic equilibrium.

All support forces must be equal

The total must balance; individual supports can share the load unequally.

$F_N=W$ always

Use this only for a level supported object at rest when no other vertical forces change the balance.

Section 1.5

Modeling Forces in the Real World

↑ Top
Learning Target

Create and critique force models for real-world situations involving inertia, support force, net force, and equilibrium.

I can…

  • Draw a force diagram for an object in a real-world situation.
  • Decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.
  • Explain how the net force affects the object’s motion.
  • Critique an incorrect force explanation and revise it using physics vocabulary.

Summary

  • A force diagram models the forces acting on one chosen object, not every object or motion in the scene.
  • Every force arrow should come from an identifiable interaction and should communicate direction and relative magnitude.
  • Combine the force vectors to determine net force, then compare the net force with the motion description.
  • Zero net force is consistent with rest or constant-speed straight-line motion; nonzero net force means the motion changes.
  • A strong critique identifies a specific modeling error, revises it, and uses evidence, vectors, Newton’s First Law, and equilibrium vocabulary to justify the revision.
Section vocabulary: force modelforce diagramnet forcevectorinertiasupport forceweighttensionequilibriumevidence
DOK 1

Recognize the forces/terms in a familiar model.

DOK 2

Draw or interpret a force diagram and determine balanced vs. unbalanced.

DOK 3

DOK 4

Common traps to avoid

Drawing arrows for motion

A force arrow must come from an interaction. Motion itself is not a force.

Drawing every force in the scene

Choose one object first; include only forces acting on that object.

Accepting a diagram that conflicts with motion

Find net force, then check whether the stated motion is consistent with Newton’s First Law.

Calling a model wrong without evidence

Name the missing/extra force, wrong direction, unsupported magnitude, or net-force/motion mismatch, then revise it.

Unit Mastery by DOK

A second way to organize the review

Sections tell you what content you are reviewing. DOK tells you what kind of thinking you need to do with that content.

DOK 1 · Recognize

Know the language and motion states

  • Define force, inertia, vector, net force, weight, support force, and equilibrium.
  • Recognize rest, constant straight-line motion, and changing motion.
  • Identify balanced vs. unbalanced force pairs in simple models.
DOK 2 · Apply

Use diagrams and familiar relationships

  • Read and draw simple force-vector diagrams.
  • Combine same- and opposite-direction forces.
  • Use weight/support relationships and the equilibrium rule in familiar situations.
  • Explain why constant straight-line motion can have zero net force.
DOK 3 · Reason

Diagnose and connect representations

  • Correct misconceptions about inertia, motion force, support force, or equilibrium.
  • Compare a scenario, force diagram, and motion description for consistency.
  • Use evidence to explain exactly what is wrong with an incorrect model and how to revise it.
DOK 4 · Synthesize

Design, defend, or critique a model

  • Build a complete force model for an unfamiliar situation.
  • Compare competing explanations and defend the one that best fits Newton’s First Law and the evidence.
  • Design a test or demonstration that can distinguish between competing claims.
Before the Summative

Can you do these without notes?

Vocabulary & Concepts

  • Tell observation from inference.
  • Explain why scientific claims must be testable.
  • Explain inertia without calling it a force.
  • Tell force from net force.
  • Tell static from dynamic equilibrium.

Representations & Calculations

  • Read force arrows for magnitude and direction.
  • Add/subtract one-dimensional force vectors.
  • Calculate weight with $W=mg$ when needed.
  • Use $\Sigma F=0$ to reason about support/tension forces.
  • Draw a force diagram for one chosen object.

Reasoning

  • Explain why motion can continue with zero net force.
  • Explain why a scale reading is not automatically net force.
  • Spot an “inertia force” or “motion force” mistake.
  • Check whether a force diagram agrees with the stated motion.
  • Revise an incorrect force model using evidence and physics vocabulary.

Best study move: start with the section where you cannot explain the idea in your own words, then use the DOK map to raise the level of thinking from recognize → apply → reason → synthesize.
