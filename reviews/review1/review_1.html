Vocabulary

Know what the words mean in a physical situation

TermMeaning to remember
observationA direct description or measurement made with senses or tools.
inferenceAn interpretation or conclusion based on observations.
hypothesisA testable proposed explanation or prediction that evidence can support or challenge.
scientific lawA concise description of a consistent pattern or relationship in nature.
scientific theoryA broad, evidence-supported explanation of how or why natural phenomena occur.
forceA push or pull caused by an interaction.
weightThe gravitational force exerted on an object by Earth or another astronomical body.
support forceThe contact force from a surface that pushes on an object.
applied forceA push or pull exerted by a person or another object.
inertiaA property of matter: resistance to a change in velocity. It is not a force.
net force / resultantThe vector sum of all forces acting on one chosen object.
static equilibriumZero net force while the object remains at rest.
dynamic equilibriumZero net force while the object moves with constant velocity in a straight line.
Mastery Goal 1

Scientific Reasoning

Back to top
I can focus

Separate what was directly observed from what was inferred, and distinguish hypothesis, law, and theory by the job each does in science.

Observation vs. inference

Observation: “The cart traveled 1.4 m in 2.0 s.” That is measured. Inference: “The wheels must have very little friction.” That interprets the observation.

Hypothesis, law, theory

  • A hypothesis gives a testable proposed explanation or prediction.
  • A law describes a reliable pattern or relationship.
  • A theory explains how or why a broad set of observations and laws fit together.

Question to ask

“Could I point to the measurement itself?” If yes, it may be an observation. If the statement explains or interprets the measurement, it is an inference.

Common traps

“Theory means guess”

In science, a theory is a well-supported explanatory framework, not an unsupported hunch.

“A law becomes a theory”

Laws and theories do different jobs. One does not simply graduate into the other.

Mastery Goal 2

Newton’s First Law, Inertia & Common Forces

Back to top
I can focus

Explain unchanged motion with Newton’s First Law, treat inertia as a property rather than a force, and identify real forces acting on one chosen object.

Newton’s First Law in everyday language

If the forces on an object add to zero, its velocity does not change. An object already at rest stays at rest. An object already moving keeps the same speed and direction.

Inertia is not a force

Inertia describes how strongly matter resists a change in velocity. More matter means more inertia. Do not draw an “inertia force” or “motion force” on a force diagram.

Common forces

  • Weight: gravitational force, usually downward near Earth.
  • Support force: a surface pushing on the object.
  • Applied push/pull: a real interaction from a person or object.
  • Friction/resistance: a contact interaction that can oppose relative motion.

First-Law checkpoint

$\sum \vec F=0 \Rightarrow \Delta \vec v=0$

Zero net force means no change in velocity.

Common traps

“Moving means a forward net force”

A moving object can have zero net force if its velocity is constant.

“Support always equals weight”

Support equals weight only when the other vertical forces and vertical acceleration make that relationship true.

Mastery Goal 3

Equilibrium & Net Force

Back to top
I can focus

Distinguish static and dynamic equilibrium, explain how nonzero forces can still sum to zero, and use the equilibrium rule to determine missing horizontal or vertical forces.

Equilibrium rule

$\sum \vec F=0$

All force components must cancel.

Horizontal balance

$\sum F_x=0$

Total rightward force equals total leftward force.

Vertical balance

$\sum F_y=0$

Total upward force equals total downward force.

Static vs. dynamic equilibrium

  • Static: object is at rest and net force is zero.
  • Dynamic: object moves at constant velocity in a straight line and net force is zero.

Forces can exist even when net force is zero

A book on a table has weight downward and support upward. Both forces are real and nonzero. If they are equal and opposite, their vector sum is zero.

Use the equilibrium rule

If a crate moves right at constant speed while a 42 N push acts right, the total horizontal resistance must be 42 N left. If a hanging object weighs 90 N and is motionless, the total upward force must be 90 N.

Key idea: Net force controls changes in velocity. Velocity itself does not tell you the direction of net force.
Conceptual checks

Can you reason through these without calculating first?

1. Book on a desk

The book is at rest. Does “at rest” mean no forces act? Explain which forces act and why the net force can still be zero.

2. Constant-speed cart

A cart moves right at constant speed while friction acts left. What must be true about the total rightward force?

3. Inertia claim

A student says inertia is a forward force. What specific words would you change?

4. Observation or inference?

“The cart’s speed decreased from 1.2 m/s to 0.8 m/s” versus “friction caused the decrease.” Which is observation and which is inference?

5. Static or dynamic?

A train moves north at constant speed on a straight track with balanced forces. Which kind of equilibrium is this?

6. Zero net force

If a moving puck suddenly has zero net force, does it stop? Use Newton’s First Law to justify your answer.
