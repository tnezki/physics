Essential Standard

Students will analyze motion conceptually and quantitatively by describing motion relative to a frame of reference, distinguishing speed from velocity, interpreting acceleration as a change in velocity, modeling free fall, and using graphs, diagrams, data, and equations to explain how motion changes over time.

Formula & Symbol Limit

The eight relationships Unit 2 expects

These are the complete formulas and symbolic relationships listed in the Unit 2 assessment plan.

Average speed

$v=\frac{d}{t}$

Total distance divided by total elapsed time.

Distance at constant speed

$d=vt$

Use when speed can be treated as constant over the time interval.

Time from distance and speed

$t=\frac{d}{v}$

Rearrangement of the constant-speed distance–time relationship.

Acceleration

$a=\frac{\Delta v}{t}$

Acceleration compares a velocity change with the time over which it occurs.

Change in velocity

$\Delta v=v_f-v_i$

Find the change first; direction/sign matters.

Free-fall speed from rest

$v=gt$

For ideal free fall from rest, using $g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2$ near Earth.

Free-fall distance from rest

$d=\frac{1}{2}gt^2$

For an object starting from rest in ideal free fall.

Average velocity, constant acceleration

$v_{\text{avg}}=\frac{v_i+v_f}{2}$

Use when acceleration is constant over the interval.

Important: A formula is only useful when the motion model matches its conditions. For example, the free-fall equations above assume gravity is the only significant influence and the object begins from rest when stated.
Section-by-Section Review
Section 2.1

Motion and Reference Frames

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will explain that motion is relative and describe an object’s motion using an appropriate frame of reference.

I can…

  • Explain why an object can be moving relative to one reference frame but not another.
  • Identify the reference frame used in a motion description.
  • Compare motion from two different points of view.
  • Explain why everyday motion is usually described relative to Earth’s surface.

Summary

  • Motion and rest are always described relative to a reference frame.
  • The same object can be moving relative to one frame and at rest relative to another.
  • Everyday motion usually uses Earth’s surface as the unstated frame unless another frame is named.
  • Apparent disagreements about motion can often be resolved by naming each observer’s frame.
Section vocabulary:motionreference framerelative motionrest
DOK 1

Identify a reference frame and recognize whether an object is moving or at rest relative to it.

DOK 2

Compare two observers’ descriptions of the same object and name the frame used by each.

DOK 3

Resolve an apparent contradiction between two motion claims by using reference-frame reasoning.

DOK 4

Build or defend a motion model that explains how several observers can describe one event differently.

Common traps to avoid

“Really moving” without a frame

Name the comparison object or surface before deciding whether something moves.

All observers must agree

Different observers can be correct when they use different reference frames.

Earth is the only valid frame

Earth is convenient, not absolute. A vehicle, seat, Sun, or another object can also be a frame.

Leaving the frame unstated

In precise physics language, identify what position is being compared with.

Section 2.2

Speed and Velocity

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will distinguish between speed and velocity and use distance, time, and direction to describe motion.

I can…

  • Calculate average speed from distance and time.
  • Distinguish instantaneous speed from average speed.
  • Explain that velocity includes both speed and direction.
  • Compare examples of constant speed, constant velocity, and changing velocity.

Summary

  • Average speed is total distance divided by total time.
  • Instantaneous speed describes one moment; average speed describes an entire interval.
  • Velocity includes both speed magnitude and direction.
  • Constant velocity requires constant speed and constant direction; turning changes velocity even if the speed value stays the same.
  • For constant-speed motion, $d=vt$ and $t=\frac{d}{v}$ are rearrangements of the same relationship.
Section vocabulary:speedaverage speedinstantaneous speedvelocityscalar quantityvector quantityconstant speedconstant velocity
DOK 1

Recognize speed versus velocity language and identify instantaneous versus average speed.

DOK 2

Calculate average speed and compare motion cases using both magnitude and direction.

DOK 3

Critique a claim such as “same speed means same velocity” and defend a counterexample.

DOK 4

Connect numerical, directional, and path information to defend a complete motion description.

Common traps to avoid

Average = every instant

A whole-trip average does not describe every moment of the trip.

Forgetting units

A speed value needs distance-per-time units such as m/s or km/h.

Equal speed = equal velocity

Check direction as well as magnitude.

Constant speed = constant velocity

Any turn changes velocity, even when the speed stays unchanged.

Section 2.3

Acceleration

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will explain acceleration as the rate at which velocity changes and identify acceleration from motion descriptions, data, and graphs.

I can…

  • Calculate acceleration from change in velocity and time.
  • Explain that acceleration can involve speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction.
  • Identify positive, negative, or zero acceleration from a motion situation.
  • Interpret simple motion graphs and connect them to velocity or acceleration.

Summary

  • Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes: $a=\frac{\Delta v}{t}$.
  • Find $\Delta v=v_f-v_i$ before dividing by time.
  • Velocity can change because speed changes, direction changes, or both.
  • The sign of acceleration describes direction relative to the chosen positive direction; negative does not automatically mean slowing down.
  • Equal velocity changes during equal time intervals are evidence of constant acceleration.
Section vocabulary:accelerationchange in velocitydecelerationpositive accelerationnegative accelerationzero accelerationconstant acceleration
DOK 1

Recognize situations with zero or nonzero acceleration and recall the definition.

DOK 2

Calculate acceleration and interpret velocity tables or simple velocity–time graphs.

DOK 3

Explain acceleration without speeding up, including constant-speed turning, using velocity-vector reasoning.

DOK 4

Connect a graph, data table, vectors, and written story to defend a motion model.

Common traps to avoid

Fast = high acceleration

Acceleration depends on changing velocity, not on how large the velocity already is.

Negative = slowing down

Compare the directions/signs of velocity and acceleration before deciding whether speed decreases.

Ignoring direction changes

Turning changes velocity even if the speedometer reading is constant.

Using $v_f$ instead of $\Delta v$

Calculate the change in velocity first: vf − vi.

Section 2.4

Free Fall Motion

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will describe free fall as motion under the influence of gravity alone and use simple free-fall relationships to determine speed or distance fallen.

I can…

  • Explain why objects in free fall accelerate at the same rate when air resistance is ignored.
  • Use $g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2$ to determine speed gained during free fall.
  • Use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals.
  • Distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.

Summary

  • Free fall means gravity is the only significant influence; near Earth use $g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2$.
  • For an object dropped from rest, $v=gt$ describes the speed gained.
  • Distance from rest is $d=\frac{1}{2}gt^2$, so the object does not travel equal distances each second.
  • At the top of a vertical toss, velocity is momentarily zero but acceleration remains downward.
  • Air resistance can make real objects fall differently; in a vacuum, different objects share the same free-fall acceleration.
Section vocabulary:free fallgravityfree-fall accelerationair resistancevacuumaverage velocity
DOK 1

Recognize free fall and recall the approximate value of g near Earth.

DOK 2

Use free-fall formulas and patterns to determine speed or distance in routine situations.

DOK 3

Correct misconceptions about mass, air resistance, or acceleration at the top of a toss.

DOK 4

Use multiple forms of evidence to defend a model that separates ideal free fall from real falling motion.

Common traps to avoid

Heavier always falls faster

Separate gravity from air resistance; ideal free fall gives the same acceleration to different masses.

Zero velocity means zero acceleration

At the top of a toss, gravity still changes the velocity downward.

Using $v=gt$ for distance

First decide whether the question asks how fast or how far.

Equal acceleration = equal distance each second

Constant acceleration means equal velocity changes, not equal distance increments.

Section 2.5

Velocity Vectors and Motion Modeling

↑ Top
Learning Target

Students will use velocity vectors, motion diagrams, and simple graphs to model and explain the motion of objects.

I can…

  • Represent velocity with arrows that show both speed and direction.
  • Compare velocity vectors to determine whether an object’s velocity is changing.
  • Connect motion diagrams, data tables, and graphs to written descriptions of motion.
  • Critique an incorrect motion model and revise it using evidence.

Summary

  • Velocity vectors encode speed magnitude with arrow length and motion direction with arrow direction.
  • Equal-length vectors can still represent different velocities if their directions differ.
  • A change in vector length, direction, or both means velocity changed.
  • Motion diagrams, data tables, graphs, and written descriptions should tell a consistent motion story.
  • A strong critique identifies a specific mismatch, cites evidence, and revises the incorrect representation.
Section vocabulary:scalar quantityvector quantityvelocity vectormagnituderesultantmotion modelmotion diagram
DOK 1

Recognize what vector length, arrow direction, and dot spacing represent.

DOK 2

Interpret vectors, motion diagrams, graphs, and tables and connect them to a written description.

DOK 3

Diagnose a mismatch among representations and revise the inconsistent model using evidence.

DOK 4

Design, defend, or critique a complete motion model for an unfamiliar situation using several representations.

Common traps to avoid

Checking only vector direction

Compare both direction and scaled arrow length.

Equal length = equal velocity

Equal length means equal magnitude; direction must also match.

Trusting one representation alone

Cross-check a graph, table, diagram, or story for consistency.

Calling a model wrong without evidence

Point to a specific arrow, spacing pattern, table change, or graph feature.

Unit-Wide DOK Map

How deep does your motion understanding go?

The unit remains section-first, but this map gives a second way to judge the depth of your thinking.

DOK 1 · Recognize

Name and classify motion ideas

  • Identify reference frames, motion states, speed, velocity, acceleration, and free fall.
  • Recognize basic graph, vector, and motion-diagram features.
  • Recall the meaning of the Unit 2 formulas and symbols.
DOK 2 · Apply

Calculate and interpret

  • Calculate speed, time, distance, acceleration, and simple free-fall quantities.
  • Interpret familiar graphs, tables, dot diagrams, and velocity vectors.
  • Compare speed, velocity, and acceleration in routine motion situations.
DOK 3 · Reason

Connect and critique

  • Correct misconceptions about reference frames, acceleration, free fall, or velocity.
  • Explain why constant-speed turning still involves acceleration.
  • Check whether a graph, table, vector diagram, and story describe the same motion.
DOK 4 · Synthesize

Build and defend a model

  • Design or critique a motion model for an unfamiliar situation.
  • Compare competing explanations and defend the one that best fits the evidence.
  • Use words, data, graphs, formulas, and vectors together to communicate one coherent motion story.
Before the Summative

Can you do these without notes?

Vocabulary & Concepts

  • Name the reference frame before describing motion or rest.
  • Tell speed from velocity and average from instantaneous speed.
  • Explain acceleration as changing velocity, not just speeding up.
  • Define free fall and separate it from motion with significant air resistance.
  • Explain what vector length/direction and dot spacing mean.

Representations & Calculations

  • Use $v=\frac{d}{t}$, $d=vt$, and $t=\frac{d}{v}$ appropriately.
  • Find $\Delta v$ before calculating acceleration.
  • Use $g\approx10\text{ m/s}^2$ for simple free-fall speed and distance problems.
  • Interpret a simple distance–time or velocity–time graph.
  • Compare velocity vectors and motion diagrams.

Reasoning

  • Reconcile two valid motion descriptions from different frames.
  • Explain why a constant-speed turn changes velocity.
  • Correct “heavier objects fall faster” for ideal free fall.
  • Explain why velocity can be zero while acceleration is not.
  • Find and revise a mismatch among a graph, table, diagram, and written story.

Study by section first. Then use the DOK map to check whether you can move from recognizing a motion idea to applying it, reasoning about it, and connecting several representations.
