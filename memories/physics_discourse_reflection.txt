PURPOSE
=======
This portable memory is the complete build contract for Physics Discourse & Reflection documents.
It is designed to work in a brand-new chat with no prior conversation context.

Normal teacher workflow:

  "Read the PM, then build the Discourse and Reflection document for Unit 2."

The teacher will upload:
1. This portable memory text file.
2. The Physics Unit 1 Discourse and Reflection DOCX as the FORMATTING SAMPLE.
3. The CURRENT unit notes ZIP.
4. The CURRENT unit assessment plan ZIP.

Those four uploads are sufficient.
Do NOT require the Algebra 1 example, old course archives, activity ZIPs, warmups, practice sets, assessments, or CSS files to be uploaded again.

IMPORTANT: The Unit 1 sample is required for FORMAT, not content. Never copy Unit 1 physics into another unit merely because it appears in the formatting sample.

The document is teacher-facing. It is not a student handout and should not read like a script.
Its purpose is to help the teacher deliberately facilitate strong academic discourse during the unit and capture useful reflection after teaching.

===================================
Use sources in this order for CONTENT:

1. CURRENT UNIT NOTES - primary source for instructional sequence, vocabulary, examples, representations, figures, demonstrations, Stop and Discuss prompts, You Try It work, common misconceptions, and what students have actually learned at each point.

FORMAT authority is separate:
- The uploaded Physics Unit 1 Discourse and Reflection DOCX is the authoritative formatting sample.
- When the sample and a formatting description in this PM differ, the SAMPLE wins.
- Do not use the Unit 1 sample as a content source for later units.

Do not silently add later-unit concepts, vocabulary, mathematics, or formulas.
Do not redesign the course sequence.
Do not invent an instructional emphasis merely because it would make a good discussion.
The discourse document must reflect the course that actually exists in the current notes and assessment plan.

FORMATTING AUTHORITY - NON-NEGOTIABLE
======================================
The teacher will upload the approved Physics Unit 1 Discourse and Reflection DOCX with every future build.

Before building a new unit:

2. Read its page setup, paragraph styles, direct run formatting, spacing, table dimensions, borders, shading, and page-break behavior.
3. Reuse or faithfully reproduce those properties in the staging DOCX.

Do NOT create a new independent document design.
Do NOT substitute a different font because it is 'similar.'
Do NOT shrink body/table text to make the document shorter.

Measured Unit 1 reference values (fallback / QA targets):
- Page: US Letter, portrait.
- Margins: approximately 0.30 in top, 0.30 in bottom, 0.25 in left, 0.25 in right.
- Primary visible font: Cambria.
- Body, guiding questions, table text, reflection questions: 11 pt Cambria.
- Line spacing: 1.15 for normal paragraphs.
- Section heading (for example, 'Section 1.1: Thinking Like a Physicist'): Title-style hierarchy, 26 pt, bold Cambria, left aligned.
- Major labels such as Big Picture Focus, Guiding Questions for Discussion, Core Physics Idea, discourse-moment titles, and Teacher Reflection Notes: 11 pt bold Cambria.

- Table borders: thin solid black grid, approximately 0.5 pt.

- Right cells: white background, one Unicode ballot-box item per paragraph/line.
- Table autofit: OFF / fixed layout.
- Keep discourse title + GOAL attached to the table when practical.
- Each new unit section begins on a new page, matching the Unit 1 sample behavior.

These measurements exist to help QA. They do NOT replace the uploaded Unit 1 sample. If exact sample inspection reveals a property not captured here, follow the sample.

GOLD-STANDARD UNIT STRUCTURE
============================
The unit document contains Sections [U].1 through [U].5 in order.

Each section begins on a new page and uses this sequence:

1. Section heading
2. Big Picture Focus
3. Guiding Questions for Discussion
4. Core Physics Idea
5. Five purposeful discourse moments
6. Teacher Reflection Notes (Complete After Teaching)

Do NOT insert separate decorative cover pages between sections.
Do NOT create a general unit introduction unless the teacher explicitly requests one.


SECTION HEADING
===============
Use:

  Section [U].[S]: [CURRENT SECTION TITLE]

The section title must match the current course notes/assessment plan.


BIG PICTURE FOCUS
=================
Write one concise paragraph, normally 2-4 sentences.

Purpose:
- Explain what conceptual shift or reasoning habit students are developing.
- Name the most important representations, evidence, or relationships students will connect.
- Highlight the key misconception or intuition the section is designed to challenge when appropriate.

The Big Picture Focus should NOT merely restate I-can statements.
It should synthesize the section into a teacher-useful instructional story.

Good Physics examples of emphasis:
- observation vs inference,
- force vs motion,
- net force vs individual forces,
- evidence vs claim,
- model vs reality,
- energy accounting,
- wave behavior vs medium motion,
- electric current vs voltage,
- field evidence vs invisible mechanism.


GUIDING QUESTIONS FOR DISCUSSION
================================
Include exactly 6 guiding questions per section unless the current section strongly justifies a different number.

Questions should:
- span the whole section,
- surface conceptual relationships,
- connect representations,
- invite explanation rather than recall,
- expose likely misconceptions,
- be usable flexibly throughout teaching.

Avoid:
- simple vocabulary-definition questions,
- questions answerable with one word,
- prompts that require later-course content,
- generic questions that could belong to any physics lesson.


CORE PHYSICS IDEA
=================
Use the heading:

  Core Physics Idea

Write one short paragraph ending with a central question in this pattern:

  Students should repeatedly [reasoning action]. The central question throughout the lesson is: [high-leverage question]?

The central question should capture the section's deepest consistency check or reasoning habit.

Examples of the TYPE of question desired:
- What evidence supports this explanation, and what evidence could make us revise it?
- Is the object's motion changing, and what does that tell us about the net force?
- What do the arrows tell us before we calculate?
- Do all of the forces balance in a way that is consistent with the object's motion?
- Does this force model survive every consistency check?

Do not copy these examples into unrelated units.


DISCOURSE MOMENTS - EXACT CONTRACT
==================================
Default: exactly FIVE discourse moments per section.

These are not five random topics from the notes.
They should be the five most instructionally valuable moments where student talk can deepen understanding, expose misconceptions, connect representations, or justify claims with evidence.

Select moments from the CURRENT notes and assessment plan.
Good anchors include:
- a Stop and Discuss,
- a major figure/diagram,
- a demonstration or phenomenon,
- an example where two representations must agree,
- a common misconception,
- a model critique,
- a calculation whose meaning matters more than substitution,
- a transition from qualitative to quantitative reasoning,
- an assessment-plan error-analysis or DOK 2-3 reasoning demand.

The five moments should collectively cover the section rather than cluster around one idea.
They should progress in roughly the same order as the lesson/notes.

Each discourse moment contains:

A. A short descriptive title
B. GOAL ::: statement
C. A 2-column x 5-row table with these row labels:
   - TEACHER MOVES
   - TEACHER PROMPTS
   - STUDENT MOVES
   - STUDENT PROMPTS
   - LIKELY MISCONCEPTIONS


DISCOURSE MOMENT TITLE
======================
Use a concise instructional title that tells the teacher what thinking is being surfaced.

Examples of the style:
- Observation, Measurement, and Inference
- Read Direction Before Arithmetic
- No Forces Versus No Net Force
- Scale Reading Versus Net Force
- Critique and Repair a Flawed Model

Do not use vague titles such as:
- Discussion 1
- Concept Check
- Talk About It


GOAL ::: STATEMENT
==================
Format exactly:

  GOAL ::: Students ...

The goal should describe the student reasoning outcome for the discourse moment.
Use one sentence.
Prefer verbs such as:
- explain,
- justify,
- compare,
- distinguish,
- interpret,
- critique,
- revise,
- connect,
- defend,
- predict,
- model,
- analyze.

Do not inflate DOK.
A recognition task should not become strategic reasoning just because the discourse document exists.
The assessment plan remains the contract for expected complexity.


TEACHER MOVES
=============
Include exactly 3 check-box items for each discourse moment.

Teacher moves are facilitation actions, not content questions.
Good move vocabulary includes:
- Revoice student thinking
- Press for evidence
- Probe reasoning
- Compare ideas
- Connect representations
- Make thinking public
- Provide wait time
- Ask students to rephrase
- Challenge a claim
- Invite comparison of models
- Separate observation from inference
- Press for a consistency check
- Ask for a revision, not just criticism

Use moves that actually fit the moment.
Do not repeat the same three moves in every table.


TEACHER PROMPTS
===============
Include exactly 3 check-box prompts for each discourse moment.

These should be specific to the physics content at that point in the lesson.
They are the most important part of the document.

Strong prompts often ask students to:
- cite evidence,
- identify what is physically interacting,
- compare two representations,
- explain why a formula or graph makes sense,
- decide what would count against a claim,
- identify what stays constant or changes,
- distinguish an individual quantity from a net/resultant quantity,
- test a model against a motion description,
- critique a misconception,
- justify a direction, sign, trend, or relationship.

Avoid prompts that simply ask students to repeat a definition.
Avoid leading prompts that give away the answer.
Avoid teacher prompts that require content not yet taught.


STUDENT MOVES
=============
Include exactly 3 check-box items for each discourse moment.

Use short action labels such as:
- Explain
- Justify
- Compare
- Challenge
- Clarify
- Restate
- Build on
- Predict
- Observe
- Interpret
- Analyze a model
- Cite visual evidence
- Revise
- Defend
- Critique
- Connect representations
- Question assumptions

Student moves should match the teacher prompts and goal.


STUDENT PROMPTS
===============
Include exactly 3 check-box sentence stems for each discourse moment.

These should sound natural in a physics classroom and help students use evidence and relationships.

Good sentence-stem patterns:
- I can directly observe or measure ____.
- I infer ____ because the evidence shows ____.
- The forces add/subtract because they point ____.
- The model is consistent because ____.
- The representations disagree because ____.
- I would revise ____ because ____.
- This quantity represents ____.
- The data support ____ because ____.

Do not make every stem identical across the unit.
Do not over-script complete answers.
Leave enough intellectual work for students.


LIKELY MISCONCEPTIONS
=====================
Include exactly 2 check-box items for each discourse moment.

Misconceptions must be specific and plausible.
Prioritize misconceptions explicitly present in:
- the notes,
- the assessment plan,
- the section's examples,
- the representations students are asked to interpret.

Good misconception writing names the incorrect reasoning, not merely the wrong answer.

Better:
  "Students think moving objects always need a forward net force."

Weaker:
  "Students get Newton's First Law wrong."

Do not invent exotic misconceptions just to fill the row.


TABLE FORMAT - GOLD STANDARD
============================
Each discourse moment uses a 2-column, 5-row table.

LEFT COLUMN:
- narrow label column,
- approximately 28-30% of table width,
- light gray shading,
- bold uppercase row label,
- vertically centered.

RIGHT COLUMN:
- approximately 70-72% of table width,
- white background,
- one check-box item per line,
- normal body font.

Rows:
1. TEACHER MOVES
2. TEACHER PROMPTS
3. STUDENT MOVES
4. STUDENT PROMPTS
5. LIKELY MISCONCEPTIONS

Use the Unicode ballot box character:
  ☐

Do not use interactive form controls.
Do not use bullets in addition to the check boxes.

Keep a discourse moment's title and GOAL visually attached to its table when possible.
Avoid splitting the title/goal from the table across pages.


TEACHER REFLECTION NOTES
========================
End every section with:

  Teacher Reflection Notes (Complete After Teaching)

Then include 7-9 reflection questions as plain lines/paragraphs, not inside a table.

Reflection questions should help improve the course next year.
They should be specific to that section.

Required reflection categories:
1. Where was student engagement strongest?
2. Which core idea or representation created the most confusion?
3. What misconceptions appeared most often?
4. Did students demonstrate the section's key reasoning habit?
5. Which example, demonstration, figure, model, or representation produced the best reasoning when relevant?
6. What assessment evidence showed mastery or gaps?
7. What pacing adjustments should be made next year?
8. What would you revise, remove, or expand?

Not every section needs identical wording.
Use section-specific language.

Examples of specificity:
- Did students distinguish observation from inference?
- Did students use arrow direction before choosing arithmetic?
- Did students interpret scale readings as support force rather than net force?
- Did students separate wave motion from particle motion?
- Did students distinguish voltage from current?

The reflection section should NOT become a generic teacher-evaluation rubric.
It is a practical record for revising the curriculum.


PHYSICS-SPECIFIC DISCOURSE PRINCIPLES
=====================================
1. Evidence over authority.
   Ask students what in the diagram, data, observation, graph, or model supports the claim.

2. Representations must agree.
   Frequently connect words, diagrams, vectors, graphs, equations, tables, and motion descriptions when the section uses them.

3. Separate physical quantities that students commonly collapse together.
   Examples: force vs motion, speed vs velocity, current vs voltage, amplitude vs wavelength, field vs force, energy vs power.

4. Make invisible models discussable.
   Physics often relies on models of forces, fields, waves, energy, and particles. Prompts should help students state what the model explains and what evidence supports it.

5. Calculation is not the endpoint.
   When formulas appear, include meaning, units, assumptions, direction/sign, and consistency with the physical situation as appropriate.

6. Do not front-load later mathematics.
   If the notes use qualitative vector construction, do not convert discourse into trigonometric components.
   If the notes use simple circuit relationships, do not introduce later electromagnetic theory.

7. Misconceptions should drive useful talk.
   Use the common incorrect model as something students can compare, critique, or revise.

8. Do not over-script.
   The document gives the teacher options and high-leverage prompts. It should not dictate every sentence the teacher says.

CONTENT DENSITY / LENGTH
========================
The Unit 1 gold-standard prototype was approximately 16 pages for five sections.
Future units may vary based on content, but aim for similar density:
- normally 2-4 pages per section,
- five discourse moments per section,
- compact teacher-facing prose,
- no unnecessary repeated explanations.

Do not pad the document to hit a page count.
Do not shrink text excessively just to reduce pages.


SEQUENCING / ELIGIBILITY CHECK
==============================
Before writing each section, explicitly determine:
- What students know BEFORE this section.
- What this section introduces.
- What students are expected to do by the END of the section.
- Which equations and representations are currently legitimate.
- Which concepts belong later and must not appear yet.

Then choose discourse moments that fit that sequence.

A prompt may anticipate confusion, but it may not require students to use knowledge they have not yet learned.


ASSESSMENT-PLAN ALIGNMENT
=========================
Use the assessment plan to calibrate discourse complexity.

DOK 1:
- precise vocabulary,
- identification,
- classification,
- basic interpretation.

DOK 2:
- compare representations,
- explain relationships,
- use simple calculations meaningfully,
- interpret diagrams/data.

DOK 3:
- critique reasoning,
- defend a model,
- reconcile conflicting representations,
- revise an explanation with evidence,
- analyze a misconception.

Do not force every discourse moment to DOK 3.
A good section should contain a natural mix that matches the course design.


UNIT 1 GOLD-STANDARD CONTENT MAP
================================
This content map is included so future builders understand the approved level of specificity and progression. It is NOT content to copy into other units.

Section 1.1 - Thinking Like a Physicist
Big idea: separate observation/measurement from inference; evidence, models, testability, and formulas as models.
Approved discourse moments:
1. Observation, Measurement, and Inference
2. Models Preserve Relationships, Not Every Detail
3. What Makes a Claim Testable?
4. Hypotheses, Laws, and Theories
5. Formulas as Models for Thinking

Section 1.2 - Forces and Newton's First Law
Big idea: unchanged motion is consistent with zero net force; inertia is a property, not a force.
Approved discourse moments:
1. What Newton's First Law Actually Says
2. Inertia Is a Property, Not a Force
3. Predict Before Explaining: Tablecloth and Coin
4. No Forces Versus No Net Force
5. A Scale Reading Is a Force, Not Net Force

Section 1.3 - Net Force and Vectors
Big idea: read vector direction before arithmetic; net force is the resultant, not another interaction.
Approved discourse moments:
1. Read Direction Before Arithmetic
2. Net Force Is the Result, Not Another Arrow
3. Force Is a Vector
4. Resultants in Two Dimensions
5. Angled Tensions and Diagram Evidence

Section 1.4 - Equilibrium and Balanced Motion
Big idea: equilibrium means zero net force and can describe rest or constant straight-line motion.
Approved discourse moments:
1. Equilibrium Means Zero Net Force
2. Static and Dynamic Equilibrium
3. Support Forces Can Share a Load Unequally
4. Scale Reading Versus Net Force
5. Equilibrium as a Consistency Check

Section 1.5 - Modeling Forces in the Real World
Big idea: choose one object, identify real interactions, draw forces, find net force, compare to motion, revise with evidence.
Approved discourse moments:
1. Choose One Object Before Drawing Forces
2. Force Arrows Are Interactions, Not Motion
3. Make the Force Model Agree With Motion
4. Critique and Repair a Flawed Model
5. Defend a Real-World Force Model

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE DELIVERY
=============================
Ask:

- Would these prompts actually produce useful student talk, or are they just questions with check boxes?
- Are the five moments the BEST discussion opportunities in the section?
- Does the document follow the real lesson sequence?
- Are students asked to support claims with evidence from the representations they actually have?
