Summary of Terms (Knowledge)

Observation
Information gathered directly through the senses or with instruments.
Hypothesis
A proposed explanation for an observation that can be tested.
Scientific law
A general description of how something in nature behaves, based on repeated observation.
Theory
A well-tested explanation that unifies a broad range of observations and laws.
Testable claim
A statement specific enough that an observation or experiment could show it to be false.
Measurement
Comparing a quantity to a standard unit to express it as a number.
Scientific method
The general practice of forming hypotheses, testing them against evidence, and revising ideas that don't fit the evidence.

Summary of Terms (Knowledge)

Inertia
The tendency of an object to resist changes in its state of motion.
Newton's First Law
An object continues in a state of rest, or of uniform speed in a straight line, unless acted on by a nonzero net force.
Force
A push or a pull, capable of changing an object's motion.
Net force
The combined effect of all the forces acting on an object, found by adding the individual forces as vectors.
Vector
A quantity, such as force, that has both size (magnitude) and direction.
Scalar
A quantity, such as mass or temperature, that has size but no direction.
Mechanical equilibrium
The state of an object for which the net force is zero.
Equilibrium rule
The rule stating that the sum of the forces acting on an object in equilibrium is zero: ΣF = 0.
Support force
The upward force a surface exerts on an object resting on it; also called the normal force.
Weight
The gravitational force an object experiences, equal to its mass times the local gravitational acceleration: W = mg.

Think and Do (Hands-on Application)

  1. Place a coin on top of an index card balanced on a cup. Flick the card horizontally and observe what happens to the coin. Explain the result using the idea of inertia.
  2. Hang a small weight from a string and gently push it so it swings. Sketch the forces acting on the weight at the lowest point of its swing and explain whether it is in equilibrium there.
  3. Find an object in your home that is in equilibrium while at rest (such as a lamp on a table) and one that is in equilibrium while moving at constant velocity (such as a ceiling fan blade tip, approximately). Diagram the forces on each.

Plug and Chug (Equation Familiarization)

Make these simple one-step calculations and familiarize yourself with the equations that link force, weight, and net force.

  1. Calculate the weight in newtons of an object with a mass of 10 kg. (Use g = 9.8 m/s².)
  2. Calculate the weight in newtons of an object with a mass of 25 kg.
  3. Two forces of 40 N and 15 N act on a box in the same direction. Find the net force.
  4. Two forces of 60 N and 25 N act on a box in opposite directions. Find the net force and its direction.
  5. A crate sits at rest on a level floor. If its weight is 300 N, what is the support force on it?
  6. A book with a weight of 12 N rests on a table. What is the support force the table exerts on the book?
  7. Three forces act on an object in the same direction: 5 N, 8 N, and 12 N. Find the net force.
  8. An object is in equilibrium under two horizontal forces. One is 45 N to the right. What must the other force be?

Think and Solve (Mathematical Application)

  1. A 60-kg skater stands still on frictionless ice. What is her weight in newtons, and what must the net force on her be while she remains still?
  2. A crate weighing 500 N rests on a ramp. If the support force from the ramp is measured at 500 N, is the crate in equilibrium? Explain.
  3. Two students push on opposite sides of a box with forces of 80 N and 80 N. What is the net force on the box, and what does this tell you about its motion if it starts at rest?
  4. A 3-kg object experiences a net upward force of 0 N while at rest on a table. If its weight is 29.4 N, what is the support force?
  5. A rope pulls a sled with 40 N of force while friction opposes the motion with 40 N. Is the sled in equilibrium? What does this imply about its velocity if it was already moving?
  6. A hanging sign is supported by two vertical cables, each reading 150 N on a spring scale. What is the weight of the sign?

Think and Rank (Analysis)

  1. Three boxes rest on a level floor with weights of 20 N, 50 N, and 80 N. Rank the support force on each box from greatest to least.
  2. Four objects experience these pairs of opposing forces: (a) 10 N and 4 N, (b) 20 N and 20 N, (c) 15 N and 5 N, (d) 30 N and 28 N. Rank the net force on each from greatest to least.
  3. Rank the following situations from most clearly in equilibrium to least: (a) a parked car, (b) a car coasting at constant speed on a level road, (c) a car speeding up from a stoplight, (d) a car braking to a stop.

Think and Explain (Synthesis)

  1. A hockey puck sliding across smooth ice eventually slows and stops. Does this violate Newton's First Law? Explain what force is responsible.
  2. Why does a passenger in a car lurch forward when the car suddenly stops?
  3. A rock is thrown straight up. What forces act on it while it is in the air, and why doesn't Newton's First Law apply to its motion during that time?
  4. Explain why “no force acting” and “no net force acting” are not the same idea, using an example of a book at rest on a table.
  5. Why does the weight of an object depend on location, while the object's resistance to changes in motion does not?
  6. A tug-of-war is at a standstill with both teams pulling as hard as they can. What is the net force on the rope? Explain why the rope isn't moving despite large forces acting on it.
  7. Why can a boat move at constant velocity across a lake even though the engine is providing a continuous forward force?
  8. Explain why an object resting on an incline is not in equilibrium unless friction or another force balances the component of gravity along the slope.
  9. A person stands on a bathroom scale. Explain, using the equilibrium rule, why the scale reading equals the person's weight.
  10. Why is it incorrect to say that a moving object “runs out of force” and that's why it eventually stops?
  11. Two ropes support a hanging sign at different angles. Explain qualitatively why the rope closer to horizontal must exert more tension than one closer to vertical, for the same sign weight.
  12. Explain why astronauts in orbit are considered to be in free fall rather than in a location with “zero gravity.”
  13. A crate is pushed at constant velocity across a rough floor. Is the pushing force equal to, greater than, or less than the friction force? Explain.
  14. Why does a heavier object require the same force to remain at rest as a lighter object, even though its weight is greater? (Hint: consider what forces balance the weight.)
  15. Explain why the support force on an elevator passenger is greater than their weight when the elevator accelerates upward, using the equilibrium rule as a starting point for comparison.

Think and Discuss (Evaluation)

  1. Discuss whether an object can have zero velocity and still not be in equilibrium. Give an example if you think it's possible.
  2. Discuss why Newton's First Law is sometimes called the “law of inertia” rather than simply “the first law.”
  3. A classmate claims that a stationary book on a table has “no forces” acting on it, since it isn't moving. Discuss what is wrong with this claim.
  4. Discuss the difference between an object being at rest and an object being in equilibrium. Can an object be one without the other?
  5. Discuss why early thinkers like Aristotle believed a force was needed to keep something moving, and what everyday experience might have supported that mistaken idea.
  6. Two forces act on a hanging lamp: tension in the cord and gravity. Discuss what would happen to the lamp if these two forces were not equal in size.
