Summary of Terms (Knowledge)

Magnetism
A force associated with moving electric charge, producing attraction or repulsion between certain materials.
Magnetic pole
One of the two ends of a magnet, called north and south, where the magnetic field is strongest.
Magnetic field
The region of altered space surrounding a magnet, in which a magnetic force can be detected.
Magnetic domain
A small region within a magnetic material where the magnetic fields of atoms are aligned in the same direction.
Ferromagnetic material
A material, such as iron, nickel, or cobalt, that can be strongly magnetized because its domains readily align.
Electromagnet
A magnet whose field is produced by an electric current, typically flowing through a coiled wire.
Solenoid
A coil of wire that produces a magnetic field similar to a bar magnet when carrying a current.
Galvanometer
A sensitive instrument that detects small electric currents by their magnetic effect on a compass needle or coil.
Magnetic force on a moving charge
The force a magnetic field exerts on a charged particle, which depends on the charge's speed and direction relative to the field.

Summary of Terms (Knowledge)

Electromagnetic induction
The process by which a changing magnetic field induces a voltage (and current, in a closed circuit) in a nearby conductor.
Generator
A device that converts mechanical energy into electrical energy through electromagnetic induction.
Alternating current (AC)
Electric current that periodically reverses direction, produced by generators that rotate a coil in a magnetic field.
Direct current (DC)
Electric current that flows in one direction only.
Transformer
A device that uses electromagnetic induction to raise or lower AC voltage between two coils.
Primary coil
The input coil of a transformer, connected to the source voltage.
Secondary coil
The output coil of a transformer, in which a voltage is induced by the primary coil's changing field.
Turns ratio
The ratio of the number of wire turns in the secondary coil to the number in the primary coil, which sets the transformer's voltage ratio.

  2. Look up the turns ratio of a small transformer used in a household charger (often printed on the device) and determine whether it steps voltage up or down.

Plug and Chug (Equation Familiarization)

Make these simple one-step calculations using the transformer turns-ratio equation, Vₛ/Vₚ = Nₛ/Nₚ, and the power-conservation relationship VₚIₚ = VₛIₛ.

  1. A transformer has 100 turns on its primary coil and 500 turns on its secondary coil. If the primary voltage is 12 V, what is the secondary voltage?
  2. A step-down transformer has 1000 turns on its primary coil and 100 turns on its secondary coil. If the primary voltage is 120 V, what is the secondary voltage?
  3. A transformer steps 240 V down to 12 V. If the primary coil has 800 turns, how many turns does the secondary coil have?
  4. A transformer increases voltage from 10 V to 100 V. If the primary current is 5 A, what is the secondary current (assuming no energy loss)?
  5. A step-up transformer has a turns ratio (secondary:primary) of 10:1. If the primary voltage is 9 V, what is the secondary voltage?
  6. A transformer steps voltage up from 120 V to 2400 V for transmission. If the primary current is 20 A, what is the secondary current?

Think and Solve (Mathematical Application)

  1. A power plant generator produces electricity that is stepped up from 10,000 V to 200,000 V for transmission. Explain what happens to the current, and why this reduces energy loss in the transmission lines.
  2. A wind turbine spins a generator faster on a windy day than a calm day. Explain what happens to the induced voltage as the rotation speed increases.
  3. A transformer with a turns ratio of 1:20 is used to step up a 6 V signal. What is the output voltage?
  4. If a transformer's secondary coil has half as many turns as its primary coil, and the primary voltage is 100 V, what is the secondary voltage?
  5. A generator's coil is rotated twice as fast as before. Explain qualitatively what happens to the frequency of the alternating current produced.
  6. Explain why a step-down transformer at a neighborhood substation is needed before household electricity reaches a home, given that transmission voltage is very high.

Think and Rank (Analysis)

  1. Rank these generator rotation speeds by the voltage they would induce in the coil, from greatest to least, assuming all else is equal: slow, medium, fast.
  2. Rank these transformers by their output voltage, given a 100 V input: (a) turns ratio 1:1, (b) turns ratio 1:10, (c) turns ratio 10:1, (d) turns ratio 1:2.
  3. Rank these power sources by how directly they convert motion into electricity via a generator: (a) hydroelectric dam, (b) solar panel, (c) wind turbine, (d) coal power plant.

Think and Explain (Synthesis)

  1. Explain why moving a magnet into a coil of wire induces a current, while holding the magnet still inside the coil does not.
  2. Why does a generator require mechanical energy input to keep producing electrical energy?
  3. Explain why the current from a wall outlet in your home is alternating current rather than direct current.
  4. A bicycle-powered generator light gets dimmer when you pedal slower. Explain why, using the idea of electromagnetic induction.
  5. Why can't a transformer step up or down a direct current voltage the way it can an alternating current voltage?
  6. Explain why electricity is transmitted at very high voltage and low current over long power lines, rather than at the voltage used in homes.
  7. A solar panel doesn't use a spinning coil or a magnet, unlike most other electricity sources described in this chapter. Explain why it doesn't need electromagnetic induction to generate electricity.
  8. Explain the energy transformation that occurs, step by step, from water falling at a dam to a lit light bulb in a home.
  9. Why does a step-up transformer that increases voltage also decrease current, rather than increasing both?
  10. Explain why hydroelectric, wind, and coal power plants all ultimately rely on the same physical principle (a generator) even though their energy sources are very different.

Think and Discuss (Evaluation)

  1. Discuss why power companies transmit electricity at extremely high voltages despite the added complexity and cost of transformers at each end.
  2. Discuss the trade-offs between different sources of mechanical energy used to turn generators (such as coal, wind, and falling water) in terms of reliability and environmental impact.
  3. A classmate claims that transformers can make electrical energy “from nothing” because they raise voltage. Discuss what is wrong with this claim, using the idea of energy conservation.
