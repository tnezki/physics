Conceptual Physics — Unit 1: Forces, Motion, and Equilibrium

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will use evidence and models to explain how forces affect motion. They will identify forces acting on an object, represent forces with vectors and force diagrams, determine net force and equilibrium, apply Newton’s First Law, and connect the resulting force model to whether motion stays the same or changes.

Unit Source Alignment

Current Unit 1 Scope
  • Section 1.1 — Thinking Like a Physicist: textbook/source emphasis 1.1–1.2; measurement, scientific reasoning, evidence, models, and relationships.
  • Section 1.2 — Forces and Newton’s First Law: source emphasis 2.3 and 2.6; Newton’s First Law, inertia, net force, support force, weight, and simple force identification.
  • Section 1.3 — Net Force and Vectors: source emphasis 2.4; force vectors, magnitude/direction, and resultant/net force.
  • Section 1.4 — Equilibrium and Balanced Motion: source emphasis 2.5 and 2.7; mechanical equilibrium, balanced forces, and constant velocity.
  • Section 1.5 — Modeling Forces in the Real World: synthesis of 2.3–2.7; force diagrams, model critique, and motion prediction.

Section Outline

Section 1.1: Thinking Like a Physicist

Learning Target: Students will use observations, measurements, evidence, and models to build and evaluate testable physics explanations.

I Can Statements

  • U1-MG01-IC01I can distinguish an observation from an inference.
  • U1-MG01-IC02I can distinguish a hypothesis, scientific law, and scientific theory by what each does in science.
  • U1-MG01-IC03I can decide whether a claim is testable and use evidence to judge whether the claim is supported.
  • U1-MG01-IC04I can explain what a formula or model says about the relationship between physical quantities.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: observation, inference, hypothesis, scientific law, scientific theory, evidence, model, testable claim, relationship.

Key Representations: observations and measurements; short evidence sets; claim/evidence/reasoning; simple formulas and models as relationships.

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating an inference as a direct observation; accepting an untestable claim because it sounds plausible; listing evidence without connecting it to a claim; treating a formula as a plug-in rule with no physical meaning.

Evidence Expectations: Students should classify or interpret scientific statements, evaluate a short claim from evidence, and explain the meaning of a simple represented relationship.

Section 1.2: Forces and Newton’s First Law

Learning Target: Students will use Newton’s First Law and inertia to explain when motion stays the same and identify the forces involved in simple situations.

I Can Statements

  • U1-MG02-IC01I can explain Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
  • U1-MG02-IC02I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • U1-MG02-IC03I can distinguish between no forces acting and zero net force.
  • U1-MG02-IC04I can identify weight, support force, and applied pushes or pulls acting on an object in a simple situation.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: force, inertia, Newton’s First Law, net force, support force, weight, applied force.

Key Representations: motion descriptions; simple force diagrams; support-force/weight situations; qualitative \(\Sigma F\) reasoning.

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Calling inertia a force; assuming continued motion requires a continuing forward net force; confusing zero net force with no forces; placing forces on the wrong object.

Evidence Expectations: Students should explain Newton’s First Law, diagnose inertia-as-force reasoning, distinguish no force from zero net force, and identify the forces acting on a chosen object.

Section 1.3: Net Force and Vectors

Learning Target: Students will represent forces as vectors and determine resultant/net force in simple force situations.

I Can Statements

  • U1-MG03-IC01I can use vector arrow direction and length to represent a force’s direction and relative magnitude.
  • U1-MG03-IC02I can combine forces acting in the same or opposite directions to determine net force.
  • U1-MG03-IC03I can determine the direction and relative magnitude of the resultant/net force from a simple force-vector diagram.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: vector, magnitude, direction, resultant, net force, \(F_{net}\).

Key Representations: scaled force arrows; one-dimensional vector sums; simple force-vector diagrams.

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Ignoring vector direction; treating arrow length as decorative; adding opposing force magnitudes instead of subtracting; reversing resultant direction.

Evidence Expectations: Students should read and construct force arrows, determine net force in same/opposite-direction cases, and interpret the resultant shown by a simple diagram.

Section 1.4: Equilibrium and Balanced Motion

Learning Target: Students will use the equilibrium rule to explain balanced-force situations at rest and in constant-velocity motion.

I Can Statements

  • U1-MG03-IC04I can distinguish static equilibrium from dynamic equilibrium.
  • U1-MG03-IC05I can explain how an object can have forces acting on it and still have zero net force.
  • U1-MG03-IC06I can use the equilibrium rule to determine an unknown horizontal or vertical force.
  • U1-MG03-IC07I can explain why zero net force means an object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity in a straight line.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: mechanical equilibrium, static equilibrium, dynamic equilibrium, balanced forces, constant velocity, \(\Sigma F=0\).

Key Representations: balanced force diagrams; horizontal/vertical force balances; rest and constant-velocity motion descriptions.

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Equating equilibrium with rest only; assuming forces disappear at equilibrium; treating constant speed with changing direction as equilibrium; using the equilibrium rule when net force is not zero.

Evidence Expectations: Students should classify equilibrium, explain how balanced forces can coexist, solve a simple missing-force balance, and connect zero net force to unchanged velocity.

Section 1.5: Modeling Forces in the Real World

Learning Target: Students will create, use, and critique force models for real-world situations.

I Can Statements

  • U1-MG04-IC01I can draw a force diagram that includes only the forces acting on the chosen object.
  • U1-MG04-IC02I can use a force diagram to decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.
  • U1-MG04-IC03I can use net force to predict whether an object’s velocity will stay the same or change.
  • U1-MG04-IC04I can critique and revise an incorrect force model or force explanation using evidence and physics vocabulary.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: force model, system, interaction, net force, equilibrium, evidence.

Key Representations: real-world force diagrams; written scenarios; motion descriptions; observations/evidence; model critiques.

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Including forces that act on other objects; omitting support or weight; adding a force in the direction of motion simply because the object is moving; matching a force model to the wrong motion state.

Evidence Expectations: Students should construct a force model, use it to classify balance and predict motion change, and diagnose/revise a flawed model or explanation.

Evidence Opportunity Plan

Atomic evidence rule: Each I Can above is one judgeable evidence address. A single task may provide evidence for more than one I Can only when the student work contains separately judgeable evidence for each target. Multiple judgments from one task/source do not become multiple independent opportunities for the same I Can.

Short Evidence

Short checks should sample exact I Cans directly, using physical situations and representations that reveal the intended thinking. They should not be generated from a section quota or from old WTC/Example/YTI inventory.

Richer Evidence

Investigations, model critiques, CER, whiteboarding, diagram construction, and multi-representation reasoning may provide richer evidence when the task genuinely exposes the targeted I Can. The artifact type itself does not make evidence stronger; the student action does.

Repeated Evidence

Revisit important I Cans through meaningfully different situations or representations. Repeated prompts inside one source are useful for learning but do not count as separate independent demonstrations of the same I Can.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 1 Summative Assessment

Evidence purpose: Collect secure, independent evidence across all four Mastery Goals listed above. The Assessment Plan defines the learning/evidence identity; the later secure Bank/Summative builder selects the actual questions.

Breadth: Selected-response or concise classification/interpretation can sample broad Unit knowledge efficiently.

Stronger evidence: Constructed-response/show-work items should require students to interpret or create force/vector models, determine or reason about net force/equilibrium, connect force to motion, or explain/critique a physical situation.

Representation expectations: Use an appropriate mix of force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, net-force/equilibrium relationships, and evidence/model reasoning. Do not assess only vocabulary recognition or naked plug-and-chug.

Security: Summative prompts must be semantically independent from exposed Student Bank and Practice examples while preserving the same exact I Can evidence demand.

Implementation boundary: Question counts/forms belong to the active course Summative contract. No Learning Ladder, DOK quota, WTC quota, Notes-example quota, or practice-inventory requirement is created by this plan.
