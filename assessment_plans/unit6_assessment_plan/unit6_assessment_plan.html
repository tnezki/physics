Conceptual Physics — Unit 6: Light and Optics

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze light and optical systems by describing light as an electromagnetic wave, explaining how light interacts with transparent and opaque materials, modeling reflection and mirror images, explaining refraction and total internal reflection, interpreting lenses and image formation, and applying optics ideas to real-world devices and visual phenomena.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Course Structure: Conceptual Physics, Unit 6 of 8, five sections.
  • Textbook Alignment: 6.1 Nature of Light — 26.1–26.6; 6.2 Reflection and Mirrors — 28.1, 28.2 + mirrors extension; 6.3 Refraction and Total Internal Reflection — 28.3–28.6; 6.4 Lenses and Optical Systems — 28.7; 6.5 Optical Applications — synthesis/application
  • Course-Specific Emphasis: Conceptual understanding before computation; investigations/phenomenon tasks; Reading Apprenticeship discourse; cooperative learning, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and structured application.
  • Pacing Guidance: Framework presents a recurring concept-development → investigation/discussion → structured application/CER arc for each of the five sections; treat this as flexible guidance rather than a required Day 1/2/3 calendar.
  • Assessment Boundary: The plan describes evidence for current Unit 6 learning and recurring scientific practices. It does not prescribe daily timing or write assessment questions.

Section Outline

Section 6.1: Nature of Light

Textbook Sections: 26.1–26.6

Learning Target: Students will explain light as electromagnetic radiation and describe how light travels, interacts with matter, forms shadows, and allows vision.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that light is an electromagnetic wave.
  • I can describe the relationship between wavelength, frequency, and wave speed.
  • I can identify visible light as one part of the electromagnetic spectrum and distinguish transparent, translucent, and opaque materials.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electromagnetic wave, wavelength, frequency, visible spectrum, transparent, translucent, opaque

Key Representations: EM spectrum representations; $c=f\lambda$; ray paths; transmission/absorption/shadow models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Thinking light needs a material medium; treating color order as speed order in vacuum; confusing transparent and translucent

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Explain vision, shadows, transmission, absorption, and the place of visible light in the electromagnetic spectrum

Recurring Practice Opportunities: modeling; representation interpretation; explanation

Section 6.2: Reflection and Mirrors

Textbook Sections: 28.1, 28.2 + mirrors extension

Learning Target: Students will use the law of reflection to explain reflected light, plane mirror images, diffuse reflection, and basic mirror behavior.

I Can Statements

  • I can define reflection as light bouncing from a surface.
  • I can identify the incident ray, reflected ray, normal line, angle of incidence, and angle of reflection.
  • I can apply the law of reflection to ray diagrams and explain why smooth surfaces make clear images and rough surfaces produce diffuse reflection.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: reflection, incident ray, reflected ray, normal, angle of incidence, angle of reflection, diffuse reflection

Key Representations: ray diagrams; $\theta_i=\theta_r$; plane-mirror geometry; surface models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal; treating diffuse reflection as no reflection; placing virtual images on the mirror surface

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Use ray models to predict reflection and explain clear versus diffuse images

Recurring Practice Opportunities: diagram modeling; quantitative reasoning; model critique

Section 6.3: Refraction and Total Internal Reflection

Textbook Sections: 28.3–28.6

Learning Target: Students will explain refraction as bending caused by changes in light speed and use this idea to analyze optical illusions, dispersion, rainbows, and total internal reflection.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that light bends when it changes speed entering a different medium at an angle.
  • I can predict whether light bends toward or away from the normal.
  • I can explain why objects can appear shifted or bent in water and explain dispersion as separation of colors caused by different amounts of refraction.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: refraction, index of refraction, normal, dispersion, critical angle, total internal reflection

Key Representations: refracted ray diagrams; $n=c/v$; Snell’s law; critical-angle and prism/fiber models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Saying light bends because it “chooses” a path; reversing toward/away from normal; applying total internal reflection in the wrong direction

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze light crossing media, apparent shifts, prisms, rainbows, and optical fibers

Recurring Practice Opportunities: multi-representation modeling; quantitative reasoning; explanation

Section 6.4: Lenses and Optical Systems

Textbook Sections: 28.7

Learning Target: Students will use ray diagrams and conceptual lens models to explain how lenses form images.

I Can Statements

  • I can distinguish converging lenses from diverging lenses.
  • I can draw simple ray diagrams for lenses.
  • I can identify focal point, focal length, object distance, image distance, and magnification and distinguish real and virtual images formed by lenses.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: converging lens, diverging lens, focal point, focal length, real image, virtual image, magnification

Key Representations: principal-ray diagrams; thin-lens equation; magnification; object/image diagrams

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Assuming every converging lens makes a real image; confusing focal point with image position; inconsistent sign/diagram interpretation

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Use ray diagrams and lens relationships to explain image type, orientation, and size

Recurring Practice Opportunities: modeling; quantitative reasoning; representation translation

Section 6.5: Optical Applications

Textbook Sections: synthesis/application

Learning Target: Students will apply reflection, refraction, lenses, and light-wave ideas to optical devices, vision, color effects, and real-world design problems.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain how optical devices use reflection, refraction, or lenses.
  • I can describe how the eye focuses light and why corrective lenses are useful.
  • I can explain how optical fibers guide light and analyze real-world phenomena such as mirages, rainbows, glare, shadows, and image formation.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: optical system, vision, corrective lens, optical fiber, dispersion, image formation

Key Representations: device diagrams; eye/lens models; fiber paths; multi-step ray models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Using one optical principle to explain a multi-part device; confusing image formation with simple reflection; ignoring energy/light-path constraints

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze eyes, corrective lenses, fibers, periscopes, cameras, and atmospheric/visual phenomena

Recurring Practice Opportunities: systems modeling; application/transfer; argument from evidence

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Light is electromagnetic radiation that can be represented with both wave relationships and ray models.
  • Reflection and refraction depend on geometry and interactions at boundaries.
  • Optical systems combine reflection, refraction, lenses, and total internal reflection to form images or guide light.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: EM spectrum models, ray diagrams, mirror/lens diagrams, refraction/fiber models, optical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: EM spectrum models, ray diagrams, mirror/lens diagrams, refraction/fiber models, optical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving wave speed, reflection/refraction, index, critical-angle, lens, and magnification relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: EM spectrum models, ray diagrams, mirror/lens diagrams, refraction/fiber models, optical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student identifies what evidence would test a claim or model, uses observations/data appropriately, and evaluates whether results support the explanation.

Representations / Contexts: EM spectrum models, ray diagrams, mirror/lens diagrams, refraction/fiber models, optical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Treating one observation as proof; failing to control/identify relevant variables; reporting data without connecting it to the claim or model.

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 6.1, 6.2, 6.3, 6.4, 6.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: EM spectrum models, ray diagrams, mirror/lens diagrams, refraction/fiber models, optical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U6-MG01

Mastery Goal: Explain light as electromagnetic radiation and connect wavelength, frequency, transmission, absorption, and shadow formation to observable behavior.

Supporting Learning: Sections 6.1.

Recurring Practices: modeling; representation interpretation.

Key Representations: EM spectrum, $c=f\lambda$, ray paths, material-interaction models.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence describes light without requiring a material medium and correctly interprets how wavelength/frequency and material interactions affect observable behavior.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Light-needs-medium; transparent/translucent confusion; spectrum misconceptions.

U6-MG02

Mastery Goal: Use the law of reflection and ray models to explain reflected light, plane-mirror images, and diffuse reflection.

Supporting Learning: Sections 6.2.

Recurring Practices: diagram modeling; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: incident/reflected rays, normal line, $\theta_i=\theta_r$, mirror diagrams.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence measures angles from the normal, constructs a consistent ray model, and distinguishes clear image formation from diffuse reflection.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Angles measured from surface; diffuse means no reflection; virtual-image placement errors.

U6-MG03

Mastery Goal: Explain and quantify refraction, dispersion, critical angle, and total internal reflection as consequences of changes in light speed across media.

Supporting Learning: Sections 6.3.

Recurring Practices: multi-representation modeling; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: Snell’s law, index of refraction, refracted-ray diagrams, prism/fiber models.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence predicts bending direction, uses source-supported relationships appropriately, and identifies conditions for total internal reflection.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Toward/away reversal; applying TIR in wrong medium direction; refraction without speed-change reasoning.

U6-MG04

Mastery Goal: Model image formation with lenses and apply optical principles to eyes, corrective lenses, fibers, and other systems.

Supporting Learning: Sections 6.4, 6.5.

Recurring Practices: systems modeling; application/transfer.

Key Representations: ray diagrams, thin-lens relationship, magnification, device/system diagrams.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence uses a coherent ray/lens model to determine image behavior and explains which optical principle enables a real device or phenomenon.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Every converging lens gives real image; focal point=image point; one-principle explanation for multi-part devices.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 6 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U6-MG01, U6-MG02, U6-MG03, U6-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of EM spectrum models, ray diagrams, mirror/lens diagrams, refraction/fiber models, optical relationships. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 6 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
