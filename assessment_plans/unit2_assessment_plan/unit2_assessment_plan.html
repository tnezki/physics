Conceptual Physics — Unit 2: Motion and Kinematics

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze motion conceptually and quantitatively by describing motion relative to a frame of reference, distinguishing speed from velocity, interpreting acceleration as a change in velocity, modeling free fall, and using graphs, diagrams, data, and equations to explain how motion changes over time.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Course Structure: Conceptual Physics, Unit 2 of 8, five sections.
  • Textbook Alignment: 2.1 Motion and Reference Frames — 3.1; 2.2 Speed and Velocity — 3.2, 3.3; 2.3 Acceleration — 3.4; 2.4 Free Fall Motion — 3.5; 2.5 Velocity Vectors and Motion Modeling — 3.6

Section Outline

Section 2.1: Motion and Reference Frames

Textbook Sections: 3.1

Learning Target: Students will explain that motion is relative and describe an object’s motion using an appropriate frame of reference.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain why an object can be moving relative to one reference frame but not another.
  • I can identify the reference frame used in a motion description.
  • I can compare motion from two different points of view and explain why everyday motion is usually described relative to Earth’s surface.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: motion, reference frame, relative motion, position

Key Representations: reference-frame sketches; position descriptions; paired-observer narratives

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating motion as absolute; switching reference frames mid-explanation; omitting what the motion is measured relative to

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Compare the same object from Earth, vehicle, and moving-observer reference frames

Recurring Practice Opportunities: modeling; perspective/representation reasoning; explanation

Section 2.2: Speed and Velocity

Textbook Sections: 3.2, 3.3

Learning Target: Students will distinguish between speed and velocity and use distance, time, and direction to describe motion.

I Can Statements

  • I can calculate average speed from distance and time.
  • I can distinguish instantaneous speed from average speed.
  • I can explain that velocity includes both speed and direction and compare examples of constant speed, constant velocity, and changing velocity.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: distance, time, average speed, instantaneous speed, velocity, direction

Key Representations: $v=d/t$; distance-time data; motion descriptions; velocity arrows

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Using speed and velocity as synonyms; reporting velocity without direction; confusing instantaneous and average speed

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze trips, moving objects, and timing data; compare constant speed with constant velocity

Recurring Practice Opportunities: quantitative reasoning; data interpretation; communication with units

Section 2.3: Acceleration

Textbook Sections: 3.4

Learning Target: Students will explain acceleration as the rate at which velocity changes and identify acceleration from motion descriptions, data, and graphs.

I Can Statements

  • I can calculate acceleration from change in velocity and time.
  • I can explain that acceleration can involve speeding up, slowing down, or changing direction.
  • I can identify positive, negative, or zero acceleration from a motion situation and interpret simple motion graphs and connect them to velocity or acceleration.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: acceleration, velocity, change in velocity, positive/negative/zero acceleration

Key Representations: $a=\Delta v/t$; velocity-time data; motion graphs; motion diagrams

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Defining acceleration only as speeding up; using sign alone to decide speeding up/slowing down; confusing velocity with acceleration

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Interpret changing-motion data and graphs; connect changes in speed or direction to acceleration

Recurring Practice Opportunities: graph/data interpretation; quantitative reasoning; model translation

Section 2.4: Free Fall Motion

Textbook Sections: 3.5

Learning Target: Students will describe free fall as motion under the influence of gravity alone and use simple free-fall relationships to determine speed or distance fallen.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain why objects in free fall accelerate at the same rate when air resistance is ignored.
  • I can use $g \approx 10\text{ m/s}^2$ to determine speed gained during free fall.
  • I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: free fall, gravity, $g$, air resistance, acceleration

Key Representations: $v=gt$; $d=\frac{1}{2}gt^2$; falling-motion diagrams; time/speed/distance tables

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Believing heavier objects fall faster in ideal free fall; treating any falling object as free fall; confusing $g$ with speed

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Compare ideal free fall with falling through air; predict speed and distance patterns over time

Recurring Practice Opportunities: mathematical reasoning; model evaluation; explanation of phenomena

Section 2.5: Velocity Vectors and Motion Modeling

Textbook Sections: 3.6

Learning Target: Students will use velocity vectors, motion diagrams, and simple graphs to model and explain the motion of objects.

I Can Statements

  • I can represent velocity with arrows that show both speed and direction.
  • I can compare velocity vectors to determine whether an object’s velocity is changing.
  • I can connect motion diagrams, data tables, and graphs to written descriptions of motion and critique an incorrect motion model and revise it using evidence.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: velocity vector, motion diagram, position, velocity, acceleration

Key Representations: velocity arrows; dot diagrams; tables; simple position/velocity graphs; written motion descriptions

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating vector direction as optional; matching graph height to physical height; failing to distinguish position, velocity, and acceleration representations

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Build and critique multi-representation models of an object’s motion

Recurring Practice Opportunities: develop/evaluate models; representation translation; argument from evidence

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Motion descriptions depend on a stated reference frame.
  • Velocity and acceleration require attention to direction as well as magnitude.
  • Graphs, tables, vectors, diagrams, and equations should tell a consistent story about motion.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: motion diagrams, reference-frame sketches, tables, graphs, velocity vectors, kinematics relationships

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: motion diagrams, reference-frame sketches, tables, graphs, velocity vectors, kinematics relationships

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving speed, acceleration, and free-fall relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: motion diagrams, reference-frame sketches, tables, graphs, velocity vectors, kinematics relationships

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student identifies what evidence would test a claim or model, uses observations/data appropriately, and evaluates whether results support the explanation.

Representations / Contexts: motion diagrams, reference-frame sketches, tables, graphs, velocity vectors, kinematics relationships

Common Weaknesses: Treating one observation as proof; failing to control/identify relevant variables; reporting data without connecting it to the claim or model.

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 2.1, 2.2, 2.3, 2.4, 2.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: motion diagrams, reference-frame sketches, tables, graphs, velocity vectors, kinematics relationships

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U2-MG01

Mastery Goal: Describe motion relative to a chosen reference frame and compare how the same motion can be described from different viewpoints.

Supporting Learning: Sections 2.1.

Recurring Practices: modeling; scientific explanation.

Key Representations: reference-frame sketches and verbal motion descriptions.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence identifies the frame explicitly and gives internally consistent relative-motion descriptions.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Treating motion as absolute; changing frames without noticing.

U2-MG02

Mastery Goal: Distinguish speed from velocity and use distance, time, and direction to describe motion quantitatively and conceptually.

Supporting Learning: Sections 2.2.

Recurring Practices: quantitative reasoning; communication with units.

Key Representations: distance/time data, $v=d/t$, velocity arrows, written descriptions.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence calculates or interprets average speed correctly and includes direction when describing velocity.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Using speed and velocity interchangeably; omitting units/direction; confusing instantaneous and average speed.

U2-MG03

Mastery Goal: Explain acceleration as change in velocity and analyze free-fall motion when gravity is the dominant force.

Supporting Learning: Sections 2.3, 2.4.

Recurring Practices: graph/data interpretation; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: velocity-time information, acceleration calculations, free-fall tables/diagrams, $g$ relationships.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence recognizes speeding up, slowing down, or direction change as acceleration and applies free-fall relationships with an appropriate gravity model.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Acceleration-only-means-speeding-up; heavier-falls-faster; treating $g$ as speed.

U2-MG04

Mastery Goal: Build and critique multi-representation motion models using vectors, diagrams, tables, graphs, and equations.

Supporting Learning: Sections 2.5 with 2.1–2.4.

Recurring Practices: develop/evaluate models; representation translation.

Key Representations: motion diagrams, velocity vectors, tables, graphs, written scenarios.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence makes consistent connections among at least two representations and explains any change in velocity.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Graph-shape misconceptions; mixing position, velocity, and acceleration; vector direction errors.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 2 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U2-MG01, U2-MG02, U2-MG03, U2-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of motion diagrams, reference-frame sketches, tables, graphs, velocity vectors, kinematics relationships. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 2 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
