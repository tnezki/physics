Conceptual Physics — Unit 4: Momentum and Energy

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze momentum and energy as conserved quantities by explaining impulse, momentum change, collisions, work, power, kinetic energy, potential energy, conservation of energy, energy transfer, and energy use in real-world systems.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Course Structure: Conceptual Physics, Unit 4 of 8, five sections.
  • Textbook Alignment: 4.1 Momentum and Impulse — 6.1–6.3; 4.2 Conservation of Momentum — 6.4–6.7; 4.3 Work and Energy — 7.1–7.3; 4.4 Conservation of Energy — 7.4, 7.5; 4.5 Energy in Society — 7.8

  • Assessment Boundary: The plan describes evidence for current Unit 4 learning and recurring scientific practices. It does not prescribe daily timing or write assessment questions.

Section Outline

Section 4.1: Momentum and Impulse

Textbook Sections: 6.1–6.3

Learning Target: Students will explain momentum as mass in motion and use impulse to describe how forces change momentum.

I Can Statements

  • I can define momentum as a quantity that depends on mass and velocity.
  • I can explain why a large mass, high speed, or both can produce large momentum.
  • I can explain impulse as force acting over a time interval and connect impulse to change in momentum.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: momentum, impulse, force, time interval, change in momentum

Key Representations: $p=mv$; $J=Ft$; $J=\Delta p$; before/after velocity data

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating momentum as the same as force; ignoring direction; assuming a short large force and long small force cannot have the same impulse

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze cushioning, stopping, catching, and collision situations by relating force-time effects to momentum change

Recurring Practice Opportunities: quantitative reasoning; data interpretation; explanation

Section 4.2: Conservation of Momentum

Textbook Sections: 6.4–6.7

Learning Target: Students will use conservation of momentum to analyze collisions, explosions, recoil, and interacting systems.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain what it means for momentum to be conserved.
  • I can identify the system being analyzed in a momentum situation.
  • I can distinguish internal forces from external forces and explain why momentum is conserved in both elastic and inelastic collisions when no net external impulse acts.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: conservation of momentum, system, internal force, external force, collision, recoil

Key Representations: before/after momentum diagrams; system boundaries; $p_{before}=p_{after}$; collision data

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Conserving each object’s momentum instead of system momentum; ignoring external impulse; confusing elastic with momentum-conserving

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze collisions, explosions, and recoil by defining the system and comparing total momentum before and after

Recurring Practice Opportunities: system reasoning; conservation modeling; argument from evidence

Section 4.3: Work and Energy

Textbook Sections: 7.1–7.3

Learning Target: Students will explain work as energy transfer and distinguish kinetic energy, potential energy, mechanical energy, and power.

I Can Statements

  • I can determine whether a force does work on an object.
  • I can explain that work requires force and motion in the direction of the force.
  • I can distinguish kinetic energy from potential energy and calculate simple work, power, gravitational potential energy, and kinetic energy.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: work, energy, kinetic energy, gravitational potential energy, power

Key Representations: work diagrams; $W=Fd$; $P=W/t$; $KE=\frac12mv^2$; $PE=mgh$

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Assuming any applied force does work; confusing power with energy; treating kinetic and potential energy as substances

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Compare work and power in lifting/moving situations; connect motion and position to stored or kinetic energy

Recurring Practice Opportunities: quantitative reasoning; energy modeling; unit interpretation

Section 4.4: Conservation of Energy

Textbook Sections: 7.4, 7.5

Learning Target: Students will apply the work-energy theorem and conservation of energy to explain motion, stopping distance, energy transformations, and mechanical systems.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that work changes energy.
  • I can use the work-energy theorem to connect net work and change in kinetic energy.
  • I can explain how potential energy transforms into kinetic energy and thermal energy and explain why energy is conserved even when mechanical energy seems to “disappear.”

Key Vocabulary / Notation: conservation of energy, work-energy theorem, mechanical energy, thermal energy, energy transformation

Key Representations: energy bar charts/flow diagrams; before/after models; work and energy calculations

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Saying energy disappears when friction acts; treating conservation as constant kinetic energy; overlooking thermal transfer

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Explain stopping, falling, ramps, and mechanical systems through energy transfer and transformation

Recurring Practice Opportunities: conservation modeling; explanation of phenomena; evidence-based reasoning

Section 4.5: Energy in Society

Textbook Sections: 7.8

Learning Target: Students will evaluate energy sources, energy transformations, efficiency, and real-world energy decisions using conservation of energy.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that energy is not created or destroyed but can be transferred or transformed.
  • I can distinguish energy source, energy carrier, and energy transformation.
  • I can explain why useful energy often becomes thermal energy and compare energy sources using evidence such as availability, efficiency, environmental impact, and reliability.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: energy source, energy carrier, energy transformation, efficiency, reliability, environmental impact

Key Representations: energy flow diagrams; comparison tables; efficiency evidence; claims supported by data

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Calling electricity an energy source in every context; treating efficiency as energy conservation failure; comparing energy choices with one criterion only

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Evaluate real-world energy systems using multiple evidence criteria while preserving conservation of energy

Recurring Practice Opportunities: data evaluation; argument from evidence; systems reasoning

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Momentum and energy are system quantities that support before/after reasoning.
  • Impulse changes momentum; work transfers energy; power describes rate of energy transfer.
  • Conservation reasoning requires defining the system and tracking transfers or external influences.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: before/after system diagrams, momentum vectors, energy bars/flows, data tables, conservation and work/energy relationships

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: before/after system diagrams, momentum vectors, energy bars/flows, data tables, conservation and work/energy relationships

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving momentum, impulse, work, power, and mechanical-energy relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: before/after system diagrams, momentum vectors, energy bars/flows, data tables, conservation and work/energy relationships

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student identifies what evidence would test a claim or model, uses observations/data appropriately, and evaluates whether results support the explanation.

Representations / Contexts: before/after system diagrams, momentum vectors, energy bars/flows, data tables, conservation and work/energy relationships

Common Weaknesses: Treating one observation as proof; failing to control/identify relevant variables; reporting data without connecting it to the claim or model.

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, 4.4, 4.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: before/after system diagrams, momentum vectors, energy bars/flows, data tables, conservation and work/energy relationships

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U4-MG01

Mastery Goal: Relate momentum, impulse, force, and time to explain and quantify changes in motion.

Supporting Learning: Sections 4.1.

Recurring Practices: quantitative reasoning; data interpretation.

Key Representations: momentum vectors/data, $p=mv$, $J=Ft$, $J=\Delta p$.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence connects impulse to momentum change, includes direction where relevant, and interprets force-time tradeoffs.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Momentum=force; ignoring direction; treating impulse and force as identical.

U4-MG02

Mastery Goal: Use conservation of momentum to analyze collisions, explosions, and recoil after defining an appropriate system and considering external impulse.

Supporting Learning: Sections 4.2.

Recurring Practices: system reasoning; conservation modeling.

Key Representations: before/after diagrams, system boundaries, $p_{before}=p_{after}$.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence identifies the system, compares total momentum before/after, and states when external impulse limits the conservation model.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Conserving each object separately; ignoring external forces/impulse; confusing collision type with momentum conservation.

U4-MG03

Mastery Goal: Explain and calculate work, power, kinetic energy, and gravitational potential energy as connected descriptions of energy transfer and storage.

Supporting Learning: Sections 4.3.

Recurring Practices: quantitative reasoning; energy modeling.

Key Representations: work/energy diagrams, $W=Fd$, $P=W/t$, $KE$, $PE$.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence determines when work occurs, uses appropriate relationships, and distinguishes energy amount from power.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Any force means work; power=energy; ignoring direction/displacement.

U4-MG04

Mastery Goal: Use conservation of energy to explain transformations in mechanical and societal systems and evaluate real-world energy claims using evidence.

Supporting Learning: Sections 4.4, 4.5.

Recurring Practices: systems reasoning; argument from evidence.

Key Representations: energy bar/flow diagrams, before/after models, comparison data.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence tracks energy into kinetic, potential, thermal, or transferred forms and supports comparisons with more than one relevant criterion.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Energy “disappears”; mechanical energy mistaken for total energy; one-criterion energy-source claims.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 4 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U4-MG01, U4-MG02, U4-MG03, U4-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of before/after system diagrams, momentum vectors, energy bars/flows, data tables, conservation and work/energy relationships. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 4 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
