Conceptual Physics — Unit 7: Electricity and Circuits

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze electric charge, electric forces, voltage, current, resistance, power, energy, and circuits by modeling how charge interacts, how voltage drives charge flow, how resistance affects current, how electrical energy is transferred, and how series and parallel circuits behave in real-world systems.

Section Outline

Section 7.1: Electric Charge and Forces

Textbook Sections: 22.1–22.6

Learning Target: Students will explain electric charge, electric force, conservation of charge, conductors, insulators, and common charging processes.

I Can Statements

  • I can describe positive charge, negative charge, and neutral objects.
  • I can explain that like charges repel and opposite charges attract.
  • I can explain conservation of charge during charging interactions and distinguish conductors from insulators.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electric charge, positive, negative, neutral, conservation of charge, conductor, insulator, polarization, induction

Key Representations: charge diagrams; interaction sketches; Coulomb-style separation models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating neutral as no charges; saying charge is created during rubbing; confusing polarization with net charging

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Explain attraction/repulsion and charging by contact, polarization, and induction using conservation of charge

Recurring Practice Opportunities: particle/model reasoning; system conservation; explanation

Section 7.2: Electric Potential and Current

Textbook Sections: 22.9, 23.1, 23.2

Learning Target: Students will explain voltage as electric potential difference and current as the rate of charge flow in a complete circuit.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain electric potential energy in terms of separated charges.
  • I can describe voltage as energy per charge.
  • I can explain that current is the flow of electric charge and explain why a sustained current requires a voltage source and a complete path.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electric potential energy, voltage, electric potential difference, current, charge flow, circuit

Key Representations: $V=E/q$; $I=Q/t$; energy-per-charge models; complete-circuit diagrams

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Calling voltage “amount of current”; thinking current is used up; omitting the complete path or source

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Model how a voltage source and complete conducting path produce sustained charge flow

Recurring Practice Opportunities: systems reasoning; quantitative reasoning; explanation

Section 7.3: Resistance and Ohm’s Law

Textbook Sections: 23.3, 23.4

Learning Target: Students will use Ohm’s Law to explain how voltage, current, and resistance are related in simple circuits.

I Can Statements

  • I can define electrical resistance as opposition to charge flow.
  • I can explain how wire length, thickness, material, and temperature affect resistance.
  • I can use Ohm’s Law to calculate voltage, current, or resistance and predict how changing voltage affects current when resistance is constant.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: resistance, Ohm’s Law, voltage, current, conductor, resistivity factors

Key Representations: $V=IR$; current-voltage graphs; wire geometry/material comparisons

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating resistance as current; assuming current stays fixed when voltage changes; ignoring material/geometry effects

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Compare components and wires; use data or graphs to relate voltage, current, and resistance

Recurring Practice Opportunities: data analysis; quantitative reasoning; model evaluation

Section 7.4: Power and Electrical Energy

Textbook Sections: 23.5–23.7, 16.7

Learning Target: Students will explain electric power and electrical energy use in devices, homes, and energy systems.

I Can Statements

  • I can define electric power as the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.
  • I can calculate power using voltage and current.
  • I can calculate electrical energy use from power and time and compare energy use of household devices.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electric power, electrical energy, kilowatt-hour concept, voltage, current

Key Representations: $P=IV$; $E=Pt$; appliance labels; energy-use tables

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Confusing power with energy; comparing devices without time; treating higher power as automatically less efficient

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze household devices, power ratings, and energy use over time

Recurring Practice Opportunities: quantitative reasoning; data interpretation; evidence-based comparison

Section 7.5: Complex Electric Circuits

Textbook Sections: 23.9

Learning Target: Students will analyze series, parallel, and mixed circuits by tracing current paths, comparing voltage drops, and explaining how circuit changes affect brightness, current, resistance, and safety.

I Can Statements

  • I can distinguish series circuits from parallel circuits.
  • I can explain why a complete circuit is required for steady current.
  • I can trace current paths through simple and branched circuits and compare voltage and current in series and parallel circuits.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: series circuit, parallel circuit, branch, equivalent resistance, voltage drop, current path, short circuit

Key Representations: circuit schematics; current-path tracing; series/parallel comparison tables; equivalent-resistance relationships

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Thinking current chooses only one parallel branch; treating voltage/current rules as identical for series and parallel; failing to distinguish open and short circuits

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Model and troubleshoot simple series, parallel, and mixed circuits; connect design choices to brightness and safety

Recurring Practice Opportunities: systems modeling; diagram interpretation; troubleshooting/argument from evidence

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Charge interactions and conservation provide the particle-level basis for electrical phenomena.
  • Voltage, current, resistance, power, and energy describe different roles in an electric system.
  • Circuit behavior depends on complete paths and the topology of series, parallel, and mixed connections.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: charge distributions, force/interaction models, circuit schematics, I-V data/graphs, electrical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: charge distributions, force/interaction models, circuit schematics, I-V data/graphs, electrical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving electric force/potential/current, Ohm’s law, power/energy, and equivalent-resistance relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: charge distributions, force/interaction models, circuit schematics, I-V data/graphs, electrical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student identifies what evidence would test a claim or model, uses observations/data appropriately, and evaluates whether results support the explanation.

Representations / Contexts: charge distributions, force/interaction models, circuit schematics, I-V data/graphs, electrical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Treating one observation as proof; failing to control/identify relevant variables; reporting data without connecting it to the claim or model.

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: charge distributions, force/interaction models, circuit schematics, I-V data/graphs, electrical relationships

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U7-MG01

Mastery Goal: Explain charge interactions, conservation of charge, conductors/insulators, and charging processes with particle and interaction models.

Supporting Learning: Sections 7.1.

Recurring Practices: particle/model reasoning; system conservation.

Key Representations: charge distributions, force/interaction diagrams, conductor/insulator models.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence tracks charge without creating/destroying it and uses an appropriate model for attraction, repulsion, polarization, or transfer.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Neutral=no charges; charge created by rubbing; polarization=net charging.

U7-MG02

Mastery Goal: Explain voltage as energy per charge and current as charge flow in a complete circuit.

Supporting Learning: Sections 7.2.

Recurring Practices: systems reasoning; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: energy-per-charge models, $V=E/q$, $I=Q/t$, complete-circuit diagrams.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence distinguishes voltage from current and explains why a source plus complete path is needed for sustained current.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Voltage=current; current used up; open path overlooked.

U7-MG03

Mastery Goal: Relate voltage, current, resistance, power, and electrical energy quantitatively and interpret data or graphs describing components/devices.

Supporting Learning: Sections 7.3, 7.4.

Recurring Practices: data analysis; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: Ohm’s law, $P=IV$, $E=Pt$, I-V graphs, appliance data.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence selects and interprets the correct relationship, uses units consistently, and distinguishes power from energy over time.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Resistance=current; power=energy; proportional reasoning errors.

U7-MG04

Mastery Goal: Analyze series, parallel, and mixed circuits by tracing paths and explaining how changes affect current, voltage, equivalent resistance, brightness, and safety.

Supporting Learning: Sections 7.5 with 7.2–7.4.

Recurring Practices: systems modeling; troubleshooting; argument from evidence.

Key Representations: circuit schematics, branch/path tracing, equivalent resistance, comparison tables.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence follows current paths accurately, applies series/parallel relationships consistently, and explains a circuit change with the model rather than memorized slogans.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Current chooses one branch; series/parallel rules swapped; open/short confusion.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 7 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U7-MG01, U7-MG02, U7-MG03, U7-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of charge distributions, force/interaction models, circuit schematics, I-V data/graphs, electrical relationships. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 7 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
