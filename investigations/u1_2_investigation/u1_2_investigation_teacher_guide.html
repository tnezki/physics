Is Inertia a Force?

Coin-card phenomenon + teacher-selected PhET cases + force-model CER

Time35-45 minutes
Groups2-3 students
Core materialsCoin · card · cup · iPad

Purpose and Section 1.2 Alignment

This rebuild replaces the cart/Vernier investigation with a different physical phenomenon while preserving the exact Section 1.2 evidence scope. Students begin with an unresolved prediction, use the coin-card interaction as evidence, test their developing idea in a few teacher-selected PhET cases, and then confront the stationary-book counterexample before writing a CER.

  • I can state Newton's First Law in everyday language.
  • I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • I can distinguish between "no forces acting" and "no net force acting" and identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.
Boundary: Keep the lesson conceptual. Do not turn this into Section 1.3 vector addition or Section 1.4 formal equilibrium terminology. The useful representation here is a simple force model tied to actual interactions.

Materials

Per group:

  • 1 paper or plastic cup (avoid glass)
  • 1 index card or other stiff, smooth card
  • 1 coin or metal washer
  • 1 iPad with slow-motion video available; recording is optional, not required for every group
  • 1 book and desk/table for the counterexample
  • Access to the approved PhET link: https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/forces-and-motion-basics

Before Class

  1. Test the coin-card-cup setup. A stiff card that slides cleanly across the cup works better than a soft or highly textured card.
  2. Use a cup opening comfortably larger than the coin/washer. The task should reveal the interaction, not become a trick-shot challenge.
  3. Preview the live PhET simulation and choose two or three short cases that are visibly useful for the student table. Because the approved link does not lock a particular live interface, this guide intentionally does not prescribe button names or settings.
  4. Have a stationary book available for every group or plan to use one class demonstration.
Safety / setup: Use an unbreakable cup. Students should flick the card horizontally across the tabletop and away from faces. If a technique launches the coin, reset and reduce the flick angle rather than increasing force.

Expected Physical Pattern

During a slow pull, contact with the moving card can produce a horizontal frictional interaction on the coin for long enough to noticeably change the coin's horizontal motion. During a clean fast flick, the card is removed quickly; the coin tends to undergo much less horizontal change before the support disappears and gravity changes its vertical motion. Results will not be perfect: card texture, flick angle, coin shape, and technique all matter.

What matters: The investigation does not depend on the coin landing perfectly in the cup every time. Students need evidence about contact, interactions, and whether the coin's motion changes while those interactions act.

Force-Model Guidance

Before the card movesWeight downward; support force from the card upward.No "inertia" arrow. The coin can be at rest while forces are acting.
While card and coin remain in contact and the card moves sidewaysWeight; support; a horizontal frictional interaction from the card when the contact changes the coin's horizontal motion.The exact size is not needed. Tie every arrow to an interaction.
After the card no longer supports the coin, before it landsWeight downward; air resistance may be neglected for this lesson.The loss of support matters; inertia is still not a force.

PhET Use

The approved simulation is used only as a brief transfer check, not as a second full lab. Select a few live cases that let students compare motion, change in motion, and forces/interactions. Useful contrasts are cases in which motion is not changing and cases in which it is changing. If the current simulation version does not clearly support a planned contrast, do not invent a setting; choose a different visible case or skip that row and continue to the book counterexample.

For each case, press students with questions such as:

  • What is the object actually doing right now?
  • Is its motion changing, or staying the same?
  • What interactions can you identify?
  • What does the motion evidence suggest about the net force?

The Stationary-Book Twist

The book is the cleanest Section 1.2 counterexample to "not moving means no forces." A book resting on the desk has weight downward and a support force upward. Its motion is not changing even though forces are present. Students should articulate that no net force does not mean no forces.

Answer / Model Guidance

Student promptStrong response should include
Should inertia be a force arrow?No. Inertia describes an object's resistance to a change in motion; it is a property of matter, not an interaction force.
No forces vs. no net forceNo forces means no interactions are exerting forces on the object. No net force means forces may be present but their combined effect is zero, so the motion does not change.
Newton's First Law in everyday languageIf the net force is zero, an object keeps the same state of motion: it remains at rest or continues moving at constant velocity. A change in motion requires a nonzero net force.
CERReject the claim that inertia belongs as a force arrow; use specific observations from at least two sources and connect them to Newton's First Law, interactions, and net force.

Useful Press / Discussion Questions

  • What object is actually causing the force you drew?
  • If the coin keeps doing what it was already doing for a moment, what force would be required to explain that?
  • Why does the fast flick not need a hidden forward or backward "inertia force"?
  • How can the book have two forces acting on it without changing motion?
  • What evidence today would convince someone who says, "If it is not moving, there are no forces"?
  • What evidence today would convince someone who says, "Something must keep pushing an object for it to keep moving"?

Common Problems

  • Coin travels with the card on every fast trial: use a stiffer/smoother card, shorten the contact time, and keep the flick horizontal.
  • Coin launches sideways: students are striking upward or catching the coin. Reset the technique; do not turn it into a harder flick contest.
  • Students treat cup landing as the only evidence: redirect them to contact time, coin motion, and the interaction list.
  • Students draw "motion force" or "inertia force": ask, "What other object is exerting that force?"
  • Students say "momentum keeps it moving": keep the explanation in this section with Newton's First Law and inertia; momentum is not needed here.
  • PhET becomes a software lesson: keep the teacher-selected cases short. The simulation is evidence transfer, not the main phenomenon.

Connection Forward

Section 1.3 will formalize force direction and net-force representations. This investigation should leave students with the simpler foundation: forces come from interactions, inertia is not one of those forces, and motion changes when the net force is not zero.
