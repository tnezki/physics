Physics · Unit 1 · Section 1.2 · Investigation Teacher Guide

Is Inertia a Force?

Annotated student investigation · discourse moves · possible answers · force-model key

Time35-45 minutes
Groups2-3 students
Core materialsCoin · card · cup · iPad

Purpose and Section 1.2 Alignment

Students use a short hands-on inertia phenomenon, three specific PhET comparisons, and a stationary-book counterexample to build an evidence-based explanation of Newton's First Law. The activity stays inside Section 1.2: inertia is a property rather than a force; forces come from interactions; and zero net force is different from no forces.

  • I can state Newton's First Law in everyday language.
  • I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • I can distinguish between "no forces acting" and "no net force acting" and identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.
Boundary: Do not turn this into Section 1.3 vector addition or Section 1.4 formal equilibrium terminology. Arrow direction and relative balance can be discussed qualitatively, but the instructional goal is the force-motion relationship.

Materials and Prep

  • Per group: unbreakable cup, stiff index card, coin or washer, iPad if recording slow motion.
  • Book and desk/table for the counterexample.
  • Approved simulation: PhET: Forces and Motion: Basics.
  1. Test the coin-card setup. A stiff, smooth card should slide cleanly across the cup.
  2. Plan to model one clean horizontal flick. Students do not need written software directions for slow motion.
  3. For PhET, use the three cases printed on the student page: balanced at rest, unbalanced forces, and moving after the push ends in a no-friction condition.
Safety: Use an unbreakable cup. Flick the card horizontally across the tabletop and away from faces. If the coin launches, reduce the upward angle rather than increasing the force.

Student Page 1 · Predict and Test

Part 1 · Predict — Coin, Card, Cup

Student sees: Place a card across a cup and a coin near the center. Predict what the coin will do if the card is pulled slowly and if it is flicked quickly. Then decide whether you currently think inertia is a force acting on the coin.

Discourse moves
Have partners commit before testing. Ask: "What do you think will actually touch or pull on the coin?" Then: "If you say a force keeps the coin where it is, what object is providing that force?" Accept competing claims without resolving them.
Possible responses
Slow pull: students may predict the coin travels with the card, tips, or eventually falls. Fast flick: students may predict the coin stays nearly over the cup and falls in, or that it travels with the card. Initial inertia claim: any well-stated prediction is acceptable here. A common misconception is "yes, inertia is a force that holds it in place." Do not correct it yet.

Part 2 · Test — Collect Observations

Student procedure: one slow pull, three fast horizontal flicks, and optional slow-motion video. Students record what the card does, what the coin does, and one key observation for each trial.

Discourse moves
After the slow pull, ask groups to describe only what they observed before explaining it. After the first fast trial, ask: "What changed first - the card or the coin?" and "For how long were the card and coin interacting?" Invite groups with imperfect trials to compare useful evidence rather than rerun until they get a trick-shot result.
Student rowPossible observation / answer
Slow pullThe card moves sideways and the coin tends to move sideways with it while contact lasts. Depending on technique, the coin may move away from the cup instead of dropping through.
Fast trialsThe card moves sideways quickly while the coin changes horizontal motion much less. Once the card is no longer supporting it, the coin moves downward toward the cup.
Key evidenceThe fast flick greatly shortens the sideways interaction between card and coin. The coin does not need a sideways "inertia force" to remain nearly where it was horizontally for that brief interval.
Watch for: Students may treat "coin fell in cup" as the entire evidence. Redirect to the order of events, duration of contact, and which interactions were actually present.
Student Page 2 · Explain the Puzzle

Part 3 · Examine — What Interacted With the Coin?

Students describe the coin before the card moves, while card and coin are in contact, and after the card no longer supports the coin. They then draw force models for two moments and decide whether inertia belongs as a force arrow.

Discourse moves
Use the repeated press: "What object causes that force?" If a student says "motion force," "inertia force," or "the coin wants to stay," ask them to identify the second object in the interaction. Revoice productive language: "So you are saying the coin keeps its motion unless an interaction changes it."
MomentPossible student description
Before the card movesCoin is at rest. Earth pulls downward; the card supports upward.
While card and coin contactEarth pulls downward, card supports upward, and the card can exert a horizontal friction force on the coin while surfaces interact.
After support is goneThe card no longer exerts support or friction. The Earth still pulls downward; air resistance can be ignored here.

What should NOT appear

Possible answer - Should inertia appear as a force arrow?
No. Inertia is a property of matter: the tendency to resist a change in motion. It is not an interaction between the coin and another object, so there is no "inertia force" arrow.
Possible answer - Slow pull vs. fast flick
During the slow pull, the card remains in contact long enough for friction from the card to change the coin's horizontal motion noticeably. During the fast flick, that horizontal interaction lasts for much less time, so the coin's horizontal motion changes much less before the card is gone.
Student Page 3 · Three PhET Cases + Book Counterexample

Part 4 · Transfer — Three PhET Cases

Case A - Balanced at rest: equal-strength pushes/pulls in opposite directions.

Case B - Unbalanced: make one side stronger than the other.

Case C - Moving after the push ends: in a no-friction condition, get the object moving, then remove the applied push.

Students record what the object does, whether its motion changes, and whether net force is zero or nonzero.

Discourse moves
Run each case long enough for students to commit to a description before discussing. For Case A ask: "Are there forces, even though nothing changes?" For Case B ask: "What is different about the combined effect?" For Case C ask: "The push is gone - what evidence says the object does or does not need a continuing forward force?"
CasePossible observationNet-force interpretationSection 1.2 idea
A · Balanced at restObject remains at rest while opposite forces act.Zero net horizontal force.Zero net force does not mean no forces.
B · UnbalancedMotion changes toward the stronger side.Nonzero net force.A change in motion is associated with nonzero net force.
C · Moving after push endsWith no friction and applied push removed, the object continues moving rather than instantly stopping.Zero net horizontal force after the push is removed.Continuing motion does not require a continuing forward force.
Possible pattern statement
When the net force is nonzero, motion changes. When the net force is zero, the object's motion stays the same: if it is at rest it remains at rest, and if it is moving it keeps moving at constant velocity.

Part 5 · Counterexample — A Book Sitting on the Desk

Students choose between: A. No forces act on the book; or B. Forces can act even though its motion is not changing. They explain, draw a force model, and distinguish no forces from no net force.

Discourse moves
Before revealing the force model, ask for a silent vote A/B. Then ask one student from each side to explain. Press with: "If Earth still pulls on the book, why does it not fall through the desk?" and "If the desk pushes up, why does the book not launch upward?"

Possible book force model

Possible answers
Choice: B. The book has weight downward and support force upward. Its motion is not changing because the forces have zero combined effect. No forces vs. no net force: "No forces" means there are no force interactions acting on the object. "No net force" means forces may be present but cancel in their combined effect.
Student Page 4 · Build the Explanation

Part 6 · Synthesize — Newton's First Law + CER

Students state Newton's First Law in everyday language, then support or reject: "Inertia should be drawn as a force acting on an object." Evidence must come from at least two sources: coin/card, PhET, or book.

Discourse moves before writing
Use a 60-second partner rehearsal: one student states a claim and one piece of evidence; the partner asks, "How does that evidence support your claim?" Switch roles. During whole-class share, separate evidence from reasoning explicitly: "That is an observation. What physics idea connects it to your claim?"
Possible Newton's First Law statement
If the net force on an object is zero, its motion does not change: an object at rest stays at rest, and a moving object continues at constant velocity. A nonzero net force is needed to change motion.
Possible CER
Claim: Reject. Inertia should not be drawn as a force.
Evidence: In the fast coin-card trial, the coin changed its horizontal motion very little after the card was removed quickly; once the card was gone, there was no horizontal "inertia" interaction to draw. In PhET Case C, the object continued moving after the applied push was removed in the no-friction condition. The book also had real forces acting even while its motion stayed unchanged.
Reasoning: Forces come from interactions with other objects. Inertia is a property of matter, not an interaction. Newton's First Law says motion remains unchanged when net force is zero; a force is required to change motion, not to maintain motion.

Closing Discussion - possible answers and moves

Common Problems and Teacher Responses

  • Coin travels with the card on every fast trial: use a stiffer/smoother card and keep the flick horizontal.
  • Coin launches sideways: reduce upward angle; do not make it a harder-flick contest.
  • Students treat cup landing as the evidence: redirect to contact, interaction time, and change in motion.
  • Students draw "motion force" or "inertia force": ask what other object exerts it.
  • Students say "momentum keeps it moving": stay with Newton's First Law and inertia in this section.
  • Students think balanced means no forces: return to the book and PhET Case A.

Connection Forward

Section 1.3 will formalize force direction and net-force representations. This investigation should leave students with the simpler foundation: forces come from interactions, inertia is not a force, zero net force can occur while forces are present, and motion changes when net force is nonzero.
