Does Motion Need a Continuing Force?

Use a cart and Vernier motion data to test an everyday idea about force and motion.

Learning target: Explain Newton's First Law and use inertia to predict whether an object's motion will stay the same or change.
Research Question: After a brief push ends, what does the cart's motion tell us about force, net force, and inertia?

1. Make a Prediction

A cart receives a short push and then the hand loses contact with it. What do you predict will happen to the cart's velocity immediately after the push ends? Explain what you think is causing that motion.

Choose the claim closest to your current thinking:

2. Set Up Vernier Graphical Analysis

Equipment: dynamics cart, level track or smooth tabletop, Vernier motion detector, device running Vernier Graphical Analysis, and a rough-surface strip (felt, towel, or similar).
1
Place the motion detector at one end of the track. Start the cart far enough away that the detector can read it reliably.
2
Display a velocity-versus-time graph. Use a time range long enough to see the push, release, and several seconds of motion afterward.
3
Practice one gentle push away from the detector. Release the cart completely after the brief push.

4. Run B - Change the Push, Not the Idea

Repeat the run with a noticeably different initial push. Your goal is not to hit a particular speed; it is to see whether the same pattern occurs after contact ends.

What feature of the two graphs is similar after the push ends? What feature is different?

6. Forces Are Still Acting

Choose the cart during a smooth post-push interval. Draw and label the important forces acting on it. Include vertical forces even though the cart is moving horizontally.

Explain the difference between "no forces acting" and "no net force acting."
