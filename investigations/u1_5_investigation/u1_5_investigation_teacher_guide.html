Physics · Unit 1 · Section 1.5 · Investigation Teacher Guide

Force Model Poster + Peer Presentation

Unit synthesis through individual modeling, critique, revision, and short peer explanation

Prep30 minutes
Presentation~3 minutes per presenting pair
Student productOne 8.5 × 11 poster per student

Purpose

This replaces the previous station-based U1.5 investigation with a synthesis task aligned directly to the three section outcomes. Students must make a force diagram for a real situation, decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced, connect net force to motion, and critique/revise incorrect force reasoning using physics vocabulary.

Student Outcomes

  • I can draw a force diagram for an object in a real-world situation.
  • I can decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.
  • I can explain how the net force affects the object's motion and critique an incorrect force explanation and revise it using physics vocabulary.

Materials

  • One blank 8.5 × 11 sheet per student, or one digital page in Notability
  • Pencils/markers or stylus
  • Student assignment page projected or distributed
  • Optional: notes/practice resources for reference during the 30-minute build

Suggested Flow

MinutesStudent WorkTeacher Focus
0-5Choose a real-world situation and one object to model.Reject vague systems; require one clearly identified object.
5-18Build the force diagram and decide balanced/unbalanced.Ask: “What interaction causes that arrow?” and “Do arrow sizes support your net-force claim?”
18-25Explain motion; write, critique, and revise an incorrect explanation.Push vocabulary precision and separate velocity from acceleration.
25-30Finish poster and rehearse.Check for readable visuals and complete reasoning.
30+Students form pairs; each pair presents to another pair.Each presenting pair gets ~3 minutes, then the two pairs switch roles.

Presentation Structure

Students prepare posters individually, then join one partner. Pair A presents its two posters to Pair B (two listeners) for about three minutes: roughly 75 seconds per student plus a final 30-second joint critique/revision highlight. Pair B gives one brief feedback statement, then the pairs switch. One matched set of two pairs therefore takes about 6-7 minutes.

Poster Requirements

Required elementWhat counts as strong evidence
Real-world force diagramOne chosen object; only forces acting on that object; correct directions; reasonable relative arrow lengths; meaningful labels.
Balanced/unbalanced decisionDecision matches the diagram; net force is zero or has a justified direction.
Net force → motionZero net force is connected to no acceleration/constant velocity; nonzero net force is connected to acceleration in the net-force direction.
Critique + revisionA believable misconception is identified precisely and rewritten with correct vocabulary and causal reasoning.
Do not accept “moving means unbalanced” as a complete explanation. Students should distinguish motion from a change in motion. An object can move at constant velocity while the net force is zero.

Useful Incorrect Explanations Students Might Critique

  • “The box is moving right, so the rightward force must be bigger.”
  • “The book is at rest, so no forces act on it.”
  • “Inertia is a force that keeps the cart moving.”
  • “If two forces act on an object, they are automatically balanced.”
  • “A scale reading is the net force.”

Students may use other misconceptions, but the error should be plausible and the revision should explain why it is wrong rather than merely replacing one sentence with a correct statement.

Fast Teacher Check

A quick four-part completion check can be used while circulating: diagram → balance/net force → motion → critique/revision. If any one part contradicts another, ask the student to repair the model before spending time on poster decoration.

Optional Scoring

If you want a lightweight score, use 8 points: 2 points each for (1) force diagram, (2) balanced/unbalanced + net-force reasoning, (3) motion explanation, and (4) critique/revision. Treat visual polish as communication quality rather than a separate art grade.

Closure

After both pairs have presented, ask for two or three examples of force explanations that were improved during the activity. Use the class discussion to reinforce the distinction between velocity and acceleration and the idea that a force diagram must represent interactions, not motion itself.
