Can Your Force Model Survive the Evidence?

Evidence-based force-model stations · new synthesis investigation

Time25-30 minutes
Groups2-3 students
Core ideaModel ↔ evidence ↔ motion

Why This Investigation Fits Section 1.5

The current final section is not asking students for another isolated force calculation. It asks them to create and critique models that combine the unit's ideas: inertia, support force, weight, tension, vectors, net force, equilibrium, and motion. This station sequence makes the model answer to real evidence instead of becoming a drawing exercise.

Materials

  • Books/textbooks and level tables
  • Hanging masses or safe small objects
  • Spring scales
  • Dynamics cart + Vernier motion detector + Graphical Analysis
  • Optional bathroom scale for an additional support-force example
  • Student investigation in Notability

Room Setup

Stations 1 and 2 can be duplicated cheaply. Station 3 needs the Vernier setup; if sensors are limited, use one projected cart graph collected live by the teacher and let all groups analyze it. Station 4 is paper-based and can serve as the holding station while students wait for equipment.

Suggested Flow

MinutesStudent WorkTeacher Focus
0-3Review five-part model-check routineEmphasize one chosen object and real interactions.
3-18Rotate through Stations 1-4Ask “What causes that arrow?” and “Does net force agree with motion?”

Station Expectations

StationExpected Reasoning
1. Book on tableUntouched book: weight down and support up, balanced. During a gentle downward press while still at rest, the table's support force increases so it can balance both weight and the added downward hand force.
2. Hanging objectTension up and weight down. At rest, they balance; the scale reading represents tension, not net force.
3. Released cartNo continuing hand force after release. Weight/support remain. Horizontal resistance may be small but real; graph evidence determines whether net horizontal force is near zero or clearly nonzero.
4. Competing modelsModel B (30 left, 30 right) matches constant straight-line speed because net force is zero. Model A has 25 N net left and therefore requires changing motion, not unchanged constant speed.
Important: Do not let students draw “motion arrows” inside the force diagram unless they are explicitly separated and labeled as motion information. A force arrow needs an interaction source.

Strong Final Models

A strong model identifies one object, includes only forces acting on it, uses reasonable vector directions and relative lengths, states whether forces are balanced, and explains why the net-force conclusion agrees with the observed motion. The deliberate-error prompt should name a specific bad revision: add an unsupported “inertia force,” remove the support force, make one balanced arrow longer, reverse a tension direction, and so on.

Common Misconceptions to Surface

  • Inertia as a force.
  • Support force omitted because it is invisible.
  • A scale reading called “net force.”
  • A forward force added only because an object moves forward.
  • Equal arrow lengths drawn automatically without considering evidence.
  • Correct-looking force diagram paired with an incompatible motion description.

Optional Extension

Ask groups to trade their final classroom-object models. The receiving group must identify one question or measurement that would test whether the model is complete. This raises the task from “draw a model” to “defend a model with evidence” without adding new physics content.

Unit 1 Closure

The final discussion can use one recurring sentence stem: “The model is believable because…” Require students to finish with a specific interaction, vector relationship, net-force statement, and motion observation.
