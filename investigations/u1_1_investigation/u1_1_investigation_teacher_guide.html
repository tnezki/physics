Bouncy Ball Evidence & Models

Full-period scientific investigation · adapted from the legacy Bouncy Ball Experiment

Time35-40 minutes
Groups2-3 students
Core toolsVideo Analysis + Graphical Analysis

Why This Investigation Fits Section 1.1

The legacy activity already used a bouncy ball to connect data collection, regression, prediction, and scientific method. This revision keeps that strong experimental core but removes acceleration and energy analysis that occur later in the current course. The new emphasis matches Section 1.1: direct measurement, observation versus inference, evidence, testable claims, mathematical models, prediction, and revision.

Learning Target Connection

  • Students make a testable claim before collecting data.
  • Students distinguish measured quantities from inferences about the relationship.
  • Students use a graph and linear fit as a model rather than treating the equation as an answer-producing trick.
  • Students test the model with a new measurement and decide whether the evidence supports or challenges it.

Materials Per Group

  • 1 bouncy ball or racquetball; use the same ball for all trials in a group
  • 1 meter stick or metric scale reference
  • Phone/iPad for recording
  • Vernier Video Analysis or the class-approved video measurement workflow
  • Vernier Graphical Analysis for the scatter plot and linear fit
  • Hard, level floor
  • Student investigation downloaded into Notability before class

Before Class

  1. Open the student file on a device and confirm the workspace sizes are comfortable in Notability. Every response box uses an inline --ws height that can be edited directly in the HTML.
  2. Test one video setup. The camera should be perpendicular to the ball path with the scale reference in the same plane as the motion.
  3. Confirm students can enter a manual data table and create a linear fit in Vernier Graphical Analysis.
  4. Choose a safe maximum drop height based on the room and camera framing. The old activity used roughly 0.5-2.0 m; use the range that works in your space.

Suggested 40-Minute Flow

MinutesStudent WorkTeacher Focus
0-5Research question, initial claim, evidence that would challenge itPush for a claim that can actually fail. “Greater drop height gives greater rebound height” is testable; “the ball behaves normally” is not.
5-9Variables, setup, practice dropCheck camera geometry and measurement reference. Insist on measuring the center of the ball consistently.
9-225-6 recorded drops and height measurementsPrioritize usable evidence over rushing. If a trial is poor, students should mark it rather than hide the problem.
22-29Enter data in Graphical Analysis, make scatter plot, apply fitAsk students what the graph means before asking for the equation.

34-40CER and revision/limitationLook for evidence-based revision, not a ceremonial conclusion.

Expected Pattern

For a consistent ball and surface, rebound height should generally increase as drop height increases. The legacy activity expected an approximately linear relationship with a positive slope and an intercept near zero. Typical slope values in that activity were roughly 0.40-0.70, but the exact value depends on the ball, surface, release technique, and measurement quality.

Do not turn the slope into an energy lesson here. The old version asked where “missing energy” went and included acceleration calculations. Those ideas are intentionally removed because the current Section 1.1 target is scientific reasoning and modeling.

Strong Student Responses to Listen For

PromptEvidence of Strong Reasoning
What would challenge the initial claim?A specific measurable pattern: rebound height staying constant, decreasing as drop height increases, or data showing no consistent trend.
Observation vs inferenceObservation names a measured height or visible pattern. Inference interprets what the pattern means about the relationship.
Why use the equation?The equation is a compact model of the measured pattern and can generate a prediction that is checked with new evidence.
Why is the model imperfect?Measurement uncertainty, inconsistent release, camera angle/parallax, ball spin, floor variation, limited data range, or a relationship that is only approximately linear.

Common Problems & Fixes

  • Camera moves: brace it against a desk, stand, or stable object.
  • Scale and ball are in different planes: move the meter stick next to the ball path to reduce parallax.
  • Students chase a perfect fit: remind them that unexpected variation is evidence to analyze, not data to erase.
  • Graph made with axes reversed: drop height is the input/change-on-purpose variable and belongs on the horizontal axis.
  • Students call the fitted line “the truth”: ask what future measurement could make them revise it.

Optional Shortening to 25-30 Minutes

Use four drop heights instead of six, skip the second control-variable response, and make the model-check drop a teacher-collected class data point. Keep the initial claim, Graphical Analysis model, observation/inference distinction, and final CER; those are the pieces most tightly aligned to Section 1.1.

Connection to the Next Section

Do not pre-teach force explanations during this investigation. If students ask why the ball changes motion at the floor, record the question and use it as a bridge into Section 1.2: Forces and Newton's First Law.
