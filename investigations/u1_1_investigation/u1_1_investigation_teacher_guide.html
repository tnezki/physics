Bouncy Ball Relationships

Simple measurement → graph → Desmos model → CER

Time35-40 min
GroupingPairs
MaterialsBall, meter stick, iPad

Purpose

Students practice making a testable claim, collecting measurements, using a formula as a model of a relationship, and writing a short CER.

Section 1.1 connection: Physics formulas are compact relationships between physical quantities. Students should read what the variables mean and use the equation to describe the pattern, not treat it only as a calculator shortcut.

Materials Per Pair

  • 1 bouncy ball
  • 1 meter stick
  • 1 iPad with slow-motion video
  • Hard, level floor
  • Desmos

Before Class

  1. Choose a clear wall/floor area where a meter stick can stand vertically beside the bounce.
  2. Test one slow-motion recording so the full ball path and meter stick are visible.
  3. Confirm Desmos is available. Students use its regression/model menu rather than typing regression syntax.
  4. Print the three-page student investigation.

Class Flow

Suggested Flow

TimeStudent WorkTeacher Move
0-5Initial claimStudents choose a possible shape: linear, quadratic, absolute value, exponential, radical, or other. The claim only needs to be testable.
5-18Five slow-motion dropsSame ball, same floor, no downward push, meter stick beside the bounce.
18-25Plot dataAsk what the graph appears to show before opening Desmos.
25-31Desmos modelStudents start with the model type they predicted, then test another if needed.
31-40CER + discussionPress for measured evidence and a clear connection between the graph and formula.

Measurement Method

  1. Meter stick stands vertically beside the bounce path.
  2. One student records in slow motion while the other drops the ball.
  3. Use drop heights of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 cm.
  4. Replay and pause at the highest point after the first bounce.
  5. Read the rebound height using the center of the ball.
Do not add Vernier or formal video tracking. The point is simple measurement and scientific reasoning, not learning another analysis tool.

Desmos

Students enter the five points in a table and use the regression/model menu. They should begin with the model type from their claim, then compare with another model if needed. Do not require regression syntax.

Evidence Guide

Expected Pattern

With the same ball and surface, rebound height should generally increase as drop height increases. Over this limited range, the relationship is often approximately linear, though student data will not be perfect.

Useful teacher move: Do not tell students the model is linear before they graph. Let the measurements and model comparison do that work.

What to Listen For

PartStrong Student Thinking
Initial claimNames a possible relationship and gives a reason. The claim can be wrong; it just needs to be testable.
GraphDrop height is x. Rebound height is y. Student describes the overall pattern rather than one point.
FormulaStudent can say what x and y represent and explain how the equation summarizes the measured relationship.
ClaimNames the relationship supported by the final data/model.
EvidenceUses specific measurements, the graph pattern, or the Desmos model.
ReasoningExplains why the evidence supports the claim and connects the formula to the physical relationship.

CER Emphasis

Claim: What relationship is supported?

Evidence: What measurements or model features support it?

Reasoning: Why does that evidence make the claim reasonable?

Discussion and Troubleshooting

Common Problems

  • Ball leaves the frame: move the iPad farther back.
  • Hard to read height: keep the camera level and meter stick close to the ball path.
  • Ball is pushed: repeat the trial and release without adding force.
  • Axes reversed: drop height is what students choose, so it belongs on x.
  • Students chase a perfect fit: a model summarizes a pattern; experimental points can vary.

Discussion Question Notes

  1. Original claim: Either answer is fine if it refers to the data.
  2. Which model: Students should refer to graph shape and/or model fit.
  3. What does the formula tell you: Look for how rebound height changes with drop height.
  4. Why not perfect: Measurement uncertainty, camera angle, release variation, ball spin, floor variation.
  5. Why groups differ: Different balls, surfaces, measurement choices, or release technique.
  6. Predict an untested height: Use the fitted formula as a model; predictions far outside the measured range are less trustworthy.

Whole-Class Close

Ask: “What did the formula add that the table did not?” A useful answer: it summarizes the pattern in one relationship and can make predictions.

Then ask: “Does a formula prove the model is true?” No. The formula is a model supported by measurements and can be revised when new evidence does not fit.

Keep the level simple. The main wins are a testable claim, usable measurements, a relationship shown in a formula, and a CER that points back to evidence.
