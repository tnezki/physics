Physics - Unit 5 - Section 5 - Investigation

Tune by Beats

Use beat rate as measured evidence for the frequency difference between two sound sources.

Learning target: Students will analyze sound interference and beats using wave superposition and frequency differences.
Research Question: How accurately does the observed beat rate match the difference between two tuning-fork frequencies?

1. Make a Prediction

For two close frequencies, predict which pair will make faster beats: a pair 2 Hz apart or a pair 6 Hz apart. Explain before listening.

2. Set Up

Equipment: at least three tuning forks with nearby labeled frequencies, rubber strikers, stopwatch, quiet room/area; optional additional fork pair for mystery challenge
1
Choose a close-frequency pair. Use fbeat = |f1 − f2| to predict the beat frequency before sounding the forks.
2
Strike the two forks with similar strength and hold them near each other without touching. Count loud pulses for 10 s. Repeat once and average the count.
3
Convert the count to observed beats per second. Compare with the predicted beat frequency.
4
Repeat with at least two more frequency pairs. Keep listening position and timing method as consistent as practical.
5
Use your results to explain why beats slow down as two sources are tuned closer together, and why equal frequencies would produce zero beat frequency.

4. Analyze the Evidence

1. Which fork pair produced the fastest beats? Does that agree with its frequency difference?

2. For each pair, compare observed beat rate with |f1 − f2|. What is the strongest evidence that the relationship works?

3. List two reasons human beat counting may differ from the labeled-frequency prediction.

4. Explain why beats are not the same as permanent destructive interference.

5. Connect beat tuning to noise cancellation: what extra phase/timing requirement is needed for a canceling signal?

5. Defend or Revise Your Model

Write a short claim-evidence-reasoning response explaining how musicians can use beat rate to judge whether two notes are approaching the same frequency.

Use your group’s actual observations and measurements. Classroom data may vary; explain variation instead of forcing a perfect result.
