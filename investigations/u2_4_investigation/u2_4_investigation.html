How Far in Each Interval?

Free fall and air resistance • Two videos • Approximately 35–45 minutes

Name: Class: __________
I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.

Question: Does an object fall the same distance in each equal time interval? Does a cotton ball show the same pattern as a dense ball?

1. Record Both Drops 5–10 min

Materials: iPad, meter stick, Vernier Video Analysis. Your teacher will release a dense ball and a cotton ball.
  1. Record your teacher dropping the dense ball from rest. Record again for the cotton ball.
  2. Film from the side. Keep your iPad still and the meter stick visible. Capture the entire fall.
  3. Save two separate videos. Stay clear of the drop area.

2. Track the Dense Ball 10–12 min

  1. Import the ball video into Vernier Video Analysis. Set the distance scale with the meter stick. Set the origin at the release point and +Y downward.
  2. Open Settings (gear) → Advanced Video Options → Advance Frames = 5.
  3. Find the frame when the ball is released. Choose Add and mark the center of the ball. Continue marking the same spot at each five-frame advance.
  4. Copy your actual times and Y positions from Vernier. For each new row, subtract the previous position to find the distance in that interval.

What About a Cotton Ball?

Repeat the same five-frame method with the cotton ball video.

3. Track the Cotton Ball 8–12 min

  1. Import your cotton ball video. Set the scale with the meter stick. Set the origin at release and +Y downward.
  2. Set Advanced Video Options → Advance Frames = 5 again.
  3. Start at release. Mark the center of the cotton ball at each five-frame advance, keeping track of the same point even if it drifts sideways.
  4. Record actual Vernier times and vertical (Y) distances below. Subtract neighboring positions to find each interval distance.
Compare fairly: Within each video, five frames is the same time step from point to point. If the two videos have different frame rates, their time steps may be different—compare the patterns, not the raw interval sizes across objects.

What Do the Spacings Show?

Use numbers from your tables and the drawings you created.

Remember: The circles represent positions at equal frame intervals. Larger gaps mean more distance traveled in the same amount of time. Gravity still acts even when air resistance is important.

4. Explain Your Evidence 8–12 min

1. Give the first and last measured interval distances for the ball. How did they change?

2. Look at your ball diagram. What do the gaps between circles tell you about the ball's speed?

3. Compare two interval distances for the cotton ball. Did its distance pattern match the ball's? Explain what you saw.

4. Which of your two motion diagrams is more like free fall? Support your choice with one observation or number.

5. If an object fell at an almost constant speed, how would the gaps in its motion diagram look?

6. Gravity pulls on both objects. Why might the cotton ball fall differently than the dense ball?
