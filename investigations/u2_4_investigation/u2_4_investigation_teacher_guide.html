How Far in Each Interval?

Teacher-controlled drops • Students record two videos • Vernier Video Analysis • 35–45 minutes

Target: I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.
Focus: Students measure distance traveled over equal five-frame steps, then draw position-by-position motion diagrams.
TeacherRelease dense ball and cotton ball from safe height.
StudentsIndividually record two videos; analyze on iPads.
EvidenceActual times, Y positions, interval distances, two drawings, six questions.

Prepare the Drops

  • Choose a safe teacher-only release from approximately 1.5–2.0 m above floor. Students stay on floor and out of drop path. Catch or safely stop dense ball if needed; no climbing.
  • Use a small dense ball and a loose cotton ball. Release each from rest, without pushing. Repeat each drop if several students need a clean recording.
  • Put a vertical meter stick near the falling path, in the same plane as the objects. Film side-on with stable devices and high-contrast background.
  • Prefer 60 fps normal-speed video if students can select it. A 2 m ball drop lasts only about 0.64 s. Five-frame steps at 60 fps are about 0.083 s, allowing several usable points. At 30 fps, five-frame steps are about 0.167 s; students may obtain fewer than six points.
  • Students can each record their own two short clips of your drops. They should begin recording before release, capture landing, and keep the meter stick in frame. Use normal video time from Vernier, not slow-motion playback time.

Vernier Walkthrough — 5-Frame Advances

  1. Open Vernier Video Analysis and import the clip. Set the distance scale using the meter stick.
  2. Set origin at release; select the vertical axis so +Y points down. Then gear icon → Advanced Video Options → Advance Frames → 5 frames.
  3. Go to a frame at/just after release. Choose Add. Mark the same center point of the object on each five-frame advance, before impact.
  4. Read each point’s actual time (s) and Y position (m). The first time might not show 0.00 s; that is fine. Record successive rows and find interval distance = current Y − previous Y.
  5. Draw a circle for each recorded position. Label with its recorded time. Put larger vertical gaps on the sketch when the measured interval distance is larger; exact drawing scale is not required.
Important: Five frames produces equal time steps within a constant-frame-rate clip. Different cameras or clips may have different seconds per five frames. Students compare the pattern across successive intervals within each video. Do not require predetermined time stamps or cross-video distance totals.

What Patterns Are Reasonable?

  • Dense ball: This is the closer approximation to free fall. Its vertical distance per equal interval usually increases. Tracking noise or a weak camera angle can slightly disturb the pattern.
  • Cotton ball: Its low weight relative to drag makes air resistance important. It may initially speed up, then show less growth in interval distances, near-equal intervals, or uneven gaps as it drifts. Do not require evidence of exact terminal velocity.
  • Time labels: Use actual Vernier times. Data starting at 1.37 s or 0.04 s can be completely correct if the intervals come from the video frames.
  • Fewer than six rows: If the ball lands early, students label remaining rows N/A. Four well-spaced tracked points are enough to compare three intervals; repeating the drop or using 60 fps may help.

Responses to the Six Questions

QExpected reasoning
1Ball interval distances usually increase from first to last; accept actual data and note obvious measurement noise.
2Increasing gaps on a fixed-time-step motion diagram mean increasing speed downward.
3Cotton ball interval distances often increase less, become similar, or vary due to flutter. Evidence must match the student's readings.
4Dense ball better approximates free fall; support with enlarging gaps and/or interval-distance trend.
5Constant speed would make roughly equal gaps over equal intervals.
6Gravity still pulls both downward. Upward air resistance is a larger fraction of the cotton ball's weight and changes its acceleration.

Quick Troubleshooting / Safety

  • App reports upward as negative: Reorient +Y down or consistently work with downward distance relative to release. Do not mix sign conventions in one table.
  • Fuzzy cotton ball: Use a dark background, mark its center consistently, and avoid touching/tracking after impact.
  • Short drop / 30 fps: Six points may be impossible with 5-frame advances. Allow N/A rather than guessing positions. Longer safe drop or 60-fps camera helps.
  • Recording the teacher: Students stand outside the marked landing area; keep the meter stick and iPads clear of the falling object.
Do not overclaim: A cotton ball can still accelerate even with significant air resistance; a shorter demonstration may not show stable terminal speed. The conceptual goal is less regular or less rapidly growing interval distances compared with a dense ball.
