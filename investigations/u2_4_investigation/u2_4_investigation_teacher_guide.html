How Far in Each Interval?

Student-recorded ball + coffee filter • Vernier Video Analysis • 35–45 minutes

Target: I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.
Research question: Does distance per equal interval grow the same way for a ball and a coffee filter?
EquipmentDense rubber ball, open coffee filter, meter stick, iPad, safe drop zone
SoftwareVernier Video Analysis (not Graphical Analysis); student-recorded clips
Student evidence2 short videos, two six-row tables, interval changes, 6 questions

Before Class — Crucial Setup

  • Test a side-on 1.5–2.0 m drop with a ball and one open coffee filter. Keep both fully visible. Use a stable, fixed camera and a meter stick in the same plane as the falling objects. Use a high-contrast background and ordinary video at 30 fps or higher.
  • Keep students on the floor; no chairs/desks. Use a nonbreakable ball and clear drop zone. Mark release height in advance.
  • Ball starts from rest and usually lands in about 0.55–0.65 s, allowing 0.10 s readings through 0.5 s. An open coffee filter often falls more slowly, allowing 0.20 s readings through 1.0 s.
  • Have one practice clip ready in case a group’s video is blurry. Do not require calculation of g or an acceleration fit.

Student Workflow (Suggested)

TimeWhat happens
0–5 minPredict: do distances in equal intervals grow, stay the same, or shrink?
5–13 minStudents film both drops from the same height. Keep camera still.
13–23 minVernier: scale/origin, track ball at 0.10 s, record six positions, subtract intervals.
23–34 minTrack filter at 0.20 s; record six positions and subtract intervals.
34–45 minCompare trends and answer six evidence questions; brief whole-class debrief.

Quick Vernier Coaching

  1. Open videoanalysis.app, choose Import Video, then System → Scale; use a known length on the visible meter stick. Use System → Origin to place (0,0) at the release point and orient +Y down.
  2. Select the release frame. Add a point at the same feature of the object in each chosen frame. Vernier uses the first marked frame as t = 0; no need to reset the clip’s start time.
  3. For regular 30-fps video, step 3 frames ≈ 0.10 s for ball and 6 frames ≈ 0.20 s for filter; use Advanced Video Options → Advance Frames if helpful. For 60-fps, use 6 and 12 frames. Use nearest valid times.
  4. Show the table’s Y position (m) with the origin at release. With +Y down, values are downward distance traveled. Students subtract new position − previous position to find each equal-interval distance.
Measurement caution: Students may be tempted to compare the ball’s 0.10-s interval distances directly with the filter’s 0.20-s intervals. Instead compare how the distances change across equal intervals within each object. If the filter lands early, write N/A; don't measure after impact. Fluttering can make the filter’s pattern irregular.

Optional: If time remains, students sketch distance vs. time and compare whether the graph gets steeper. Vernier guidance: first marked frame and video-analysis setup.

Interval distances grow, even though each interval is the same length of time. Real video measurements will be noisier; look for the trend, not an exact match.

What to Expect from the Coffee Filter

  • The open filter experiences appreciable air resistance. It may speed up at first but eventually approach a near-constant downward speed, creating roughly equal distance steps. Over short drops, it may not fully reach constant speed.
  • Air resistance does not turn off gravity; the downward force of gravity continues, while upward drag changes its acceleration.
  • If the filter tumbles or moves sideways, the vertical distances might be inconsistent. Students should report this observation rather than invent a terminal-velocity result.

Expected Answers to the Six Questions

QuestionWhat a strong response includes
1. Greatest ball intervalUsually 0.40–0.50 s, with the greatest Δdistance in the data; use their actual result.
2. Ball speedMore distance every equal 0.10 s means speed increased while falling.
3. Which is closer to free fall?Ball; its equal-time interval distances increase progressively, consistent with gravity-dominated motion.
4. Evidence of air resistanceFilter’s interval distances grow more slowly, become nearly equal, or fluctuate from fluttering; cite a pair of intervals.
5. Near-constant filter speedApproximately the same distance in every equal interval.
6. Gravity still acting?Yes. Gravity acts on both; the filter has more noticeable opposing air resistance relative to its weight.

Discussion Moves / Common Mistakes

  • Total distance vs interval distance: Total distance from release will keep increasing; students must subtract neighboring positions to find distance traveled in an interval.
  • Equal-time condition: It is only meaningful to compare interval distances from equally spaced times for the same object.
  • Not proof of perfect free fall: The ball approximates free fall; real falling objects still have some drag. The filter may still accelerate with significant drag.
  • Why different sampling rates? Ball falls in under a second, filter takes longer; different durations allow five usable intervals without landing.
