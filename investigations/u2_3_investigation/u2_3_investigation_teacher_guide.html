Ramp Acceleration

Vernier dynamics carts and tracks • Guided Trial 1 → independent higher-track Trial 2 • 35–45 minutes

EquipmentVernier dynamics cart and track, support, iPad
SoftwareVernier Video Analysis
Student record5 time/velocity rows and 3 calculations per trial

Learning Goal

Students compare velocity–time evidence from a cart rolling down a slightly elevated track and again after increasing the track incline. They calculate the average acceleration three times per trial using pairs of time/velocity readings. No ramp-height measurement is required.

Materials and Before Class

  • Vernier dynamics cart and dynamics track with built-in tape measure, stable books/blocks/risers, one stationary iPad camera per group, soft catch/stop, and Vernier Video Analysis.
  • Secure a gentle incline. Ensure the dynamics cart travels safely within the visible length of track and the filming iPad is out of the cart's path.
  • Film side-on without moving the iPad during a trial. Keep the track's built-in tape measure visible throughout, in approximately the same image plane as the cart, so students can set scale from a known distance.
  • Use the same cart, track, release position, camera placement, and coordinate convention across trials. For Trial 2, raise the support further but keep the setup stable. Students do not measure or record ramp height.
  • Before class, test importing one sample video and check that Vernier displays the velocity component along the track with enough valid values for five table entries. Track more frames in the app if necessary; students write only five complete time/velocity records.

Teacher-led Vernier Walkthrough — Trial 1

  1. Students record one clear full run, releasing the cart without pushing. Do not move or pan the iPad.
  2. Import the video into Vernier Video Analysis (not Graphical Analysis). Set the scale using two marked positions from the track's built-in tape measure. Convert the selected tape measurement to the units Vernier requires. Align the coordinate axis along the incline and choose positive down the track.
  3. Open the gear icon, then Advanced Video Options → Advance Frames → 5 frames. This is Vernier's frame-advance setting; it controls how far the video steps between tracking marks.
  4. Select Add, and click/tap the same visible point on the dynamics cart for each analyzed frame (for example, the center of the front axle).
  5. Display the velocity–time graph and data table; use the velocity component parallel to the track. Have students transcribe five chronologically ordered points with complete time and velocity values spread across the run. Do not copy the position column: Vernier records it, but it is not needed for the acceleration calculation.
  6. On the next printed page, model three acceleration calculations using the prescribed point pairs 1→3, 2→4, and 3→5. Show each initial/final time and velocity, then Δv, Δt, and a = Δv/Δt. The three results need not be identical; look for approximately similar positive values.
Time/velocity checks: Do not use slow-motion playback duration. Read actual time from Vernier. When the first/last tracked frame lacks a calculated velocity, track additional frames and choose five valid entries for the student table. If five-frame advances skip too much of a short run, use a smaller advance after demonstrating where the control is.

Trial 2 — Independent

  1. Groups increase the height of the raised end while preserving the same car, track, starting point, and fixed filming position.
  2. Students record one clean video, import it, verify scale/origin/positive direction, and repeat the tracking with Advance Frames = 5 frames when appropriate.
  3. They select five valid time/velocity readings and calculate acceleration three times (points 1→3, 2→4, 3→5), recording the full work in the larger printed boxes.
  4. Groups compare the overall velocity–time patterns and acceleration results across trials, then complete the original three analysis questions and the conclusion.

Example Calculation and Expected Pattern

Suppose one set of measurements gives vi = 0.24 m/s at ti = 0.40 s, and vf = 0.88 m/s at tf = 1.20 s. Then Δv = 0.64 m/s, Δt = 0.80 s, and a ≈ 0.80 m/s². This example is illustrative, not a target result.

With positive down the track, measured velocity generally rises over time. Increasing the incline generally produces greater positive acceleration, though camera angle, friction, and video-tracking error can produce noisy results. Values calculated from overlapping point intervals may vary somewhat.

Original Analysis Questions — Unchanged

  1. What graph evidence shows that velocity changed?
  2. Was the acceleration approximately positive, negative, or zero using your chosen positive direction? Explain.
  3. Would a steeper or flatter velocity-time trend mean a larger or smaller acceleration? Defend with your data.

Expected reasoning: A non-horizontal trend in velocity over time means velocity changed. With positive down the ramp/track, positive Δv over positive Δt indicates positive acceleration. A steeper velocity–time trend represents greater acceleration magnitude; cite comparisons of calculated acceleration.

Original Final Model Prompt — Unchanged

Write a concise conclusion that answers the research question using specific evidence from your group’s data or observations.

Safety and Cleanup

  • Keep the support secure and the track level side to side; catch the moving dynamics cart before it leaves the end.
  • Keep the camera/other iPads away from the cart path. Avoid moving the iPad while tracking: even slight motion changes the reference frame.
  • Keep the tape measure in frame at the same depth as the cart; incorrect scale or reversed axis can invert/significantly change calculations.
