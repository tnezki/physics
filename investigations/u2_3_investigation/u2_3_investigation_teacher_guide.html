Physics • Unit 2 • Section 2.3 • Investigation Teacher Guide

Ramp Acceleration

One guided video trial, then one independent trial with a higher ramp • 30–40 minutes

Equipment4-ft board + toy car + iPad
SoftwareVernier Video Analysis
EvidenceTwo 12-row printed tables + Δv/Δt

Purpose

Students track a car rolling downhill and use Vernier-generated position, velocity, and time data to calculate average acceleration. After a whole-class guided run, groups increase ramp height and independently reproduce the analysis. Preserve the original three analysis questions and final model.

Materials and Setup

  • Each group: four-foot board, toy car or cart, stable supports, tape, ruler/meterstick, soft stop, iPad with video access and Vernier Video Analysis.
  • Use the same 0–4 ft marks and basic setup as the Section 2.2 Speed Trap. Choose a low first height, then a distinctly higher but still safe second height. Record the vertical rise in cm.
  • Record from the side, with the camera fixed perpendicular to the motion plane. Include a visible known length (for example the full marked 4 ft = 1.2192 m, if both marks are in view).
  • Test the workflow and permissions ahead of class: import from Photos, set scale/origin, make points, display velocity vs. time and the data table. Standard video (30 fps or higher) is a simple starting choice; verify any slow-motion clip plays at the correct recorded time in the software.
Software distinction: The app feature Advanced Video Options → Advance Frames belongs to Vernier Video Analysis. Vernier Graphical Analysis is a separate product; do not direct students to hunt for the frame setting in Graphical Analysis.

Suggested Flow

TimeTeacher / Students
0–5 minPrediction. Set up marked ramp. Discuss positive direction down the ramp.
5–10 minStudents record one clean run at low height. Do not push the car.
10–22 minTeacher models importing and tracking. Everyone marks their car through the clip and fills Trial 1 table.
22–32 minRaise ramp, record one new clean run, and independently track/complete Trial 2.
32–40 minCalculate Δv, Δt, a from two points per trial. Discuss the three original questions and conclusion.

Vernier Video Analysis — Teacher Walkthrough

  1. Import: Open video (Vernier Video Analysis). Trim to the rolling portion if useful.
  2. Scale: Use the System → Scale tool, placing endpoints at a known distance in the same plane as the car. Enter 1.2192 m for a full 4-ft mark-to-mark length, or use a clearly visible meterstick.
  3. Axes: Set System → Origin at the release mark and align the position axis along the ramp, with positive direction downhill. Position and velocity are then reported along that axis (typically x).
  4. Frame increment: Open the gear icon (Advanced Video Options) and select Advance Frames: 5 frames. With 30 fps this marks about every 0.17 s; with 60 fps about every 0.083 s. If the run is too short for sufficient points, a smaller increment is fine.
  5. Mark: Select Add (crosshairs). Mark the exact same feature on the car in every selected frame (e.g., center of front wheel). Continue to the bottom.
  6. Display: Show velocity vs. time for motion down the ramp and open the data table. Students transcribe time (s), position along ramp (m), and velocity along ramp (m/s) into the printed large tables. Vernier calculates velocity from tracked positions. Do not ask students to invent velocity values from successive points without checking the software readout.
  7. Independent repeat: Change only ramp height; keep car, board, release position, measurement scale, and positive direction consistent. The second video needs its own scale/origin settings checked.
Helpful check: Vernier labels the setting Advance Frames, not “frame skips.” Students may leave unused table rows blank; the printed tables provide 12 writing-sized entries for each run. More than 12 tracked points is fine—select 12 well-spaced points in chronological order.

Calculations / Expected Pattern

Pick two recorded points from the same trial, preferably widely spaced. For example, if velocity rises from 0.20 to 0.90 m/s while time rises from 0.30 to 1.30 s, then Δv = 0.70 m/s, Δt = 1.00 s, and a ≈ 0.70 m/s² (example only).

Velocity usually increases downhill, so the velocity–time graph tends to slope upward if the positive axis points downhill. A higher ramp generally produces a steeper velocity–time trend and larger positive acceleration. Student data may be uneven due to tracking, friction, and camera angle.

Analysis Questions — Original Wording Preserved

  1. What graph evidence shows that velocity changed?
  2. Was the acceleration approximately positive, negative, or zero using your chosen positive direction? Explain.
  3. Would a steeper or flatter velocity-time trend mean a larger or smaller acceleration? Defend with your data.

Expected answers: The plotted velocity changes over time (nonhorizontal trend); with positive direction downhill, acceleration is generally positive; a greater absolute slope of velocity versus time indicates larger magnitude of acceleration. A steeper ramp often yields larger acceleration, supported by each group's measured Δv/Δt.

Final Model — Original Prompt

Write a concise conclusion that answers the research question using specific evidence from your group’s data or observations.

Look for: Claims connected to numerical readings from both tables, comparison of acceleration calculations, and discussion of why a nonhorizontal velocity–time trend indicates changing velocity.

Common Pitfalls and Safety

  • Tracking the front bumper in one frame and the wheel in the next introduces noise. Track one consistent feature.
  • Using the slow-motion playback clock instead of the video's true recording time produces incorrect velocities. Use Vernier time values.
  • If the camera is oblique or scale is at a different depth from the car, the measured distances will be off.
  • Secure the board and support; catch the car safely. Do not let the raised ramp shift or the car hit the iPad.
