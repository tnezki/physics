Physics - Unit 5 - Section 2 - Investigation

When Waves Meet and Stand

Create overlapping pulses, then build stable standing-wave patterns on a rope.

Learning target: Students will explain how waves interact through interference, standing waves, and the Doppler effect.
Research Question: What evidence distinguishes constructive interference, destructive interference, nodes, and antinodes?

1. Make a Prediction

Two identical upward pulses approach each other. Predict the rope shape at the instant they overlap. Then predict what happens if one pulse is upward and the other is downward.

2. Set Up

Equipment: long rope or Slinky, fixed support or partner at far end, tape markers, clear floor lane
1
With one student at each end, send two small upward pulses toward each other. Watch the overlap and what happens afterward.
2
Repeat with one upward and one downward pulse. Sketch the rope shape at maximum overlap and after the pulses separate.
3
Fix or firmly hold one end. Drive the other end continuously until a stable one-segment standing-wave pattern is visible. Mark locations that barely move and locations that move most.
4
Increase the driving rate until a stable pattern with more segments appears. Count nodes, antinodes, and segments. Do not use the term resonance to explain the cause yet; focus on the observed standing-wave pattern.
5
Compare the pulse-overlap evidence with the standing-wave evidence. Identify where repeated constructive and destructive interference must be occurring.

4. Analyze the Evidence

1. When two pulses overlapped, were the original pulses permanently changed? Cite an observation.

2. Explain why a node can remain nearly still even though waves are present there.

3. As more standing-wave segments fit on the same rope length, what happens to wavelength?

4. Connect the moving-source demo to the section model: what changes about wavefront spacing ahead of and behind a moving source?

5. Defend or Revise Your Model

Create a concise rule set for (1) superposition, (2) nodes/antinodes in standing waves, and (3) how relative motion changes observed frequency.

Use your group’s actual observations and measurements. Classroom data may vary; explain variation instead of forcing a perfect result.
