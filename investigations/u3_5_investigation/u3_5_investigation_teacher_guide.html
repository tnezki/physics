Physics - Unit 3 - Section 3.5 - Investigation Teacher Guide

Predict the Landing Point

Projectile prediction + test - designed for 25-30 minutes

Time25-30 minutes
Groups3-4
Core toolVideo analysis + horizontal launch

Why This Investigation Fits Section 3.5

Students have now learned x = v_x t and y = 1/2 gt^2 as separate component relationships linked by the same time. Predicting a real landing point makes the independence of horizontal and vertical motion operational rather than just descriptive.

Materials Per Group

  • Small ball/marble
  • Short ramp or track
  • Table or lab bench
  • Meter sticks
  • Tape
  • Paper target
  • Phone/iPad/camera
  • Vernier Graphical Analysis video analysis

Before Class

  1. Choose a launch point with a clear floor landing zone.
  2. Secure the ramp so the release point and launch direction are repeatable.
  3. Mark a consistent release point; do not allow students to push the ball.
  4. Test the range so the ball lands safely away from walls and feet.

Tool / Data Setup

For video analysis, use a meter stick in the plane of motion and track the ball over the last several frames before it leaves the table. A position-time slope or velocity readout can provide v_x. Students do not need a full trajectory fit.

Suggested Flow

MinutesStudent WorkTeacher Focus
0-6Measure h and v_xCheck that students measure launch height vertically and speed horizontally.
6-13Compute fall time and predicted rangeRequire the same t in both component equations.
13-22Three launch trialsStudents record first contact, not bounce/roll distance.
22-30Model evaluation and misconception critiqueConnect success/mismatch to assumptions and measurement rather than inventing a forward force.

Expected Evidence

Predictions should land reasonably near the measured first-contact region when the launch is horizontal and repeatable. Horizontal speed remains approximately constant in the ideal model; gravity changes vertical velocity. The same flight time connects y = 1/2 gt^2 and x = v_x t.

Watch for: The ball may bounce or roll after impact; only first floor contact counts. Students may also use total path length or a slanted distance by mistake. Keep horizontal range and vertical drop separate.

Strong Responses

PromptWhat to Look For
Vertical calculationSolves t from h = 1/2 gt^2 using the measured table height.
Horizontal calculationUses x = v_x t with the same t and measured launch speed.
Model explanationStates that gravity accelerates vertically while ideal horizontal velocity remains constant; the combined motions create the curved trajectory.
CritiqueRejects a forward gravitational component in the ideal model and explains that any real horizontal slowing would come from air resistance, not gravity.

Common Problems

  • Launch speed varies: Use the same release point and do not push the ball.
  • Video speed estimate is poor: Track the ball over a longer horizontal interval near the edge and keep the camera perpendicular.
  • Landing marks scatter: Use three trials and discuss experimental spread; check that the ramp is not twisting the launch direction.

Safety

Keep the landing zone clear. Do not launch toward people. Use a low-mass ball and collect it immediately after each trial to prevent trip hazards.

Connection Forward

This investigation completes Unit 3 by combining inertia, gravity, acceleration, and modeling: no forward force is required to maintain the horizontal component in the ideal projectile model.
