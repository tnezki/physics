Resultant Vector Lab

Eight short vector scenarios: six two-force cases and two three-force extensions, each predicted before PhET verification.

Time35–45 minutes
GroupsPairs
Core toolPhET Vector Addition

Purpose and Exact Section Alignment

Learning Target: Students will represent forces as vectors and determine resultant/net force in simple force situations.

  • U1-MG03-IC01: use vector arrow direction and length to represent a force’s direction and relative magnitude.
  • U1-MG03-IC02: combine forces acting in the same or opposite directions to determine net force.
  • U1-MG03-IC03: determine the direction and relative magnitude of the resultant/net force from a simple force-vector diagram.
Scope guard. Scenarios 1–4 are direct Section 1.3 evidence. Scenarios 5–6 apply the current 1.3 Notes’ simple two-dimensional resultant geometry. Extensions 7–8 are intentionally labeled extensions. Students do not need component trigonometry. They may use signed addition/subtraction, a scale drawing, symmetry, and the Pythagorean relationship when useful.

Materials and Setup

  • One iPad/device per pair with internet access.
  • Student investigation, pencil, and a ruler/straightedge if students want cleaner scale drawings.
  • Approved simulation: https://phet.colorado.edu/sims/html/vector-addition/latest/vector-addition_en.html

Before starting, briefly establish the angle convention: measure counterclockwise from +x, so 0° is right, 90° is up, 180° is left, and 270° is down. The student page carries this reference.

What Students Should Physically Do

  1. Read the two or three given force vectors and interpret each magnitude/direction.
  2. Draw the vectors from a common origin on the printed grid using a scale that fits.
  3. Construct a resultant using a head-to-tail/parallelogram idea and make a numerical or graphical prediction.
  4. Write enough math/geometric reasoning to show why the prediction makes sense.
  5. Recreate the same vectors in PhET and record the checked resultant.
  6. If their prediction changes, identify what changed in the drawing or reasoning.

Press Questions

  • What information is lost if you ignore the vector directions and only add the magnitudes?
  • In Scenario 4, why is “0 N @ 0°” less accurate than “0 N, no defined direction”?
  • In Scenario 5, where must the resultant point before you calculate anything? Why?
  • In Scenario 6, what cancels because the two vectors are mirror images?
  • Why can three nonzero forces in Extension 7 still produce a zero resultant?
  • What single change turns Extension 7 into Extension 8, and what does that do to the resultant?

Common Misconceptions / Setup Problems

  • Adding magnitudes regardless of direction: ask students to point along each arrow and identify which effects reinforce or oppose.
  • Arrow length treated as decoration: require the larger force to have the longer arrow when the same drawing scale is used.
  • Clockwise angle measurement: return to the 0°/90°/180°/270° reference printed on every grid.
  • Calling the resultant a third independent force: the resultant is a single vector model of the combined effect of the given forces.
  • Trying to force a direction onto a zero vector: zero magnitude has no meaningful direction.
  • Jumping to component trig: redirect to scale drawing, symmetry, simple signed sums, or the right-triangle relationship used in the current Notes.

Closing / Answer Guidance

Why not always add magnitudes?Because vector direction changes how forces combine. Same-direction forces reinforce; opposite forces can partially or completely cancel; non-collinear forces combine geometrically.
What produces zero resultant?The vector effects must balance completely so their combined vector is zero. Equal opposite pairs are one example; the three equally spaced vectors in Extension 7 are another.
Prediction vs. PhET evidenceStrong responses name a specific mismatch or confirmation—direction, arrow length, symmetry, signed sum, or geometry—and explain how the simulation evidence changed or strengthened the student’s model.
Good stopping point: students should leave seeing the resultant as a direction-and-magnitude summary of the given force vectors, not as a separate force that appeared after the calculation.
