CER: Which Object Is Closer to Free Fall?

Teacher overview and CER example

Three I-can targets: (1) Explain why free-falling objects accelerate equally when drag is ignored. (2) Use g ≈ 10 m/s² to find speed gained. (3) Compare distance-interval patterns and distinguish free fall from significant air resistance.

Sequence

Student page 1: one CER. Page 2: 8 vocabulary questions. Pages 3–4: 8 conceptual questions. Page 5: 4 formula problems. Assign all as a longer practice, or use the CER first and remaining pages across subsequent lessons.

Interpretation: The dense-ball intervals come from g ≈ 10 m/s²: 0.05, 0.15, 0.25, 0.35, and 0.45 m. The running totals are 0.05, 0.20, 0.45, 0.80, and 1.25 m. The cotton-ball numbers illustrate motion approaching approximately constant speed; they are not measured values.

One strong CER response

Claim: The dense ball behaves more like an object in free fall.

Evidence: It falls 0.05 m in the first 0.10 s and 0.45 m in the final interval; its total reaches 1.25 m. The cotton ball's last three interval distances are nearly constant at 0.08 m.

Reasoning: Larger distances in equal amounts of time mean greater speed. The dense ball's interval distances increase steadily, which fits nearly constant gravitational acceleration. The cotton ball faces significant upward air resistance, so its speed increases less and may become nearly constant. Gravity still acts on both.

Facilitation checks

  • Differentiate interval distance from running total. A total always rising does not necessarily mean increasing speed.
  • Do not infer exact terminal velocity from a brief cotton-ball example. Real cotton balls may drift or flutter.
  • The dense ball approximates ideal free fall; the table uses a classroom model, not collected evidence.

Vocabulary & Conceptual Answer Key

Questions 1–16 • Accept equivalent scientifically sound explanations

Vocabulary: Questions 1–8

QExpected answer
1Motion when gravity is the only significant force acting on an object.
2The change in velocity per unit time (a change in speed, direction, or both).
3g; approximately 10 m/s² downward near Earth’s surface.
4An opposing force from the surrounding air; for a downward-moving object it acts upward.
5Its initial velocity is zero at the instant of release.
6A fixed duration repeated, e.g. 0.00–0.10 s and 0.10–0.20 s.
7The magnitude of velocity does not change; equal distances occur in equal time intervals.
8Total is accumulated distance since release; interval is only the new distance covered during one selected interval.

Conceptual: Questions 9–16

QExpected answer
9Both have the same downward acceleration g when no air resistance is present. Gravity’s force grows with mass, but so does inertia.
10It is speeding up: each successive equal-time interval covers more distance.
11It may be approaching constant speed; gravity still acts downward, while upward air resistance offsets more of its effect.
12Larger mass does not imply larger free-fall acceleration without drag; gravitational force and inertia both scale with mass.
13Speed is increasing as it falls; equal-time snapshots become farther apart.
14It slows while rising, because gravitational acceleration remains downward (opposite its upward velocity).
15No; total distance rises even at constant speed. Compare distances covered in successive equal time intervals.
16Downward motion alone is not enough. A cotton ball with strong upward air resistance is not moving under gravity alone.

Formula Answers & Teaching Notes

Questions 17–20 • Worked solutions

Formula assumptions: Starts from rest; negligible drag; take g ≈ 10 m/s². Speed gained after t seconds is v = gt. Distance fallen is d = ½gt². State direction separately when asked.

Worked Formula Answers: Questions 17–20

17. Speed after 2.0 s:

v = gt = (10 m/s²)(2.0 s) = 20 m/s downward.

18. Time to gain 30 m/s:

t = v ÷ g = (30 m/s) ÷ (10 m/s²) = 3.0 s.

19. Distance after 2.0 s:

d = ½gt² = ½(10 m/s²)(2.0 s)² = 20 m.

20. Distance in two intervals:

First second: d(1) = ½(10)(1²) = 5 m.
First two seconds: d(2) = ½(10)(2²) = 20 m.
Second second alone: 20 − 5 = 15 m.
Extra distance during second second: 15 − 5 = 10 m farther.

Suggested use

  • The CER is a 10–15 minute standalone task. The 20 new questions are supplementary, not another 10-minute assignment; use them as independent practice, homework, or a class activity.
  • In vocabulary answers, prioritize accurate ideas over exact textbook wording.
  • For mathematical responses, look for an appropriate relationship, substitution, units, and a meaningful physical answer.
  • For conceptual responses, look for an explanation of the cause, not just a direction or an answer word.

Alignment and misconceptions

Target 1 (equal acceleration): Q1–4, Q9, Q12, Q14, Q16.
Target 2 (g and speed): Q3, Q5, Q17–18.
Target 3 (distance patterns and drag): CER, Q6–8, Q10–11, Q13, Q15, Q19–20.

Watch for: “More total distance means faster”; “gravity turns off when drag matters”; “heavy always falls faster”; and “upward-moving objects accelerate upward.”
