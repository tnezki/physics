Which Object Is Closer to Free Fall?

Claim–Evidence–Reasoning • 10–15 minutes • Independent or partners

Learning target: I can use free-fall patterns to compare distance fallen during different time intervals and distinguish free fall from falling with significant air resistance.

Purpose

Students interpret a short table of distances during equal 0.10 s intervals and write one CER about the difference between gravity-dominated motion and motion with significant air resistance. No additional video recording, equations, or acceleration calculations.

Simple classroom flow

  1. 2 min — Read: Students compare the two columns and underline two useful measurements.
  2. 3 min — Claim + evidence: Write a claim and support it with at least two numerical observations.
  3. 5 min — Reasoning: Connect equal-time interval distances to speed change; explain gravity and air resistance.
  4. 2–5 min — Partner check: Ask “Does your reasoning explain why your evidence supports the claim?” Revise once.

Expected CER (one possible answer)

Claim: The dense ball is closer to free fall than the cotton ball.

Evidence: The dense ball travels 0.05 m in the first 0.10 s but 0.44 m in the last 0.10 s. The cotton ball's interval distances reach about 0.08 m and stay near 0.08 m for the last three intervals.

Reasoning: An object traveling farther during successive equal time intervals is speeding up. The dense ball's growing distances are consistent with gravity-dominated acceleration. The cotton ball has appreciable air resistance opposing its fall, so its increase in speed becomes much smaller; nearly equal distances indicate nearly constant speed. Gravity still acts on both.

Look for

  • Claim: Names the dense ball, not merely “one object.”
  • Evidence: Uses actual interval numbers and units, ideally comparing both objects.
  • Reasoning: Explains that greater distance in the same time means greater speed, and that air resistance can reduce acceleration while gravity remains present.

Important scientific cautions

  • These are illustrative example values, not purported measurements from the class. Students may substitute their own Vernier values if they cite actual times and equal intervals.
  • The sample depicts a cotton ball approaching nearly constant speed; real cotton-ball videos may not reach terminal speed in a short drop, and its motion may be uneven.
  • The dense ball is an approximation to free fall; real air resistance is not exactly zero. The activity compares which object is closer, not which is perfectly free.

Optional one-question extension: If the objects are both accelerating downward at the instant you release them, can air resistance still change their motion later? Explain.
