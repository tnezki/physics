Station 1 Answers

Question 1

1. A compass needle is itself a tiny magnet. What two pole regions does it have?

Answer: A compass needle has a north-seeking pole region and a south-seeking pole region. Rationale: A compass is a tiny magnetic dipole, so both pole types are present.

Question 2

2. State the interaction rule for like magnetic poles and for unlike magnetic poles.

Answer: Like poles repel; unlike poles attract. Rationale: N–N and S–S push apart, while N–S pull together.

Question 3

3. What does closer spacing of magnetic-field lines indicate about the field?

Answer: Closer field-line spacing represents a stronger magnetic field. Rationale: Field-line density is the diagram model used to compare relative field strength.

Question 4

4. Why does cutting a magnet not produce a north-only piece and a south-only piece?

Answer: Each smaller piece still contains aligned magnetic dipoles/domains, so each fragment develops both north and south pole regions. Cutting does not isolate a single pole.

Question 5

5. How does a strongly magnetized piece of iron differ from ordinary iron in its domain alignment?

Answer: Strongly magnetized iron has many more domains aligned in a common direction. Ordinary iron has domains oriented more randomly, so their effects largely cancel.

Question 6

6. Use the two-panel field-pattern figure to compare what happens in the space between unlike-facing poles and like-facing poles. Describe one difference in the pattern.

Answer: Unlike-facing poles show field lines connecting across the gap, while like-facing poles show lines bending away from the central gap. Rationale: Unlike poles attract and like poles repel.

7. Region A of a field diagram has tightly crowded lines; Region B has sparse lines. Rank the field strength and explain the model evidence.

Answer: Region A is stronger than Region B. Evidence: The field lines are more closely spaced in A, and line density represents relative field strength.

Question 8

8. A compass near a magnet rotates strongly, while a compass much farther away changes direction only slightly. What does this suggest about relative field strength?

Answer: The field is stronger near the magnet and weaker farther away. Evidence: The nearby compass experiences a much larger turning effect.

Question 9

9. An iron nail is stroked repeatedly with a magnet and then attracts paper clips. Explain the change using domain alignment rather than saying magnetism was “added.”

Answer: Stroking the nail causes more magnetic domains to align in a common direction. The nail’s existing microscopic magnetic regions become organized; magnetism is not a substance that is “added.”

Question 10

10. A student draws field arrows entering the north pole and leaving the south pole outside a bar magnet. Identify and correct the diagram error.

Answer: Reverse the outside arrows: magnetic-field arrows leave the north pole and curve toward/enter the south pole outside a bar magnet.

Question 11

11. A student breaks a magnet and expects one fragment to contain only north behavior. Use the repeated-cutting evidence and a domain/pole model to explain why that prediction fails.

Answer: The prediction fails because magnetic material is made of dipole-like regions/domains. Breaking the magnet makes smaller magnets, each with both pole types; repeated cutting does not isolate a stable north-only piece.

Question 12

12. Two students disagree about field strength. One uses line spacing; the other uses arrow length that was drawn inconsistently. Decide which evidence is more defensible and justify the choice.

Answer: The line-spacing evidence is more defensible. Rationale: In a consistently drawn field map, spacing is the intended model for relative strength, while inconsistent arrow lengths are not reliable evidence.
