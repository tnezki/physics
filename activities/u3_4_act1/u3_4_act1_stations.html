Station 1 Answers

Question 1

1. Define a force interaction in terms of two objects.

Answer / rationale: A force interaction involves two objects exerting forces on one another. Each object experiences one member of the interaction pair.

Question 2

2. A swimmer pushes water backward. What object exerts the forward reaction force on the swimmer?

Answer: The water exerts the forward reaction force on the swimmer. Rationale: The swimmer pushes water backward, so the water pushes the swimmer forward with equal magnitude.

Question 3

3. A book pushes down on a table. Identify the Third-Law partner of that force.

Answer: The table pushes upward on the book. Rationale: That force is the Third-Law partner to the book’s downward push on the table; the pair acts on two different objects.

Station 2 Answers

Question 4

4. If an action force points east, what can be said about the reaction-force direction and magnitude?

Answer / rationale: The reaction force points west and has the same magnitude as the eastward action force. Third-Law forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

Question 5

5. What must be defined before deciding whether a force is internal or external?

Answer: The system (system boundary) must be defined first. Rationale: Whether a force is internal or external depends on whether the interacting objects lie inside or outside that boundary.

Question 6

6. Use the car–truck collision image. Describe the Third-Law force pair during contact without claiming one vehicle exerts the larger force.

Rationale: During contact, the car pushes on the truck and the truck pushes on the car with equal force magnitude in opposite directions. The forces act on different vehicles, so neither interaction force is larger.

Question 7

7. Two carts interact through a spring. One has mass 2 kg and the other 8 kg. The spring exerts 16 N on each. Calculate each acceleration magnitude.

Work: For the $2\text{ kg}$ cart, $a=16/2=8\text{ m/s}^2$. For the $8\text{ kg}$ cart, $a=16/8=2\text{ m/s}^2$. The equal interaction forces produce different accelerations because the masses differ.

Question 8

8. A person pushes a wall with 120 N. Determine the magnitude of the wall’s force on the person and explain why the forces are not both on the person.

Answer / rationale: The wall pushes on the person with $120\text{ N}$ in the opposite direction. One force acts on the wall and the partner force acts on the person, so both are not forces on the person.

Question 9

9. For the system “boat + paddler,” classify the water’s force on the paddle as internal or external and explain why.

Answer: External. Rationale: The chosen system contains the boat and paddler, but not the surrounding water, so the water’s force crosses the system boundary.

Question 10

10. For a system containing Earth and a falling ball together, classify the gravitational interaction between Earth and ball as internal to that system.

Answer: Internal. Rationale: Both Earth and the ball are inside the chosen system, so their gravitational interaction is between objects within the system boundary.

Station 5 Answers

Question 11

11. A student lists “the ball’s weight and the table’s support force on the ball” as a Third-Law pair. Explain why that is incorrect and identify the partner to one of those forces.

Rationale: Weight and the table’s support force both act on the ball, so they cannot be a Third-Law pair. The partner to the ball’s weight is the ball’s gravitational pull on Earth; the partner to the table’s support force is the ball’s force on the table.

Question 12

12. A cannon fires a small cannonball. Explain how equal and opposite interaction forces can give the cannonball a much larger acceleration than the cannon.

Rationale: The cannon and cannonball exert equal-magnitude interaction forces on one another, but $a=F/m$. Because the cannonball has much less mass, the same force gives it a much larger acceleration.

Question 20

20. Which statement about action and reaction is correct?

A. They act on different objects, so they are not added as forces on one object.
B. They always cancel every object’s acceleration.
C. One is larger when masses differ.
D. They require contact in every case.
E. They point in the same direction.
Answer: A — They act on different objects, so they are not added as forces on one object.
Work / rationale: Third-Law partners act on different objects, so they are not added together in the force sum for a single object.
