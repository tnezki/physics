Conceptual Physics — Unit 8: Magnetism and Electromagnetism

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze magnetism and electromagnetism by explaining magnetic poles, magnetic fields, magnetic domains, the relationship between electric current and magnetic fields, electromagnets, magnetic forces on moving charges and current-carrying wires, electromagnetic induction, generators, alternating current, and modern electromagnetic systems.

Unit Source Alignment

  • Textbook Alignment: 8.1 Magnetism and Magnetic Fields — 24.1–24.4; 8.2 Electricity and Magnetism — 24.5; 8.3 Electromagnets and Magnetic Forces — 24.6, 24.7; 8.4 Generators and Alternating Current — 25.3; 8.5 Modern Electromagnetic Systems — synthesis/application

Section Outline

Section 8.1: Magnetism and Magnetic Fields

Textbook Sections: 24.1–24.4

Learning Target: Students will explain magnetic poles, magnetic fields, and magnetic domains using field models and everyday magnetic interactions.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that magnets have north and south poles.
  • I can describe how like magnetic poles repel and unlike magnetic poles attract.
  • I can explain that magnetic field lines show the direction and relative strength of a magnetic field and explain why magnetic poles are not isolated as single north or south poles.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: magnetic pole, magnetic field, field line, magnetic domain, north, south

Key Representations: field-line diagrams; compass evidence; domain models; pole-interaction sketches

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating field lines as physical strings; assuming isolated magnetic poles are ordinary; confusing field direction with object motion

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Use magnets, compasses, and domain models to explain attraction, repulsion, and field strength/direction

Recurring Practice Opportunities: field modeling; evidence interpretation; explanation

Section 8.2: Electricity and Magnetism

Textbook Sections: 24.5

Learning Target: Students will explain how electric current produces magnetic fields and how changing electric and magnetic fields are connected.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that moving electric charges create magnetic fields.
  • I can describe the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire.
  • I can explain how a compass can detect a magnetic field produced by current and compare magnetic fields around a straight wire, loop, and coil.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electric current, moving charge, magnetic field, current-carrying wire, loop, coil

Key Representations: field diagrams around wire/loop/coil; compass observations; current-direction models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Thinking only permanent magnets make magnetic fields; reversing current without considering field reversal; confusing electric and magnetic field models

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Investigate and compare magnetic fields produced by straight wires, loops, and coils

Recurring Practice Opportunities: investigation evidence; field modeling; pattern analysis

Section 8.3: Electromagnets and Magnetic Forces

Textbook Sections: 24.6, 24.7

Learning Target: Students will analyze electromagnets and magnetic forces on moving charges and current-carrying wires.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that a current-carrying coil of wire is an electromagnet.
  • I can describe how increasing current or coil turns affects electromagnet strength.
  • I can explain how an iron core can strengthen an electromagnet and explain that moving charges can experience magnetic forces.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electromagnet, coil, current, iron core, magnetic force, moving charge, current-carrying wire

Key Representations: electromagnet models; force-direction diagrams; $F=qvB$ and $F=BIL$ relationships

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Assuming more turns always increases strength regardless of current/core; treating magnetic force as parallel to motion; confusing field with force

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze electromagnet strength and force interactions in coils, wires, and moving-charge situations

Recurring Practice Opportunities: modeling; quantitative reasoning; experimental reasoning

Section 8.4: Generators and Alternating Current

Textbook Sections: 25.3

Learning Target: Students will explain how generators use electromagnetic induction to convert mechanical energy into electrical energy and why alternating current changes direction.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that electromagnetic induction can produce voltage when magnetic conditions change.
  • I can describe how rotating a coil in a magnetic field can induce voltage.
  • I can explain that a generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy and explain why the output of a common generator is alternating current.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: electromagnetic induction, induced voltage, generator, alternating current, magnetic flux/change

Key Representations: generator diagrams; changing-field models; AC graphs; qualitative Faraday relationship

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Thinking a steady magnetic field automatically induces voltage; confusing motor and generator energy direction; treating AC as energy disappearing and reappearing

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Explain generator operation and alternating output through changing magnetic conditions and energy conversion

Recurring Practice Opportunities: systems modeling; graph interpretation; scientific explanation

Section 8.5: Modern Electromagnetic Systems

Textbook Sections: synthesis/application

Learning Target: Students will apply magnetism, electromagnets, magnetic forces, and electromagnetic induction to real-world technologies and systems.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain how electromagnetic systems use energy transformations.
  • I can describe how generators, motors, transformers, speakers, microphones, maglev systems, MRI, and electric meters use electromagnetism.
  • I can explain why energy input is required for electrical energy output and critique claims that a device produces energy from magnetism alone.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: transformer, motor, generator, speaker, microphone, electromagnetic system, energy transformation

Key Representations: system block diagrams; transformer turn/voltage ratios; energy-flow diagrams; device models

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Claiming magnets supply unlimited energy; ignoring energy input/output; using device labels without explaining field/current/induction mechanism

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze modern electromagnetic technologies and critique claims about their operation or energy source

Recurring Practice Opportunities: systems reasoning; model evaluation; argument from evidence

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Magnetic fields provide a model for interactions among magnets, currents, and moving charges.
  • Electric current can create magnetism; changing magnetic conditions can induce electric potential difference.
  • Electromagnetic technologies transform energy through fields, forces, current, and induction rather than creating energy.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 8.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: magnetic field/domain diagrams, current/coil models, force diagrams, generator/transformer models, AC graphs and energy flows

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 8.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: magnetic field/domain diagrams, current/coil models, force diagrams, generator/transformer models, AC graphs and energy flows

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 8.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving magnetic-force/field, transformer, and electric-power relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: magnetic field/domain diagrams, current/coil models, force diagrams, generator/transformer models, AC graphs and energy flows

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 8.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student identifies what evidence would test a claim or model, uses observations/data appropriately, and evaluates whether results support the explanation.

Representations / Contexts: magnetic field/domain diagrams, current/coil models, force diagrams, generator/transformer models, AC graphs and energy flows

Common Weaknesses: Treating one observation as proof; failing to control/identify relevant variables; reporting data without connecting it to the claim or model.

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 8.1, 8.2, 8.3, 8.4, 8.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: magnetic field/domain diagrams, current/coil models, force diagrams, generator/transformer models, AC graphs and energy flows

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U8-MG01

Mastery Goal: Explain magnetic poles, fields, and domains using field representations and evidence from magnetic interactions.

Supporting Learning: Sections 8.1.

Recurring Practices: field modeling; evidence interpretation.

Key Representations: field-line diagrams, compass evidence, domain models.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence interprets field direction/relative strength and uses domains/poles to explain attraction and repulsion without treating field lines as material objects.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Field lines as physical strings; isolated pole assumptions; field/force confusion.

U8-MG02

Mastery Goal: Explain how electric current produces magnetic fields and how coils/cores create controllable electromagnets.

Supporting Learning: Sections 8.2, 8.3.

Recurring Practices: investigation evidence; modeling; pattern analysis.

Key Representations: wire/loop/coil field models, electromagnet diagrams, investigation observations.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence connects moving charge/current to magnetic field and predicts how current, turns, or core changes affect an electromagnet qualitatively.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Only permanent magnets make fields; field reversal mistakes; one-factor electromagnet reasoning.

U8-MG03

Mastery Goal: Analyze magnetic forces on moving charges and current-carrying wires using direction, field, motion/current, and source-supported quantitative relationships.

Supporting Learning: Sections 8.3.

Recurring Practices: quantitative reasoning; multi-representation modeling.

Key Representations: force-direction diagrams, $F=qvB$, $F=BIL$, field/motion/current representations.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence distinguishes field from force, interprets direction relationships, and uses the quantitative model only when its represented conditions are appropriate.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Force parallel to motion assumption; field=force; variable/units confusion.

U8-MG04

Mastery Goal: Explain electromagnetic induction, generators, alternating current, transformers, and modern systems as linked field/current processes and energy transformations.

Supporting Learning: Sections 8.4, 8.5.

Recurring Practices: systems reasoning; graph interpretation; argument from evidence.

Key Representations: generator/transformer diagrams, AC graphs, changing-field models, energy-flow diagrams.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence explains that changing magnetic conditions induce voltage, traces energy input/output, and rejects claims of energy creation from magnetism alone.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Steady field induces by itself; motor/generator direction confusion; magnets as unlimited energy source.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 8 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U8-MG01, U8-MG02, U8-MG03, U8-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of magnetic field/domain diagrams, current/coil models, force diagrams, generator/transformer models, AC graphs and energy flows. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 8 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
