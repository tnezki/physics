Conceptual Physics — Unit 3: Forces and Motion

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze how forces affect motion by applying Newton’s Second Law, distinguishing mass from weight, explaining friction and air resistance, comparing free fall and nonfree fall, identifying action-reaction force pairs, and modeling projectile motion using horizontal and vertical components.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Course Structure: Conceptual Physics, Unit 3 of 8, five sections.
  • Textbook Alignment: 3.1 Force and Acceleration — 4.1, 4.4; 3.2 Friction and Real-World Forces — 4.2, 4.3; 3.3 Free Fall and Nonfree Fall — 4.5, 4.6; 3.4 Newton’s Third Law and Interactions — 5.1–5.5; 3.5 Projectile Motion — 10.1

Section Outline

Section 3.1: Force and Acceleration

Textbook Sections: 4.1, 4.4

Learning Target: Students will explain how net force and mass determine acceleration and use Newton’s Second Law to analyze changes in motion.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that force causes acceleration, not just motion.
  • I can describe how increasing net force affects acceleration.
  • I can describe how increasing mass affects acceleration and use Newton’s Second Law to calculate force, mass, or acceleration in simple situations.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: Newton’s Second Law, net force, mass, acceleration, proportional relationship

Key Representations: $F_{net}=ma$; force diagrams; force-mass-acceleration tables/graphs

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Saying force causes motion rather than acceleration; assuming equal force gives equal acceleration for different masses; using individual force instead of net force

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Compare how changing force or mass changes acceleration; interpret simple force-motion evidence

Recurring Practice Opportunities: quantitative reasoning; variable relationships; model-based explanation

Section 3.2: Friction and Real-World Forces

Textbook Sections: 4.2, 4.3

Learning Target: Students will explain how friction, mass, and weight affect real-world motion.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain friction as a force that opposes motion or attempted motion.
  • I can distinguish static friction from sliding friction.
  • I can explain why an object can remain at rest even when pushed and distinguish mass from weight.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: friction, static friction, sliding friction, mass, weight, support force

Key Representations: $W=mg$; force diagrams with friction; force-vs-motion descriptions; qualitative friction graphs

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating friction as always equal to the applied force; confusing mass and weight; assuming friction only exists during motion

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze pushing, sliding, traction, and surfaces; compare conditions that change frictional effects

Recurring Practice Opportunities: modeling; evidence-based explanation; quantitative reasoning

Section 3.3: Free Fall and Nonfree Fall

Textbook Sections: 4.5, 4.6

Learning Target: Students will distinguish free fall from nonfree fall and explain how air resistance affects falling motion.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that free fall means gravity is the only significant force acting.
  • I can explain why objects in free fall accelerate at $g$ when air resistance is ignored.
  • I can explain why falling through air can produce acceleration less than $g$ and describe terminal speed as constant speed reached when air resistance balances weight.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: free fall, nonfree fall, air resistance, weight, terminal speed

Key Representations: falling force diagrams; speed/time behavior; terminal-speed graphs; $F_{net}=W-F_{air}$

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Calling terminal speed zero acceleration because gravity stops; assuming air resistance is constant; confusing mass and weight effects

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Compare objects falling in vacuum-like and air-resistance conditions; explain terminal speed from balanced forces

Recurring Practice Opportunities: graph interpretation; force modeling; explanation of phenomena

Section 3.4: Newton’s Third Law and Interactions

Textbook Sections: 5.1–5.5

Learning Target: Students will identify force interactions and explain Newton’s Third Law using action-reaction pairs that act on different objects.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain that forces occur as interactions between objects.
  • I can identify the action and reaction forces in common situations.
  • I can explain why action-reaction forces are equal in strength and opposite in direction and explain why action-reaction forces do not cancel when they act on different objects.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: Newton’s Third Law, interaction, action-reaction pair, system

Key Representations: interaction diagrams; paired force arrows on different objects; verbal interaction descriptions

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Pairing two forces on the same object; saying the larger object exerts the larger third-law force; claiming action-reaction forces cancel each other

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze pushes, collisions, walking, propulsion, and contact interactions by identifying the two objects

Recurring Practice Opportunities: system/interaction modeling; explanation; critique reasoning

Section 3.5: Projectile Motion

Textbook Sections: 10.1

Learning Target: Students will model projectile motion by separating horizontal and vertical components of motion.

I Can Statements

  • I can define a projectile as an object moving under the influence of gravity after being launched.
  • I can explain why horizontal motion remains constant when air resistance is ignored.
  • I can explain why vertical motion accelerates downward due to gravity and describe projectile motion as the combination of horizontal constant velocity and vertical accelerated motion.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: projectile, horizontal velocity, vertical acceleration, component motion, gravity

Key Representations: horizontal/vertical component diagrams; trajectory sketches; $x=v_xt$ and $y=\frac12gt^2$

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Assuming horizontal force is required after launch; mixing horizontal and vertical motion; assuming gravity changes horizontal velocity when air resistance is ignored

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Model launched objects by separating horizontal constant-velocity motion from vertical accelerated motion

Recurring Practice Opportunities: multi-representation modeling; quantitative reasoning; explanation

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Net force and mass together determine acceleration.
  • Interactions, friction, gravity, and air resistance must be modeled on the correct object/system.
  • Projectile motion can be understood by separating horizontal and vertical components.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: force/interaction diagrams, motion and terminal-speed graphs, component diagrams, Newton-law relationships

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: force/interaction diagrams, motion and terminal-speed graphs, component diagrams, Newton-law relationships

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving Newton’s Second Law, weight, friction/net-force, and simple projectile relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: force/interaction diagrams, motion and terminal-speed graphs, component diagrams, Newton-law relationships

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5

Representations / Contexts: force/interaction diagrams, motion and terminal-speed graphs, component diagrams, Newton-law relationships

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4, 3.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: force/interaction diagrams, motion and terminal-speed graphs, component diagrams, Newton-law relationships

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U3-MG01

Mastery Goal: Apply Newton’s Second Law to relate net force, mass, and acceleration and explain how changing one quantity affects motion.

Supporting Learning: Sections 3.1.

Recurring Practices: quantitative reasoning; model-based explanation.

Key Representations: force diagrams, $F_{net}=ma$, tables/graphs of force-mass-acceleration.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence uses net force rather than a single force, computes/interprets the relationship, and explains proportional effects correctly.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Force-causes-motion language; ignoring mass; using applied force instead of net force.

U3-MG02

Mastery Goal: Model friction, weight, air resistance, and terminal-speed situations to explain free fall and nonfree fall.

Supporting Learning: Sections 3.2, 3.3.

Recurring Practices: modeling; graph interpretation; explanation.

Key Representations: force diagrams, friction models, falling graphs, $W=mg$, $F_{net}=W-F_{air}$.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence distinguishes mass/weight and free/nonfree fall, and explains terminal speed as zero net force with nonzero forces.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Friction always fixed; terminal speed means gravity stops; confusing mass and weight.

U3-MG03

Mastery Goal: Analyze Newton’s Third Law interactions by identifying equal-and-opposite forces acting on different objects.

Supporting Learning: Sections 3.4.

Recurring Practices: system/interaction reasoning; scientific explanation.

Key Representations: interaction diagrams, paired force vectors, system labels.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence correctly identifies the two interacting objects and explains why third-law forces do not cancel on one object.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Pairing forces on the same object; larger-object-larger-force reasoning.

U3-MG04

Mastery Goal: Model projectile motion as simultaneous horizontal constant-velocity and vertical accelerated motion.

Supporting Learning: Sections 3.5.

Recurring Practices: multi-representation modeling; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: trajectory sketches, component diagrams, $x=v_xt$, $y=\frac12gt^2$.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence separates components, uses gravity only in the vertical model when air resistance is ignored, and interprets the combined path.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Adding a forward force after launch; mixing components; applying gravity horizontally.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 3 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U3-MG01, U3-MG02, U3-MG03, U3-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of force/interaction diagrams, motion and terminal-speed graphs, component diagrams, Newton-law relationships. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 3 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
