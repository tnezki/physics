Conceptual Physics — Unit 1: Forces, Motion, and Equilibrium

Essential Standard / Enduring Understanding

Students will analyze forces, motion, and equilibrium conceptually by identifying forces, representing force situations with diagrams and vectors, explaining Newton’s First Law, distinguishing balanced from unbalanced forces, and using evidence from real-world situations to determine whether an object’s motion should remain unchanged or change.

Unit Source Alignment

Framework Alignment
  • Framework Course Structure: Conceptual Physics, Unit 1 of 8, five sections.
  • Textbook Alignment: 1.1 Thinking Like a Physicist — 1.1, 1.2; 1.2 Forces and Newton’s First Law — 2.3, 2.6; 1.3 Net Force and Vectors — 2.4; 1.4 Equilibrium and Balanced Motion — 2.5, 2.7; 1.5 Modeling Forces in the Real World — 2.3–2.7 synthesis

Section Outline

Section 1.1: Thinking Like a Physicist

Textbook Sections: 1.1, 1.2

Learning Target: Students will explain how physics uses observation, measurement, evidence, models, and testable claims to understand the natural world.

I Can Statements

  • I can distinguish between an observation, inference, hypothesis, law, and theory.
  • I can explain why a claim must be testable to be scientific.
  • I can use evidence from a short scenario to decide whether a physics explanation is supported and explain why formulas in physics are guides for thinking, not just tools for calculation.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: observation, inference, hypothesis, scientific law, scientific theory, evidence, model, testable claim

Key Representations: observations and measurements; claim-evidence-reasoning notes; simple model or formula representations

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Treating an inference as an observation; assuming an explanation is scientific because it sounds plausible; treating a formula as a rule with no physical meaning

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Evaluate short physical scenarios, measurements, and competing explanations; connect a simple relationship to the quantities it represents

Recurring Practice Opportunities: observation and evidence use; model interpretation; scientific explanation

Section 1.2: Forces and Newton’s First Law

Textbook Sections: 2.3, 2.6

Learning Target: Students will explain Newton’s First Law and use the idea of inertia to predict whether an object’s motion will stay the same or change.

I Can Statements

  • I can state Newton’s First Law in everyday language.
  • I can explain that inertia is a property of matter, not a force.
  • I can distinguish between “no forces acting” and “no net force acting.” and identify support force, weight, and other common forces in simple situations.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: force, inertia, Newton’s First Law, net force, support force, weight

Key Representations: force diagrams; motion descriptions; $\Sigma F$ reasoning; support-force and weight pairs

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Calling inertia a force; assuming motion requires a net forward force; confusing no force with zero net force

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Use everyday objects, carts, books, and moving vehicles to predict whether motion should remain unchanged or change

Recurring Practice Opportunities: conceptual modeling; explanation of phenomena; misconception critique

Section 1.3: Net Force and Vectors

Textbook Sections: 2.4

Learning Target: Students will represent forces as vectors and determine the direction and relative size of the net force in one-dimensional and simple two-dimensional situations.

I Can Statements

  • I can explain why force is a vector quantity.
  • I can combine forces acting in the same direction or opposite directions.
  • I can identify the resultant force from a simple vector diagram and use arrow length and direction to compare force strength and direction.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: vector, magnitude, direction, resultant, net force

Key Representations: scaled force arrows; one-dimensional vector sums; simple two-dimensional force diagrams; $F_{net}$

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Adding vector magnitudes without direction; treating arrow length as decorative; reversing resultant direction

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Combine and compare pushes/pulls, interpret vector diagrams, and connect resultant direction to expected motion change

Recurring Practice Opportunities: diagram modeling; quantitative reasoning; representation translation

Section 1.4: Equilibrium and Balanced Motion

Textbook Sections: 2.5, 2.7

Learning Target: Students will determine whether an object is in mechanical equilibrium and explain how balanced forces relate to rest or constant straight-line motion.

I Can Statements

  • I can recognize static equilibrium and dynamic equilibrium.
  • I can explain why an object at rest can still have forces acting on it.
  • I can explain why an object moving at constant speed in a straight line can be in equilibrium and use the equilibrium rule to reason about upward/downward and left/right forces.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: mechanical equilibrium, static equilibrium, dynamic equilibrium, balanced forces, constant velocity

Key Representations: balanced force diagrams; $\Sigma F=0$; rest and constant-velocity motion descriptions

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Equating equilibrium with rest only; assuming constant speed always means equilibrium when direction changes; assuming forces disappear at equilibrium

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Analyze hanging, supported, and constant-velocity situations; defend whether the system is in equilibrium

Recurring Practice Opportunities: systems reasoning; model-based explanation; argument from evidence

Section 1.5: Modeling Forces in the Real World

Textbook Sections: 2.3–2.7 synthesis

Learning Target: Students will create and critique force models for real-world situations involving inertia, support force, net force, and equilibrium.

I Can Statements

  • I can draw a force diagram for an object in a real-world situation.
  • I can decide whether the forces are balanced or unbalanced.
  • I can explain how the net force affects the object’s motion and critique an incorrect force explanation and revise it using physics vocabulary.

Key Vocabulary / Notation: force model, system, interaction, net force, equilibrium, evidence

Key Representations: real-world force diagrams; scenario descriptions; qualitative data/observations; model critiques

Common Misconceptions / Failure Points: Including forces that act on other objects; omitting support/weight; matching a force diagram to the wrong motion description

Modeling / Application Opportunities: Create, compare, and revise force models for familiar real-world systems using observations and motion evidence

Recurring Practice Opportunities: develop/evaluate models; scientific explanation; argument from evidence

Unit Themes / Throughlines

  • Forces are interactions represented with vectors; net force, not the presence of force alone, determines whether motion changes.
  • Newton’s First Law and equilibrium connect balanced forces to rest or constant straight-line motion.
  • Physics explanations are strengthened by testable claims, models, observations, and evidence.

Recurring Practice Map

Develop and Use Models

Framework / Source Basis: Framework repeatedly calls for Concept Development, investigations/phenomenon tasks, whiteboarding, and synthesis/application work.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student constructs, interprets, revises, or defends a model that connects the unit’s physical quantities and mechanisms.

Representations / Contexts: force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, $\Sigma F$ relationships, observations and evidence

Common Weaknesses: Models are treated as pictures only; labels/relationships are inconsistent; the model is not connected to a prediction or explanation.

Interpret Data and Representations

Framework / Source Basis: Framework embeds investigations, Formula Examples, ranking/error-analysis tasks, and whiteboarding across the unit.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student extracts a physical relationship from a graph, table, diagram, observation set, or other representation and translates it into words or another representation.

Representations / Contexts: force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, $\Sigma F$ relationships, observations and evidence

Common Weaknesses: Reading a graph/diagram superficially; confusing what axes/arrows/regions represent; switching representations without preserving the physical relationship.

Mathematical / Computational Thinking

Framework / Source Basis: Framework includes Formula Examples and practice while explicitly emphasizing conceptual understanding before computation.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student selects and interprets source-supported relationships involving simple net-force, equilibrium, and weight relationships, uses units/variables coherently, and explains what a result means.

Representations / Contexts: force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, $\Sigma F$ relationships, observations and evidence

Common Weaknesses: Equation hunting without a model; arithmetic with no interpretation; incorrect units or variable meaning; computation overriding conceptual constraints.

Plan / Evaluate Investigations

Framework / Source Basis: Every section includes an Investigation / Lab / Phenomenon Task in the framework, with discussion and whiteboarding.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student identifies what evidence would test a claim or model, uses observations/data appropriately, and evaluates whether results support the explanation.

Representations / Contexts: force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, $\Sigma F$ relationships, observations and evidence

Common Weaknesses: Treating one observation as proof; failing to control/identify relevant variables; reporting data without connecting it to the claim or model.

Scientific Explanation and Argument from Evidence

Framework / Source Basis: Framework calls for discussion, whiteboarding, error analysis, CER, and collaborative problem solving throughout the course.

Sections Where It Naturally Appears: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4, 1.5

What Student Evidence Looks Like: Student makes a physics claim, cites appropriate evidence or representation, and connects that evidence to the governing model or relationship.

Representations / Contexts: force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, $\Sigma F$ relationships, observations and evidence

Common Weaknesses: Claim without evidence; evidence listed without reasoning; vocabulary used without explaining the causal/physical connection.

Mastery Goals

U1-MG01

Mastery Goal: Use evidence and models to distinguish observations, inferences, and testable physics explanations, and communicate what a representation or relationship means.

Supporting Learning: Sections 1.1.

Recurring Practices: scientific explanation; evidence use; model interpretation.

Key Representations: observations, short data sets, claim-evidence reasoning, simple model/formula representations.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence distinguishes evidence from inference, supports a testable explanation, and interprets a model or relationship without treating it as calculation only.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Unsupported claims; confusing observation and inference; using formulas without physical interpretation.

U1-MG02

Mastery Goal: Explain Newton’s First Law and inertia to predict when an object’s motion remains unchanged or changes.

Supporting Learning: Sections 1.2, 1.4.

Recurring Practices: explanation of phenomena; conceptual modeling.

Key Representations: motion descriptions, force diagrams, equilibrium relationships.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence correctly connects zero net force with rest or constant straight-line motion and treats inertia as a property of matter rather than a force.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Motion-requires-force reasoning; inertia-as-force language; confusing zero net force with no forces.

U1-MG03

Mastery Goal: Represent and determine net force using vectors, then use the result to classify balanced and unbalanced situations.

Supporting Learning: Sections 1.3, 1.4.

Recurring Practices: diagram modeling; quantitative reasoning.

Key Representations: vector arrows, one-dimensional force combinations, simple two-dimensional diagrams, $\Sigma F$.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence uses direction and relative magnitude consistently, determines the resultant/net force, and interprets whether the system is in equilibrium.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Ignoring direction; adding magnitudes blindly; mismatching a force diagram and motion state.

U1-MG04

Mastery Goal: Create, critique, and defend force models for real-world systems using evidence about interactions and motion.

Supporting Learning: Sections 1.5 with 1.2–1.4.

Recurring Practices: develop/evaluate models; argument from evidence.

Key Representations: force diagrams, written scenarios, observations, motion evidence.

Meeting Evidence: Meeting evidence identifies forces acting on the chosen object, represents them coherently, and explains the motion prediction from net force.

Common Evidence Weaknesses / Misconceptions: Forces drawn on the wrong object; missing support/weight; explanations not tied to the model or evidence.

Summative Assessment Plan

Unit 1 Summative Assessment

This summative gathers secure evidence on the Mastery Goals and Learning Ladder defined in this Assessment Plan.

Mastery Goals Assessed: U1-MG01, U1-MG02, U1-MG03, U1-MG04.

Recurring Practices: The secure assessment should sample model use, representation/data interpretation, quantitative reasoning where source-supported, and scientific explanation/argument from evidence. Investigation-design evidence may be sampled through a concise design/critique response or gathered separately through performance evidence.

Representation Expectations: Students should encounter an appropriate mix of force/vector diagrams, motion descriptions, $\Sigma F$ relationships, observations and evidence. Evidence should require interpretation and explanation, not only recognition.

Recommended Response Balance: Use selected-response or brief classification/interpretation only to sample breadth. Give substantial weight to constructed responses that require diagrams/models, calculations with interpretation, CER-style explanation, error analysis, or representation connections. A separate performance task may contribute additional portfolio evidence but is not required on the conventional summative.

Calculator / Reference / Materials: Use calculators only when computation supports the intended physics evidence; conceptual/modeling evidence should remain interpretable without calculator dependence. Any formula/reference support should be limited to source-supported Unit 1 relationships. The secure summative should not require lab materials; investigation materials belong to separate richer evidence when used.

Meeting Evidence: A Meeting judgment comes from sufficient evidence that the student can explain the unit’s central phenomena, use the relevant representations coherently, and apply source-supported quantitative relationships with correct physical interpretation across the Mastery Goals.

Exceeding Evidence: Exceeding may emerge when a student independently transfers the unit models to an unfamiliar context, evaluates competing models or evidence, or produces an especially well-supported explanation. It is not a required separate “Exceeding question.”

Performance Evidence: Optional unit-level performance evidence may supplement the conventional summative, especially when investigation reasoning, model evaluation, or multi-representation synthesis would be better demonstrated outside a timed paper/HTML assessment.

Assessment Conditions / Timing: The uploaded Framework does not specify a required summative duration or reassessment form. Keep timing and alternate-form decisions flexible and consistent with the course’s conceptual-before-computational emphasis.

DOK: Natural DOK is descriptive of the thinking required; it is not an item-production quota, and DOK 4 should not be forced into a conventional test.
