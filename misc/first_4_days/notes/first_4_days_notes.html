Course Routines + Scientific Explanations

How physicists turn observations into explanations other people can inspect.

Physics idea: A strong explanation separates what you claim, what you observed, and why the evidence supports the claim.

  • I can select relevant observations or data as evidence for a claim.

Idea 1 - Routines Protect the Science

Physics depends on observations that another person can inspect. Class procedures help protect that record. A file saved where nobody can find it, a measurement written without units, or equipment used differently by each group can make good evidence hard to compare. Procedures are not separate from the science; they help make the science usable.

Stop and Discuss
  1. How could a sloppy file or data routine damage scientific evidence?
  2. Which class routines help another person reproduce or inspect your work?

Idea 2 - Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning Have Different Jobs

A claim answers the question about a phenomenon. Evidence is the relevant observation or measurement that supports the claim. Reasoning explains why that evidence supports the claim using a scientific relationship or model. The three parts work together, but they should not simply repeat one another.

Example 1

A battery-powered bug has measured speeds of 0.18 m/s, 0.24 m/s, and 0.31 m/s at successive equal times. Label each sentence as Claim, Evidence, or Reasoning.

  1. The bug sped up.
  2. Its measured speed increased from 0.18 m/s to 0.31 m/s.
  3. Speed describes how quickly position changes with time, so larger speed measurements mean the motion became faster.

You Try It 1

A skateboard travels 0.6 m, 0.9 m, and 1.3 m during three successive equal time intervals. Write one claim, one evidence sentence, and one reasoning sentence about whether its speed changed.

Idea 3 - Evidence Is Selected, Not Dumped

An investigation may produce many facts, but strong evidence is relevant, specific, and tied to the claim. To support a claim about change, one isolated measurement is usually not enough; you need information that allows a comparison.

Stop and Discuss
  1. Why can adding more facts make an explanation weaker instead of stronger?
  2. What kind of evidence is needed to support a claim that something changed?

Example 2

A foam ball moves 0.06 m between two early video frames and 0.19 m between two later frames. The frame times are equal. The ball is blue, the room is 22 C, and the camera is on a tripod. Which facts are the strongest evidence for the claim the ball sped up? Explain why.

You Try It 2

A toy car has mass 0.42 kg, is red, travels 0.30 m during one 0.50 s interval and 0.47 m during a later 0.50 s interval, and moves across gray floor tiles. Which facts belong in evidence for the claim the car sped up? Explain your selection.

Idea 4 - Reasoning Is the Bridge

Evidence does not explain itself. Reasoning names the physical relationship that makes the evidence matter. For motion, $v=d/t$ says that speed is distance traveled per unit time. The equation can support the explanation, but the reasoning still needs to say what the numbers mean physically.

Example 3

Car A travels 9 m in 3 s. Car B travels the same 9 m in 6 s. Find each speed, then write one reasoning sentence explaining why the calculation supports the claim that Car A is faster.

You Try It 3

A cart travels 0.25 m during one 0.50 s interval and 0.45 m during a later 0.50 s interval. Find the average speed for each interval, then write reasoning that connects those values to the claim that the cart sped up.

Idea 5 - Put the Three Parts Together

A complete CER should be traceable. A reader should be able to identify the answer, point to the measurement that supports it, and follow the physical idea that connects the two.

Example 4

A cart travels 0.20 m, 0.32 m, and 0.46 m during three successive 0.50 s intervals. Write a short CER answering: Did the cart's speed stay constant or change?

You Try It 4

A ball travels 0.08 m, 0.17 m, and 0.29 m during three successive 0.10 s intervals. Write a short CER answering: Did the ball's speed stay constant or change?

Summary

  • Class procedures protect files, data, equipment, and the record of what happened.
  • A claim answers the question; evidence reports relevant observations or data; reasoning explains the physical connection.
  • Strong evidence is selected because it matters to the claim and often allows a useful comparison.
  • Equations such as $v=d/t$ describe physical relationships; they can strengthen reasoning but do not replace an explanation.
  • A strong CER lets another person trace the claim back to evidence and understand why that evidence matters.

Common Mistakes

  • Calling the conclusion "evidence" instead of reporting what was observed or measured.
  • Listing every available fact instead of selecting evidence that bears on the claim.
  • Repeating the evidence as reasoning without naming the physical relationship.
  • Using an equation without explaining what the quantities mean physically.
  • Ignoring procedures that protect files, data, equipment, or the ability to reproduce the work.
