Physics · First 4 Days
Course Routines + Scientific Explanations
How physicists turn observations into explanations other people can inspect.

Physics idea: A good explanation does not begin with an answer. It begins with a phenomenon, uses evidence from what happened, and connects that evidence to a physical idea.

Learning Target

I can successfully participate in physics class by following course procedures and using evidence-based reasoning to explain physical phenomena.

I Can…

  • I can follow the procedures and routines used in physics class.
  • I can identify the parts of a scientific explanation: Claim, Evidence, and Reasoning (CER).
  • I can make a clear claim that answers a question about a physical phenomenon.
  • I can select and use relevant observations or data as evidence to support my claim.
  • I can explain how my evidence supports my claim using scientific reasoning.

Routines Protect the Science

Physics often looks like motion, forces, energy, light, electricity, and waves. But before any of those ideas can be trusted, a class has to protect the observations. A file saved in the wrong place, a sensor used differently by each group, or a measurement recorded without units can make a good investigation impossible to reconstruct.

A procedure is an agreed sequence of steps. In class, procedures are not busywork around the science; they are part of making the evidence usable. When everyone follows the same collection, naming, recording, and cleanup routines, we can compare results instead of wondering whether different methods created the difference.

Stop and Discuss

  1. How could a sloppy file routine damage scientific evidence?
  2. Which classroom routines help another person reproduce or inspect your work?

Example 1: Protect the Record

A group tracks a cart in a video and exports a position table. One student wants to rename the file “stuff” and save it wherever there is space. A stronger routine is to use the class naming/location system so the group can identify the trial, reopen it, and connect it to the correct CER later.

Example 1 teacher move: Use the exact local file/data routine; do not let the document invent policy.

You Try It 1: Build a Reliable Routine

Your group finishes a sensor trial. Write the exact class steps you should use to save the data, identify the trial, and leave the equipment ready for the next group.

A Scientific Explanation Has Three Different Jobs

Imagine watching a small cart roll down a ramp. Saying “it got faster” may be a good start, but physics asks us to separate what we think happened from what we actually observed. A claim answers the question about the phenomenon. Evidence is the relevant observation or measurement that supports that answer. Reasoning explains why that evidence supports the claim using a scientific relationship or model.

The parts should work together without becoming duplicates. “The cart sped up” is a claim. “It traveled 0.20 m, then 0.35 m, then 0.55 m during equal time intervals” is evidence. The reasoning is the bridge: during equal time intervals, greater distance traveled means greater speed.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why is “I saw it speed up” weaker evidence than a sequence of measured distances?
  2. Can a sentence be scientifically true but still be the wrong CER part?

Example 2: Give Each Sentence One Job

A battery-powered bug crosses a desk. Its measured speeds are 0.18 m/s, 0.24 m/s, and 0.31 m/s at successive equal times.

  • Claim: The bug sped up.
  • Evidence: Its measured speed increased from 0.18 m/s to 0.31 m/s.
  • Reasoning: Speed tells how fast position changes with time, so increasing speed measurements show the bug’s motion became faster.
Example 2 answer: Claim = the bug sped up; evidence = measured speeds increased; reasoning = increasing speed measurements over time show faster motion.

You Try It 2: Sort the Jobs

A skateboard rolls down a gentle hill. During three equal time intervals it travels 0.6 m, 0.9 m, and 1.3 m. Write one claim, one evidence sentence, and one reasoning sentence.

Evidence Is Selected, Not Dumped

Real investigations create more information than any one explanation needs. The color of the cart, the time of day, a partner’s shoe size, the measured distance, and the video frame rate may all be facts, but only some facts answer the question. Strong evidence is relevant, specific, and tied to the claim.

Measurements are especially useful when they allow a comparison. If the question is whether a moving object sped up, the strongest evidence usually compares motion at more than one time. One isolated number may describe the object, but it often cannot show a change.

Stop and Discuss

  1. Why can adding more facts make an explanation weaker instead of stronger?
  2. What kind of evidence is needed to support a claim about change?

Example 3: Choose the Evidence That Matters

A foam ball is dropped in front of a meter stick. Early in the video it moves 0.06 m between frames; later it moves 0.19 m between frames. The ball is blue, the room is 22°C, and the camera is on a tripod. For the claim “the ball sped up,” the changing position between equal-time frames is relevant evidence; the color is not.

Example 3 answer: The 0.06 m versus 0.19 m frame-to-frame displacement is the evidence. Press students to explain why equal frame times matter.

You Try It 3: Keep the Useful Evidence

A toy car travels across the lab. You know its mass is 0.42 kg, its color is red, it travels 0.30 m during one 0.5 s interval and 0.47 m during a later 0.5 s interval, and the floor tiles are gray. Which facts belong in evidence for the claim “the car sped up”? Explain your selection.

Reasoning Connects Evidence to a Physical Relationship

Evidence does not explain itself. The reasoning names the physical relationship that makes the evidence matter. In physics, an equation can be part of that bridge, but the equation is not an answer machine. It represents a relationship between physical quantities.

Suppose a model car covers 6 m in 2 s and another covers 6 m in 4 s. The relationship $v=d/t$ tells us why equal distance in less time means greater speed. The numbers matter because of what distance, time, and speed mean physically. Good reasoning can be written with words, equations, diagrams, or a combination, as long as it explains the connection.

Stop and Discuss

  1. What is missing from the sentence “The car was faster because 3 is bigger than 1.5”?
  2. How can an equation strengthen reasoning without replacing an explanation?

Example 4: Use the Relationship as the Bridge

A remote-control car crosses a 9 m hallway in 3 s. Another car takes 6 s to cross the same hallway. The first car’s speed is $9/3=3$ m/s and the second car’s speed is $9/6=1.5$ m/s. Because speed is distance traveled per unit time, covering the same distance in half the time means the first car moves faster.

Example 4 answer: Car 1 = 3 m/s; Car 2 = 1.5 m/s. The physical bridge is distance per unit time, not merely “3 is bigger.”

You Try It 4: Strengthen the Reasoning

A small cart travels 0.25 m during one 0.50 s interval and 0.45 m during a later 0.50 s interval. A student claims the cart sped up. Write reasoning that uses the meaning of speed to connect the equal-time measurements to the claim.

Vocabulary with Definitions

WordDefinition
PhenomenonAn observable event or behavior that we want to describe or explain.
ClaimA clear answer to a question about the phenomenon.
EvidenceRelevant observations or measurements used to support a claim.
ReasoningThe scientific relationship or model that explains why the evidence supports the claim.
ObservationSomething noticed or measured about a phenomenon.
DataRecorded measurements or organized observations.
ProcedureAn agreed sequence of steps used to collect, organize, or communicate work consistently.

Summary

  • Class procedures protect the record of what happened and make evidence easier to trust.
  • A claim answers the question; evidence reports the relevant observation or data; reasoning explains the physical connection.
  • Strong evidence is selected because it matters to the claim, not because it happens to be available.
  • Physics equations describe relationships between physical quantities; they can support reasoning but should not replace the physical story.
  • Good CER explanations become stronger when another person can trace the claim back to observations and understand why those observations matter.
Summary notes / questions / connections

Common Mistakes

  • Calling the conclusion “evidence” instead of reporting what was observed or measured.
  • Listing every available fact instead of selecting evidence that bears on the claim.
  • Repeating the evidence as reasoning without naming the physical relationship.
  • Plugging numbers into an equation without first identifying the physical situation and what the quantities represent.
  • Ignoring class procedures that protect files, data, equipment, or the ability to reproduce the work.
