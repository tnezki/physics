Bowling Ball Drop — Teacher Guide

Claim first, record the phenomenon, learn the tool, analyze independently, then reason from evidence.

Main learning jobStudents make an initial claim, generate usable video, analyze their own motion data, and use evidence to keep or revise the claim.
GroupingPairs record and analyze. One designated person makes each drop. Three drops give every pair options if one recording is poor.
ToolsBowling ball, clear drop area, student recording devices, Vernier Video Analysis, existing Vernier Hints document in Student Resources.

Why this fits

This is an opening investigation about how physics class works: make a claim, create evidence, learn a measurement/analysis tool, examine multiple representations, and reason from what the data actually shows. The software is a tool for the investigation, not the lesson's answer source.

Key design choice: students commit to a claim about velocity before recording or seeing analyzed data. Do not confirm, reject, or steer the claim at that point.

Before class

  • Choose a safe drop location with enough vertical distance for a clear recording.
  • Use one designated dropper and establish a clear drop zone. Keep all other students behind the recording line.
  • Place any known-length reference needed for video scale in the camera plane before students record.
  • Have the Vernier demonstration ready, but plan to demonstrate features and workflow only, not interpret the bowling-ball result for students.
  • Make sure students can access the existing Vernier Hints document from Student Resources if they need a reminder during pair work.

During the claim

Teacher moves

  • Ask students to commit before data collection.
  • If a student asks, “Is this right?” respond with a process question such as, “What would you need to measure to decide?”
  • Do not introduce graph-shape or acceleration hints yet.

Listen for

  • Different meanings students attach to the word velocity.
  • Claims based on intuition, prior experience, or everyday language.
  • Students already thinking about what evidence could change their minds.

Outside recording

Run three drops with one designated dropper. Pairs record each drop so they can choose the strongest clip later. The goal is not three trials for averaging; the goal is three recording options so a bad camera angle or missed frame does not ruin the investigation.

Safety: one designated dropper, a clearly defined drop zone, and no students entering the fall/impact area during a drop.

Vernier demonstration

Model the tool features students need to work independently:

  • tracking motion through the video;
  • setting the needed reference/origin information;
  • viewing position-time and velocity-time representations;
  • using Examine to read values from a graph;
  • using regression/fit tools.

Do not interpret the bowling-ball result during the demo. The demonstration answers “How do I use this?” rather than “What does our data say?”

Pair analysis + Examine

Teacher moves

  • Check that the pair chose a video where the ball can be tracked cleanly for a useful span.
  • When data are noisy, ask students to inspect their tracking choices before assuming the physics is unusual.
  • Ask, “What specific value, feature, or relationship makes you say that?”
  • Let students decide which representations are most persuasive; do not force every row to carry equal weight.

Student moves

  • Replicate the demonstrated analysis on their own video.
  • Use multiple views of the same motion.
  • Record specific evidence in the Examine table.
  • Select the evidence they believe best addresses the original velocity question.

Expected evidence and interpretation

A strong analysis should show that the ball's velocity changes during the fall. Depending on the chosen axis direction, signed velocity may become more positive or more negative; the important point is that students interpret their own convention consistently. A useful velocity-time fit should show systematic change rather than constant velocity. Position-time data should also be inconsistent with uniform-velocity motion.

Do not require a precise value for gravitational acceleration or a formal constant-acceleration derivation. This is primarily an investigation in claim/evidence/reasoning and learning the analysis workflow.

Reasoning

Students first state a final claim, then choose evidence, then explain why the evidence supports that claim. The page intentionally does not tell them what graph shape, slope, sign, or regression result they “should” see.

Press questions

  • Which evidence would still be convincing if I had not watched your video?
  • What does that graph feature actually tell you about the motion?
  • How does your sign convention affect the numbers but not the physical conclusion?
  • What part of your evidence is strongest? What part is most uncertain?

Discussion

The final six questions are meant to discuss the whole process, not merely confirm a right answer. Good class conversation should surface:

  • whether and why claims changed;
  • which representation was most useful and why;
  • what the analysis tools revealed beyond naked-eye observation;
  • sources of measurement/tracking uncertainty;
  • agreement and variation across pairs;
  • how the investigation could be improved if repeated.
Strong closing idea: physics is not “guess, then get told the answer.” Students make an idea public, create evidence, use tools to examine the evidence, and revise their thinking when the evidence warrants it.
