Ball Drop Video Analysis — Teacher Guide

A simple tool-learning investigation whose main evidence job is CER, not a full kinematics lesson.
MaterialsTeacher-provided ball-drop video with known scale; Vernier Video Analysis; student device
Student actionSet scale/origin, track one point, inspect position vs time, write CER
Core I-cansOPEN-IC1, IC3, IC4, IC5

Before class

  • Use a short ball-drop video with a visible reference length and a camera that is approximately stationary/perpendicular to the motion plane.
  • Choose a clip where the ball is easy to track for at least six useful frames.
  • Decide the vertical sign convention you will accept; the pattern matters more than whether down is positive or negative.

Vernier Video Analysis workflow

The current Vernier manual’s basic workflow is: import a video, set scale and origin, mark a consistent point on the object frame by frame, then inspect the graph/data. Keep the student directions at that level so the software does not overwhelm the CER learning goal.

Anticipate / Monitor / Select / Sequence / Connect

PhaseTeacher move
AnticipateExpect inconsistent clicks, scale/origin confusion, and students treating a curved position graph as “speed.”
MonitorLook for pairs marking the same location on the ball and comparing position changes over similar time intervals.
SelectChoose one group with specific numerical evidence and one with a vague “it looks faster” statement.
SequenceShare the vague observation first, then the measured comparison, then a reasoning bridge.
ConnectConnect larger position changes in equal time intervals to greater speed. Do not turn this into a formal constant-acceleration derivation yet.

Discourse Moves

Teacher Moves

  • Ask, “Which two numbers from your own table make your claim believable?”
  • When students point to graph shape only, ask them to translate the graph into a change they can describe from the table.
  • Press reasoning with “What does covering more distance in the same time tell you about speed?”

Student Moves

  • Compare two tracked points and explain why the clicking location matters.
  • Use a specific pair of intervals as evidence.
  • Revise the CER so the reasoning states the distance-per-time relationship.
Likely conclusion: The falling ball speeds up; successive equal-time intervals show larger changes in vertical position. Accept equivalent evidence grounded in the student’s tracked data.
Scope guard: The purpose is a clean first CER from measured motion. Formal free-fall equations and precise g determination are not required here.
