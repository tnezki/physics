Ball Drop Video Analysis — Teacher Guide

A first data-to-CER investigation: students learn the video-analysis routine, select measured evidence, and explain what the evidence means without turning the lesson into a formal free-fall derivation.

Time / groupingAbout 25–35 minutes · pairs
MaterialsBall-drop video with visible scale · Vernier Video Analysis · student device
Student productTracked data · two interval comparisons · one CER

Why this fits the First 4 Days

Students do real physics-class work immediately: follow a measurement procedure, make a prediction, collect data, decide which data matter, and communicate a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning explanation. The content stays intentionally light. The learning job is not to derive constant-acceleration equations or determine a precise value of g.

Before class

  • Use a short ball-drop clip with a stationary or nearly stationary camera and a visible known-length reference.
  • Choose a clip where the ball can be tracked clearly for at least eight consecutive useful points.
  • Test the clip once so the scale is readable and the ball remains visible through the desired tracking interval.
  • Decide whether students should mark every frame or use a fixed frame step. Consistent time spacing matters more than the particular step.
  • Accept either vertical sign convention. Students should compare the distance moved during equal-time intervals so negative coordinates do not create a “larger vs. smaller” sign trap.

Suggested flow

PhaseStudent workTeacher moves / listen for
1. Predict
2–3 min		 Answer what happens to the ball’s speed and give an initial reason. Do not confirm the answer. Treat the prediction as a starting idea that must later be supported or revised with measurements.
2. Set up + quality check
5–8 min		 Set scale/origin, choose a consistent point on the ball, and mark with a consistent frame step. Watch for changing click location, an incorrect scale, or skipped/uneven frame steps. Ask partners to compare their first few marks before continuing.
3. Collect data
5–7 min		 Record at least eight time/position points and calculate the positive distance moved between consecutive points. Listen for sign confusion. The position coordinate may be positive or negative; the comparison column is the size of the movement between points.
4. Build evidence
5–7 min		 Check time spacing, then compare one early interval with one later interval of equal or nearly equal duration. Press for actual numbers. “It looks faster” is an observation; a measured interval comparison is stronger evidence.
5. CER + partner check
8–10 min		 Write a claim, cite measured values, and connect distance-per-equal-time to speed. Ask: “Which exact numbers make your claim believable?” Then: “Why do those numbers tell you something about speed?”

Expected evidence

Strong data pattern: For equal or nearly equal time intervals, the ball covers a greater distance during a later interval than during an earlier interval. A strong CER uses specific measured values from those intervals and concludes that the ball’s speed increases as it falls.

Strong sample reasoning

If the ball moves farther during the same amount of time, it is moving faster during that later part of the fall. Because the later equal-time interval shows a greater distance traveled than the earlier interval, the measurements support the claim that the ball speeds up.

Common problems and useful presses

What you may seeUseful teacher press
Students click different parts of the ball from frame to frame.“Pick one point on the ball. What would inconsistent clicking do to your position data?”
Students say the ball “slows down” because vertical position numbers become more negative.“Ignore the sign for a moment. How far did the ball move during each equal-time interval?”
Students compare intervals with noticeably different durations.“Can those distances be compared fairly? Find two intervals that lasted about the same amount of time.”
Evidence says only “the graph curves” or “it looks faster.”“Which two measurements from your table show that pattern?”
Reasoning repeats the claim.“Why does moving farther in the same amount of time tell you the speed is different?”

Debrief target

Bring the class back to the distinction among the three CER jobs: the claim answers the question, the evidence is measured information that supports the answer, and the reasoning explains why that evidence is relevant. If students naturally mention acceleration, acknowledge it without requiring formal acceleration equations.

Scope guard: This is a clean first CER from measured motion, not a precision free-fall lab. Do not let software troubleshooting, formal graph analysis, or calculation of g take over the evidence-reasoning goal.
