Chase the Tangent

A First 4 Days investigation that lets students experience secant slopes approaching a tangent slope before formal derivative notation or rules.

Time35–45 minutes
GroupingPairs work well
TechnologyBrowser on each device
Scope guard: Do not turn this into a derivative-rules lesson. The goal is for students to notice that nearby secant slopes stabilize toward a tangent slope, see why h=0 causes a problem, and connect tangent slope to instantaneous change.

Learning job

Students manipulate h on three different functions, use secant-slope evidence to estimate tangent slopes, then use point-slope form to write candidate tangent-line equations. The activity intentionally delays formal derivative notation.

Before class

  • Open the student page on a projector and confirm the sliders move.
  • Students need a device capable of running ordinary HTML/JavaScript in the browser.
  • No Desmos class code is required for this version; the interaction is built directly into the page.
  • Decide whether students will type responses into the page, discuss with a partner, or record work separately.

Suggested flow

ScreenStudent jobTeacher moveWhat to listen for
1 — QuadraticDefine h as the horizontal change from A to B; move B from both sides and watch the secant slope.Ask, “What changes when h changes? What stays fixed?”B approaches A as |h| shrinks; secant slopes appear to settle near one value.
2 — CubicRepeat the same process on g(x)=0.35x^3-x+3 at A = (2, 3.8).Press for evidence from both positive and negative h.Students should see slopes approaching about 3.2 from both sides.
3 — SineTrack positive and negative h deliberately on p(x)=\sin x at x=\pi/4.Keep the conversation on the process, not on trig derivative rules.Students should see slopes approaching about 0.71.
4 — Why care?Connect tangent slope to describing change at one instant.Invite examples of quantities whose rates are not constant.Tangent slope as a local/instantaneous rate, not merely “a line that touches once.”
5 — Quadratic tangent estimateChoose a candidate slope and write a tangent-line estimate through (1,1) using point-slope form.Ask students to justify the slope from earlier secant evidence before adjusting visually.Reasonable slope near 2; equation equivalent to y-1\approx2(x-1).
6 — Cubic tangent estimateRepeat with less support at A = (2,3.8).Do not simplify the point-slope form for them.Reasonable slope near 3.2; equation equivalent to y-3.8\approx3.2(x-2).
7 — Why not h = 0?Set h exactly to 0 and examine the secant-slope fraction.Ask, “What is useful about getting close to zero even though zero itself fails?”At h = 0, numerator and denominator are both 0; the quotient is unusable, but nearby values show a stable trend.
8 — SynthesisExplain secant → tangent, the role of h, instantaneous change, and the h = 0 issue.Select a few contrasting responses for discussion rather than simply confirming an answer.Students connect graphical movement, numerical slope evidence, and verbal meaning.

Common issues

  • “The tangent line touches the curve once.” Counter with Screen 6: the useful idea is local slope, not a one-touch rule.
  • Students only approach from one side. Send them back to positive and negative h.
  • Students think h is the slope. Re-anchor h as the horizontal change from A to B.
  • Students jump to derivative formulas they remember. Ask them to justify their estimate from the secant data visible in the activity.
  • Students simplify point-slope form incorrectly. Keep the target point visible and have them substitute into y=m(x-x_1)+y_1 one piece at a time.

Debrief

Strong closing question: “If we cannot use h = 0 in the secant-slope fraction, what mathematical idea would let us describe the number the slopes are approaching anyway?”

That question creates a natural bridge into limits and the derivative without formally teaching either during the First 4 Days investigation.
